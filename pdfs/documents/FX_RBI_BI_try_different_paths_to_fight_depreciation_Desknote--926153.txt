# FX: RBI, BI try different paths to fight depreciation

FOCUS FOCUS 12/06/2026 1

Chandresh Jain, EM Asia FX and Rates Strategist | BNP Paribas Singapore Branch. India and Indonesia have faced parallel challenges over the past few months as their currencies depreciated rapidly against the dollar. Year-to-date, the rupee is 6.1% lower, while the rupiah has fallen 7.2%. In response, both central banks announced measures last week designed to support their respective currencies. This note addresses key client questions related to these measures and their potential impact on the markets. Q1. What measures did the RBI announce? Attractive FCNR(B) deposits: The RBI will fully cover the FX hedging costs for banks that raise fresh 3-to-5- year Foreign Currency Non-Resident deposits (known as (FCNR(B) deposits). Concessional FX swaps: The RBI will offer cheap currency swaps to state-owned firms and select banks to hedge their external commercial borrowings. Bond access and lower taxes: The RBI has expanded the universe of securities under the Fully Accessible Route to include all new issuances of 15y, 30y and 40y government securities. Additionally, the Indian government has announced the removal of capital gains and interest income taxes for foreign institutional investors, effective retrospectively from 1 April 2026. Increased limits for equity investments: The RBI has raised the limits for investments by Non-Resident Indians (NRIs), Overseas Citizens of India (OCIs), and Persons of Indian Origin (PIOs) in equity instruments traded on the stock market, without requiring registration with the Securities and Exchange Board of India. Reduced export deadline: The timeline for exporters to repatriate their USD earnings has been shortened to nine months from 15, with the aim of increasing the domestic supply of dollars.

## KEY MESSAGES

We think recent currency stabilization measures introduced by the Reserve Bank of India will attract substantial foreign inflows over the next six months, which should help INR and enable it to outperform its peers in the region. Measures introduced by Bank Indonesia, meanwhile, may take longer to effectively halt rupiah depreciation, with Indonesian bonds can see a further sell-off in anticipation of additional rate hikes, in our view. India has been experiencing currency depreciation pressure due to capital outflows from equities, exacerbated by a deteriorating current account balance caused by higher oil prices and a decline in the Indian IT sector amid the global evolution of Artificial Intelligence (AI). In response, the RBI announced several measures last week to combat currency depreciation, including: Attractive FCNR(B) deposits: The RBI will fully cover the foreign exchange hedging costs for banks that raise fresh 3-5 year Foreign Currency Non-Resident (FCNR) deposits. Concessional FX swaps for ECB: The RBI is offering cheap currency swaps to state-owned firms and select banks to hedge their external commercial borrowings (ECBs). Bond access and lower taxes: The RBI has expanded the universe of securities under the Fully Accessible Route to include all new issuances of 15-, 30-, and 40- year government securities. Additionally, the Indian government has announced the removal of capital gains and interest income taxes for foreign institutional investors, effective retrospectively from April 1, 2026. Increased limits for equity investments: The RBI has raised the limits for investments by Non-Resident Indians (NRIs), Overseas Citizens of India (OCIs), and Persons of Indian Origin (PIOs) in equity instruments traded on the stock market, without requiring registration with the Securities and Exchange Board of India. 212/06/2026 Q2: How will the FCNR(B) FCNR(B) deposits are fixed-term bank deposits that can be opened in India by NRIs, OCIs, and PIOs. The facility will be available until 16 October 2026, for deposits mobilized by 30 September. We think this could save banks about 300bp of hedging costs in our view and allow them to offer significantly higher interest rates on 3-to-5-year foreign currency deposits. To facilitate this, the RBI has exempted these deposits from the Cash Reserve Ratio and Statutory Liquidity Ratio requirements. It has also relaxed rules to allow Indian banks to issue Standby Letters of Credit, enabling overseas banks to lend money to NRIs against their FCNR(B) deposits, which should create investment leverage opportunities. Prior to the announcement, three-year USD-denominated FCNR(B) deposits were offering interest rates in the range of 3.5–4%. With the new scheme these rates could increase to 6-7%, we think, pushing potential returns into the double digits with the addition of leverage. In 2013, a similar scheme resulted in inflows of about USD30bn over time (see Figure 1). Given global money supply (M2) has more than doubled since 2013, and allowing for a 40% rise in the overseas Indian population over the same period, we estimate inflows this time may be substantially higher, at around USD50bn. Q3: How will the measure relating to external commercial borrowings generate flows? The RBI's USD–rupee FX swap facility for external commercial borrowings with an average maturity of three years or more will be applicable to borrowings drawn after the date of the circular until 31 December 2026. This facility is available to corporates with majority state ownership (known as PSUs) and Authorized Dealer Category-I banks that raise overseas foreign currency borrowings with a minimum maturity of three years. The facility allows PSUs and authorized banks to borrow dollars and convert them into INR with hedge costs subsidized by the RBI. That lowers the effective borrowing rate to below that of domestic borrowing. We think this incentive will encourage these companies to tap into this facility before the end of the year. India raised about USD45bn through such borrowing last year. As the facility is limited to PSUs and authorized banks but providing substantial savings for them, we estimate the inflows generated may be around USD15– 20bn. Q4: How will these measures impact USDINR? We expect the measures announced by the RBI to generate USD70–80bn worth of inflows in the second half of this year. For context, India's current account deficit in H2 2025 was USD28bn. Even assuming a higher deficit in 2026 due to increased oil prices, these inflows are likely to more than cover the shortfall. As a result, the RBI's headline foreign exchange reserves would increase, while its net reserves (adjusted for forward book) should remain relatively stable. With higher headline reserves over the next 3–5 years, the RBI would then have more ammunition to intervene in the FX market if needed. This does not necessarily mean the RBI will actively push the USDINR exchange rate lower from current levels. However, we think this scenario would likely work to cap USDINR upside and prevent a significant depreciation of the currency. We therefore expect the INR to outperform other regional currencies, and its own forward rates, as the increased reserves provide a buffer against potential downward pressure on the currency. We also expect the realized volatility in USDINR to come down as the RBI can become active on both sides. If the USDINR goes higher due to depreciation pressure, it can stop the move by selling US dollars with these new flows and then buy US dollars if the USDINR comes down to recoup the FX reserves lost in the last few quarters. The RBI has introduced a US Dollar-Rupee Forex Swap Facility for External Commercial Borrowings (ECBs) with an average maturity of three years or more, applicable to borrowings drawn after the date of the circular until December 31, 2026. This facility is available to Public Sector Undertakings (PSUs) and Authorized Dealer Category I banks that raise Overseas Foreign Currency Borrowings (OFCBs) with a minimum maturity of three years. Under this facility, PSUs and authorized banks can borrow US dollars and convert them into INR at a subsidized hedge cost, courtesy of the RBI. As a result, the effective borrowing rate will be lower than their domestic borrowing costs in India. This incentive is expected to encourage these companies to tap into this facility before the end of the year. In 2025, India raised approximately USD45bn through ECBs. Given that this facility is limited to PSUs and authorized banks, we estimate that the inflows from this route could be around USD15-20bn. This is a significant opportunity for these entities to access cheaper funding, which in turn can help support the INR. Q4: How will these measures impact the USDINR? In a surprise move, BI announced an off-cycle interest rate hike of 25bp on 9 June, following a 50bp increase at its May meeting. This brings the BI rate to 5.5%. In addition to intensifying its traditional FX intervention measures, BI has introduced incentives to reduce swap hedging costs for foreign investors by 10%. This effectively provides a rebate on the current 10% tax paid by foreign investors when investing in Indonesian assets and is aimed at attracting more foreign inflows. We think these measures demonstrate the authorities' growing sense of urgency to stabilize the rupiah. By taking proactive steps to support the currency, BI is signaling its commitment to both mitigate the impact of external factors on the Indonesian economy and maintain financial stability Q7: What recent announcements were made by BI and the Ministry of Finance? Prior to the off-cycle interest rate hike meeting, BI had emphasized its intention to enhance monetary and fiscal coordination with the finance ministry. Notably, both authorities have agreed to allow market forces to determine the yields of Indonesian government bonds (SBNs), by ceasing to purchase them. Furthermore, BI will gradually sell its existing holdings of SBNs and increase the yields on its own instruments, such stated objective of these measures is to lift yields to a level that attracts foreign investors. BI Governor Perry Warjiyo has himself encouraged investors to purchase bonds, emphasizing their current attractive levels. Additionally, BI reaffirmed its commitment to maintaining ample liquidity and supporting money growth at a rate of 10%. To achieve this, BI has reintroduced weekly repo auctions with tenors to inject liquidity into the market. What are the implications for USDIDR? While BI is taking a positive step by allowing yields to rise, we think the IDR could depreciate further as the market demands a higher premium and foreign inflows take time to return. The significant decline in FX reserves, adjusted for the forward book and foreign currency liabilities, made BI's interest rate hikes necessary. This is similar to the situation in 2018, when BI raised interest rates to protect the IDR from excessive depreciation, despite low inflation. We see a risk these hikes start to hurt economic growth at a time when retail sales are already slowing, which would put further negative pressure on the currency. In the long term, though, this move may be beneficial for the IDR, as a widening interest rate differential with the USD should eventually attract inflows into the currency. The fiscal challenges posed by higher oil prices, and concerns about Indonesia's low tax base, will likely lead investors to demand a higher premium for Indonesian assets. In the long term, we believe Indonesia has significant potential, driven by a young population and rapid economic growth, which can improve the country's fiscal deficit. However, the economy faces some short-term challenges before it can realize its full potential. Q10: How do the RBI and BI policies compare?
