# Europe Blog - France, the ECB and the challenging path back to equilibrium

On overall Economics Europe Blog Date 2 October 2026

Rising inflation, tighter monetary policy and higher global yields were always going to put pressure on Europe’s fiscal weak links. This week, against the backdrop of the 2027 Budget announcement, the market pressures on France accelerated. In this note, we do three things. First, we warn against complacency. The euro area has a much better stability architecture today but there are still cyclical and structural factors that add to vulnerabilities and the potential for contagion in situations like this. Second, we think about the key elements required to unwind the current episode of market stress. Most fundamentally and importantly, the market needs confidence that France is moving towards a sustainable fiscal trajectory. In terms of backstops, these are a function of EU cohesion. Third, we think about the ECB reaction function. The market is starting to dial back the expected volume of ECB rate hikes. Earlier this week, even before the French pressures accelerated, President Lagarde said the recent rise in yields could slow growth and weaken the pass-through of energy inflation. We also consider the circumstances in which the ECB can intervene in the market using TPI. The “country specific fundamental” nature of the rise in OAT yields raises the hurdle for TPI deployment. However, the greater the sense of irrational pricing, the market overshooting fundamentals and spillover/contagion effects, the lower the bar for ECB intervention. No time for complacency The ECB will be paying very close attention to what is happening in government bond markets at the moment. A monetary and currency union without political or fiscal union – and with an incomplete banking union – has in-built, inherent vulnerabilities, and especially so at times of stress. The absence of currency risk – money can flow from one member state to another and maintain 100% of its euro value – exposes its member states to destabilising flight-to-safety dynamics at times of significant uncertainty. Through the reallocation of sovereign bond portfolios, these money flows get reflected in widening sovereign spreads and, in turn, into broader asset price and lending dynamics. These “non-fundamental” drivers of volatility can impair the transmission mechanism. The concern is there are fundamental risks, too. There are public debt vulnerabilities in Europe. Public finances have not recovered from the pandemic shock. France ranks highest on our fiscal vulnerability indicator – defined as the distance from EU fiscal targets and recommendations – and has the added challenge of a weak NIIP. Fiscal vulnerability is a spectrum and others have to be careful about their public finances, too (see our recent note on Italy). The current situation is not like the debt crises 10 -15 years ago. That was preceded by a credit bubble which via a banking shock translated into increased fiscal pressures; the exception was Greece which was a genuine fiscal crisis. After a decade of rebuilding, the European banking system is much stronger in general and French banks are not significantly exposed to OATs. A sovereign-banking negative feedback loop should not be a primary driver of events – but should be watched as a potential secondary driver. Europe also has a much more developed and broad-based stability architecture today (e.g., ESM, SRM, TPI, etc). But there are differences, too. The combination of generally higher levels of deficit and debt post-pandemic, structural spending needs (strategic autonomy including defence) and rising global yields have narrowed the fiscal room for manoeuvre for many governments across Europe and made it harder to comply with the bloc’s fiscal rules. Pathway to stability Ultimately, and most importantly and fundamentally, resolution of this episode of stress requires investor confidence in a credible and sustainable fiscal trajectory in France. The hurdle is that the divided parliament since 2024 has found it difficult to reduce the deficit much. The government expects the deficit in 2026 to be 5.4% of GDP a nd is targeting a deficit of 5.0% of GDP in the draft 2027 Budget. This might be somewhat ambitious and we think the deficit target could rise to 5.2-5.3% and with growth likely to underperform the government’s assumption, the 2027 deficit might be unchanged at 5.4%. In any case, the 2027 budget is only part of the uncertainty. There is also the 2027 election and the uncertainty of what an RN government means for the trajectory of French fiscal policy and France’s relations with the EU (for a deep-dive on what RN means for France, see our three-part note: Part 1: The path to power, Part 2: The policy agenda, and Part 3: Europe). The second element of effective resolution is market confidence in the cohesiveness of the EU. After multiple crises, Europe now has a well-developed stability architecture, but the credibility of EU backstops is a function of the cohesiveness of EU politics. Even deploying the precautionary ESM facilities would require a unanimous agreement among euro area mem ber states. The centre ground in European politics is narrowing, and politics is drifting to the right. Unanimity on burden-sharing policies could be more difficult to achieve. The third element of effective resolution is market confidence in the availability of the ECB balance sheet. Having risen from EUR1.2 trillion in 2007 to EUR8.8 trillion at the peak in 2022, the ECB balance sheet has now contracted by 33% as the TLTRO3 bank liquidity mature d and the QE asset portfolios passively roll off. Investors find it easier to re-engage in the market when the official sector is on the same side. With the ECB nervous about inflation in the face of the energy shock, the hurdle for resorting to the balance sheet could be high. Nonetheless, the emerging stress raises legitimate questions about the ECB response and the use of its balance sheet tools in particular. The scope for an ECB response One of the common questions from clients this week has been the ECB’s reaction to the situation in France and the conditions u nder which the ECB could respond, and how. In this section, we will look at the two bases for adjusting monetary policy – price stability and financial stability/smooth transmission – and how the situation in France might influence the two. Under ‘smooth transmission’, we will focus on the conditions under which the ECB can deploy TPI (Transmission Protection Instrument). But first, a quick reminder of expectations for the near-term path of monetary policy – and the role that higher yields and tighter financial conditions can play. Our baseline view is that the ECB will hike once more to a terminal rate of 2.75% in December, with risks of hiking to 3 %. The energy inflation shock is larger and more persistent than we were assuming, but there is still no evidence of second-round effects and no compelling evidence of indirect effects. With core inflation nevertheless sticky at around 2.5% (consumer electronics in particular preventing the expected convergence to 2% in 2026) and the economy proving resilient, we think a mildly restrictive stance is appropriate (the ECB believes the upper end of the range of neutral is 2.50%). Until yesterday, the market had been pricing a terminal rate at 3.50% in 2027. Concerns about France have seen the market take out one hike already (at the time of writing, the implied terminal rate in 2027 is 3.27%). The ECB i s sensitive to the market. Financial conditions can affect the path of monetary policy, as President Lagarde mentioned in her discussion with the EU Parliament on Monday. Long-term rates have risen notably “which will slow growth and reduce pass-through by more than projected in our September exercise”. With a lower pass-through of the inflation pressure, there will be less need for the ECB to take a ‘forceful response’, and, thus, a lower probability of a clearly restrictive terminal rate. We believe hiking above 3% would require clear evidence of second-round effects, strong growth or a belief that nominal neutral is closer to 3% than 2%. Basis #1. Price Stability The ECB can justify a monetary policy response if an event threatens the sustained return of inflation to target in the medium-term. The ECB is on a tightening path because of the risk of the energy price shock spilling over and inflation above target. The situation with France would need to be significant and sustained to overpower the energy shock completely. There are several potential transmission channels for France to affect the outlook for euro area price stability:

- Tighter financial conditions. Events in France would need to tighten financial conditions to the extent that the growth and inflation outlook is notably weaker in France, or through contagion, in the rest of the euro area. We can use the ECB’s new macro-finance FCI (financial conditions index) to track the risk (see below).
- Higher economic uncertainty. Political and policy uncertainty could weaken consumption, investment and employment – and, ultimately, inflation – in France or through contagion into other connected member states.
- Lower inflation expectations. The tighter are financial conditions and the higher is economic uncertainty, the greater the risk that inflation and inflation expectations weaken, too (inflation expectations have been relatively low in France for a while now, but then so, too, has core inflation). The ECB will be assessing the potential downside risks against the concerns that highly salient energy prices c ause a rise in inflation expectations. According to the ECB’s own MF-FCI model, euro area-wide financial conditions have tightened noticeably since the start of the year, will a large proportion of that tightening occurring over the last month. However, the tightening has been driven more by energy prices influencing market expectations for ECB policy rather than sovereign spreads. President Lagar de’s recent comments at the European Parliament reveal a sensitivity to the recent rise in yields, implying that tighter financial conditions could crowd out rate hikes. OAT yields are rising (Figure 1). This challenges public finances. However, an important question is the extent to which this will feed into banking system stress and sharply tighten the flow of funding to the economy. French banks are well capitalised and are not highly exposed to OATs, as we discussed previously. There is not much difference in the credit impulses across the Big4 member states (Figure 2). Figure 1: 10Y sovereign yields are rising fast in France Figure 2: Credit impulse by country (pp of GDP) Haver Analytics LP All told, the recent tightening of longer-term rates is leading to tighter financial conditions across the euro area, not just in France. As Lagarde argues, this tightening of conditions could assist the ECB in leaning against the risk of greater inflationary pressure passthrough, thereby assisting monetary policy and limiting the need for the ECB to raise rates as high as markets currently expect. Although political uncertainty might be high in France, overall uncertainty, as measured by the European Commission’s uncertainty indicator, is lower in France than the euro area average (Figure 3). Over recent history, uncertainty in France has generally behaved like the rest of the euro area. Between 2024 -25, uncertainty in France did trend higher relate to the rest of the euro area as France underwent a snap election followed by challenges of passing the 2024 and 2025 budgets. Financial Stability/Smooth Transmission A monetary policy response can also be justified by events that threaten financial stability and in particular threaten the smooth transmission of monetary policy. At the heart of its monitoring of market developments at the moment, the ECB will be watching for “unwarranted and disorderly” market dynamics. The ECB will be trying to differentiate “non-fundamental” from “country specific fundamental” drivers. The bar for a policy response is lower with the former, higher with the latter. What the ECB means by “non-fundamental” drivers is illiquid markets, exaggerated asset price corrections, dislocated intermediation and market freezes. These could impair the transmission of monetary policy and threaten financial stability. The ECB will be assessing fragmentation pressures, too. These could lead to self-fulfilling ‘flight-to-safety’ dynamics that can further undermine transmission and stability. As our Rates Strategists noted recently, there are early signs that the OAT cheapening is shifting from an orderly, largely idiosyncratic repricing to a more disorderly phase (Figure 4). There is some initial evidence of contagion to other EGBs, signs of bear flattening in the OAT spread curve and growing dislocations, which is something the ECB will certainly be attentive to. The spread between 10-year government bond yields and the OIS rate is currently increasing, and is now marginally above the levels seen following the outbreak of the US-Iran war (Figure 5). While the picture looks slightly rosier if we consider swaps instead of OIS, the underlying narrative still holds. The spread did rise further during the pandemic. However, because we are starting from a higher level, the spreads warrant further monitoring. The day before the ECB triggered flexible PEPP reinvestments in 2022, Isabel Schnabel gave a speech on market fragmentation and how it challenges monetary policy in a single currency zone. Schnabel was clearly linking market pressure to concerns about the extent of monetary tightening that could be required to restore price stability. In other words, the monetary tightening/inflation was seen as a ‘common shock’ to the euro area – the market stresses were not seen as “country specific” — and this justified a policy response. Schnabel mentioned an accelerating increase in sovereign risk premia (note: plural, not singular), mor e challenging liquidity conditions and increasing daily rises in bond yields as factors supporting a rising threat to transmission. In 2026, the ECB is tightening monetary policy, and there has been no concern expressed about spillover effects or threats to the smooth transmission of monetary policy yet. However, market conditions were deteriorating rapidly this week. An LTRO-based response cannot be ruled out. The argument is two-fold:
1. it avoids some of the political cost of asset purchases;
2. it can help limit the risk of a shock propagating via the banking sector. Instead, a LTRO-based response gives banks room to act as a stabilizing agent. Such a response would become more plausible if signs of contagion via the banking system emerged. Transmission Protection Instrument (TPI) In response to the market pressures in 2022, the ECB announced a two-part policy. The first was flexible PEPP reinvestments. The second was the creation of the Transmission Protection Instrument (TPI). In 2022, the maturing principal from the PEPP portfolio was being reinvested. When PEPP was created in 2020, a key feature/innovation relative to the APP was the flexibility to deviate from the capital key. In 2022, the ECB viewed the pandemic as having left lasting vulnerabilities on the economy and these were judged to be contributing to the uneven transmission of monetary policy. This gave the ECB the basis to introduce flexible PEPP reinvestments as a stabilisation device. With PEPP reinvestments having now ended and it being perhaps legally difficult to justify re-opening a pandemic-era facility (at least without a very significant shock), the focus for any future action by the ECB is likely to be the Transmission Protection Instrument. TPI deployment: three-step process First, the ECB would conduct an analysis of what is driving the market pressure and identify whether it is country-specific fundamentals or not. Second, the ECB would determine whether a member state suffering pressures is eligible for TPI. It would do this by looking at factors such as compliance with the EU fiscal framework, absence of severe macroeconomic imbalances, fiscal sustainability and sound and sustainable macroeconomic policies. In our view, as long as the member state in question is making a good faith attempt to correct the course of public finances, the Commission will find it difficult to conclude that the country is off-side vis-à-vis the EU fiscal rules and the ECB will view the country as eligible for TPI. Third, the ECB would decide whether intervention in the market would be proportionate to the desired monetary policy stance. The latter is to ensure the dominance of the ECB’s price stability mandate. For more on the TPI, see here. To the extent that what is driving market pressure today is a reassessment of country fundamentals i France (the trajectory of fiscal policy), the ECB does not have a basis to deploy TPI. To do so would create moral hazard. This does not necessarily mean that TPI is completely off the agenda, however. First, the ECB could still justify using TPI for France if markets reach the point of pricing irrational risk. In other words, if the ECB felt that the market was overshooting the deterioration in underlying fundamentals. There would still be a moral hazard objection and intervention in this situation would probably need clear evidence that sovereign stress is spilling over into the domestic financial system. However, if the European Commission finds France’s Draft Budgetary Plan to be broadly compliant, eligibility for TPI could be established (draft budgets are submitted to the Commission by 15 October and opinions are published on 24 November). However, there would still be uncertainty about the path of fiscal policy after next year’s election. Second, to the extent that other member states suffering from contagion and contagion is by definition not a “country-specific fundamental” risk premium, then contagion could give rise to TPI for other member states. The knowledge that TPI is available to lean against contagion should help reduce the likelihood of contagion. Possible conditions under which TPI could be used for France Above, we mentioned the possibility of TPI being used in case of:
1. irrational pricing of risk or
2. material contagion. Exactly what would trigger the conditions for TPI is inevitably (an d in some ways deliberately) ambiguous. Here, we offer a few indicative scenarios in which TPI deployment could potentially occur. Scenario 1: Pragmatic reform Spiralling yields pushes the French government towards a large-scale debt crisis. To avoid the crisis, or perhaps as a condition for support from the European Commission, the Government announces a dramatic fiscal consolidation plan and passes the budget under Article 49.3. However, despite the passing of the legislation, markets doubt the validity or practicality, perhaps because other parties upset over the decisions campaign against the budget during the upc oming presidential election. As a result, yields remain high. The ECB assesses the situation and decides that the Government’s plan is credible, and, hence, the level of market yields is unwarranted and driven by speculation about the presidential election. It would then be able to activate the TPI of compress the spreads back towards sustainable levels. Scenario 2: Market dysfunction The OAT market suffers a significant loss of liquidity and Government auctions fail. Rather than address the issue of whether such a reaction i s warranted on country-level fundamentals, the ECB decides the OAT’s important role as collateral in the repo market threatens the European financial sector’s collateral framework. The ECB, therefore,decides to step in to restore orderly market making. In its communication, the ECB states that the intervention provides a backstop to market liquidity. Scenario 3: Systemic contagion This scenario sees the TPI initially deployed to protect other parts of the euro area but ends up also purchasing French debt when the situation does not fully stabilise. Investor panic surrounding OATs spills over to the euro area periphery, leading investors to sell bonds of other highly indebted countries. After a careful assessment, the ECB declares that the rising yields in other countries are not driven by country-specific fundamentals and activate s the TPI to protect policy transmission in the wider euro area by buying bonds for countries like Italy, Spain, Greece, etc. After some limited success, the ECB determines that to safeguard the entire euro area transmission mechanism, it must stabilise also purchase semi-core bonds to help stabilise the periphery arguing that the yields on French debt (among others) have become unwarranted and disorderly. Such an intervention would likely be legally challenged by other, more frugal, countries and questions about moral hazard would likely be raised. Outright Monetary Transactions (OMT) OMT was created as a policy tool in the wake of Mario Draghi’s “whatever it takes” verbal intervention in July 2012. OMT is the ECB’s ultimate sovereign backstop, for when a euro area member state has been cut off from market financing. OMT is conditional on the member state in question being under a full ESM bailout programme or a precautionary financial assistance programme (with primary market purchase agreement). OMT is not relevant in the case of France today. BOE LDI crisis response: A new, more agile, template The ECB has never been afraid to innovate, and the ECB’s monetary policy strategy makes it clear that the Governing Council will “continue to respond flexibly to new challenges as they arise and will consider, as needed, new policy instruments”. QE is not necessarily as efficient a monetary stimulus tool as originally hoped. EUR2.6tr of ECB QE over 2015 -2018 only lowered the EA-weighted 5Y yield by 80bp (see speech by Isabel Schnabel here). However, asset purchasing can still be a powerful financial stability or transmission tool at times of market stress. A good example is the BoE purchases to quash the LDI crisis in 2023 and their quick reversal (see speech by Isabel Schnabel here). A clear exit strategy is what differentiates asset purchases for market stabilisation purposes from regular quantitative easing (QE). Schnabel referred to the BOE LDI response as how an exit strategy “can be designed successfully”. For an inflation targeting central bank, “there can be no trade off between market stabilisation and price stability”. In other words, despite a higher bar for asset purchasing relative to a decade ago, even the hawks at the ECB have not ruled them out, in certain circumstances and with sufficient exit provisions to ensure monetary policy agility. Bottom line As long as the market pressures are “country specific” and rational and contagion is limited, the ECB will likely continue to watch rather than act. Market discipline could help ensure the 2027 Budget is agreed wi th minimal slippage relative to the initial proposal and could see RN move towards a clearer stability-oriented fiscal policy. The greater the sense of irrational pricing, the market overshooting fundamentals and spillover /contagion effects, the lower the bar for ECB intervention.
