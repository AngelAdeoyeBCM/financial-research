# Sunday Tea with BNPP: The Fed under Warsh

. 31/05/2026 1

BNP P ARIBAS GLOBAL M ARKE TS AP P.

## KEY MESSAGES

- A strong US nonfarm payrolls print this week could push the market to price in more risk of the Fed hiking rates.
- We do not believe the Fed should take comfort from what’s priced into TIPS. Inflation expectations look fragile.
- We anticipate market volatility headed into the first round of Colombia’s presidential election tonight. Since January, we have looked for the Fed to keep rates on hold all year based on expectations for resilient US growth, a stable labor market and sticky inflation. Over the course of 2026, and especially since the Middle East conflict began, the challenges for the Fed in returning inflation to 2% have become more evident. The probability of rate hikes starting later this year has increased markedly. Our longstanding view is that policymakers would hike rates if either of two conditions were met: unemployment seem ing likely to move below 4%, a level at which wage-price amplification becomes easier to envision, or long-term inflation expectations (LTIE) show ing further evidence of de-anchor ing. The likelihood of both conditions being met has increased over the past month. For this week’s US jobs report, our expectation is for an 85k gain in nonfarm payrolls, a steady unemployment rate and a mild bounce in earnings growth. Looking further out, our base case has the unemployment rate averag ing 4.3% this year and fall ing to 4.1% at the end of 2027, below both consensus and Fed estimates. However, the details of this report will be especially important. We will be watching for ongoing signs of cyclical strength, which would tend to presage further momentum ahead. One key area to monitor is youth unemployment, which we see as a ‘canary in the coal mine’ for the job market. The rise in youth unemployment into 2025 was notable but has reversed swiftly this year (Figure 1). We see younger workers as a key gauge of the net effect of two cross currents in the US economy: the diffusion of artificial intelligence tools (which looks set to displace e mployees in some industries) and cyclical resilience in activity coupled with AI o ptimism (which we think is likely to increase demand for labor). While last year’s US labor market narrative focused on downside risks for new entrants, our read of incoming data is that the chance of better-than - expected outcomes is underappreciated. Fig. 1: Youth unemployment rate well down from 2025 Sources: BLS, Macrobond, BNP Paribas Indeed, i contrast to what see ms like the prevailing narrative, we find that younger workers are more exposed to job categories that are cyclically sensitive but AI-insensitive compared to their mid - or late-career counterparts. To be sure, demand for new software engineers may prove to be lower than in the past (though we note job postings are rising), but this accounts for less than 1% of workers aged 16- 24. Rather, they mostly work in leisure, retail, construction and in-person services jobs. Given strong recent labor data and the rising level of real monetary stimulus entering the US economy, another ‘boomy’ jobs print for May would likely lead us to expect unemployment to follow a declining trajectory over the year, potentially taking it to 4% by year end. The Fed would have difficulty overlooking this.. 31/05/2026 2 Meanwhile, on the inflation side, oil prices saw downward pressure last week due to expectations of a US-Iran memorandum of understanding potentially being signed. We remain skeptical concerning both the prospect of genuine de-escalation (but a halfway house solution to continue the ceasefire and keep talking with no real solution in sight does seem quite possible) and the likelihood of shipping flows through the Strait of Hormuz normalizing anytime soon. With these flows curtailed, global energy balances remain very tight, as evidenced by strong draws on US crude stocks and slow gas storage injections in Europe. Absent material change, we expect significant pressure points to develop in both oil and gas markets around the end of Q2/early Q3. Regardless of how the conflict plays out, with the ongoing energy price shock be ing the fourth successive jolt to inflation in the past five years, it is already beginning to impact expectations. The latest University of Michigan long-term inflation expectations print shot upward to 3.9% from 3.4%, making a move above 4% – returning to last year’s levels – increasingly likely. While Fed officials have cited the stability of long-term breakevens (Figure 2) as a sign of anchored LTIE, last week we wrote a note detailing why this stability is misleading and creates false optimism in our eyes. We think long-term breakevens are less swayed by evolving inflation risks and more by risk appetite and liquidity-driven investor flows in the TIPS market. Moreover, b ecause this market only opened for business in 1997 and became reasonably liquid in 2003, there is no historical evidence to demonstrate how (or if) US breakevens would respond to a broad-based loss of confidence in the Fed. In contrast, the Michigan consumer survey was conducted through the 1970s and there is literature on how the de-anchoring in LTIE played out through it. As such, should surveys of inflation expectations continue to rise, we think this should materially increase market pricing of rate hikes as well. Fig. 2: Stability of breakevens creates false optimism Sources: Bloomberg, BNP Paribas Were hikes to come, we think they would be aimed at withdraw ing 2025’s three ‘insurance ’ cuts. In doing so, we would expect the Fed to follow its playbook for the unwinding in 1999 of similar cuts that follow ed the failure of Long-Term Capital Management, which Chair Greenspan feared would create a recessionary shock to investor confidence. Like today’s environment, policymakers subsequently faced an unexpectedly resilient economy amid the internet boom, with a tightening labor market and upside inflation risks. If the Fed were to hike later this year, we think the most likely outcome would be withdraw ing last year’s ‘insurance ’ cuts at sequential meetings. While we acknowledge that new Chair Warsh will likely seek to avoid raising rates in his early days of leading the Fed, our view is that regardless of who heads the central bank, monetary policy will be determined by the US economic outlook and by the Fed’s existing policy goals and strategy, in whatever direction the data directs. Outside of monetary policy, with Warsh sworn in just a week ago, market focus will also likely turn to his other major objectives – communication and the balance sheet. Last week, we published our first deep dive on how Fed balance sheet policy could look under the new leadership. We expect a gradual, predictable and well-signposted effort to reduce the balance sheet. Changes to supervisory and regulatory requirements are a necessary prerequisite for this to happen, in our view. In our base case, the balance sheet shrinks by ~$700bn, starting in 2028. Finally, the first round of Colombia’s presidential election is taking place today with relevant investment implications based on three possible outcomes:
1. the market gain ing confidence of the opposition win ning,
2. the market realiz ing that the government-backed candidate’s chances are better than first expected and
3. an inconclusive result. In the first case (market bullish), the Coltes curve should show larger upside relative to the COP with belly maturities (30s-35s) representing the sweet spot, in our view. In the second case (market bearish), we think USDCOP reacts sharply as the COP has remained isolated from political anxiety (so far).
