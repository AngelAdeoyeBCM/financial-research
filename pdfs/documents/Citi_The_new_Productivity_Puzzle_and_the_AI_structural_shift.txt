# The new Productivity Puzzle and the AI structural shift

UK Economics

## CITI’S TAKE

Since the GFC, UK productivity growth has been poor. Various theories have been put forward to explain this pattern, but none have fully described the problem. It now appears we have the reverse. By some metrics, UK productivity growth is finally on the rise. Building on our productivity analysis from earlier this year, we show evidence it is primarily driven by ICT, although other sectors that benefit from AI advancements also play a role. We think this development is both positive and meaningful, although we remain cautious. The UK is experiencing a new productivity puzzle — After 15 years of sluggish productivity growth (let’s call it the “OG” productivity puzzle), the opposite is finally happening, although the causes of this boost to productivity are not yet fully clear. We think this is a meaningful shift, but it is sensitive to the data used — The improvement is best seen when using HMRC’s RTI (PAYE) data. Annual productivity growth averaged 1.2% per quarter since 2024Q3. The white-collar sector is leading the way, and ICT is the flag bearer — Since 2025, ICT contributed over a third of the annual productivity (PAYE) growth, despite comprising less than 10% of the entire economy. We think the productivity boost is correlated with ICT’s pervasive use of AI — Both professional, scientific & technical sector, and information & communication sectors use AI like no other sectors. Nearly half of firms in the information & communication sector report that three-quarters of their staff use AI on a daily basis. While not causation, this is highly correlated with increased productivity in that sector. The outcome on inflation is unclear. On the one hand, higher productivity growth would push down on inflation. Competing theories from academics and policymakers have been put forth to ascribe the causes of the UK Productivity Puzzle (e.g. See Barnett et al, 2014, or Van Reenen and Yang, 2024), but none have fully dominated the narrative. Now, the reverse appears to be happening. By some measures, UK productivity growth is finally picking up, and in a meaningful way too. Building on our previous productivity analysis from earlier this year, we show further evidence that, whilst the pick-up is broad-based, it is primarily driven by the “white collar” sector (excluding finance). This sector has the clearest productivity enhancing benefits from AI advancements. It is most obvious in the ICT sector, where cumulative productivity growth since 2022 is 32% (using HMRC’s PAYE measure). However, this finding is sensitive to the data used and the comparison points. Whilst—by some measures—the pick-up in sectoral productivity has been broad, it really depends on the vantage-point. Namely, first, the time-period being examined and, second, the variables used to calculate measured productivity. Productivity has grown inconsistently, so different starting points can yield different outcomes. Nevertheless, using the PAYE productivity measure, ICT is the one sector that has consistently delivered strong annual growth every quarter over the last three years, no matter how you slice the data. On average, YoY productivity growth of that sector has been greater than 7% per quarter since 2023Q3. (Aside from Transport, which averaged 2.6% annual productivity growth during this time, only two other sectors delivered positive productivity growth per quarter during this time period – Public Administration & Defence, and Healthcare at 2.6% and 1.4% respectively – but both of these sectors are distortionary given the outsized role the public sector plays there). While it is undoubtedly true that HMRC’s PAYE employment measure provide the clearest story of a boost, it also excludes the self-employed—that is a cohort of workers that is growing rapidly. Part-time self-employed workers have formed the lion’s share of the increase in self-employment. These data are captured by the Labour Force Survey, but this survey has well-publicised statistical issues and is still undergoing refurbishment, so it is difficult to know how robust this increase is. That said, calculations pooling together self- and PAYE-employment, still show a pick-up in productivity (albeit it is weaker than with the PAYE data alone). We think the fall in PAYE employees (which comes from the reliable PAYE data source) and the concurrent rise in self-employment in the LFS are connected. We believe the catalyst has been the chronically high unit labour costs faced by firms, given strong nominal wage increases and costly labour-market regulation. T ogether, we think this has put upward pressure on firms’ costs and has been the tipping point in the shedding of employees. This is not an AI story. One consequence of these changes, however, may be that

1. employers have been replacing permanent employees with a more flexible (and cheaper) contract-based workforce, and/or
2. using AI, where it permits. If true, then these employee protections seem to have had the reverse effect on the very employees they were designed to shield (at least in the short-term). The unintended consequence, therefore may have been to catapult UK businesses to swiftly embrace cost-cutting AI technology—a coping mechanism for the rapidly rising labour costs. The new Productivity Puzzle and the AI structural shift UK Economics The ONS BICS results support this story. By far, the biggest reported rise of AI usage amongst firms is in the information & communication sector. But we also note that all sectors, to varying degrees—both at the intensive and extensive margin—are using AI technology primarily to improve their processes, programming and data analysis, and increasingly also for “creativity” use. Absent a market correction, we think the long-term impact of the AI shift will be to structurally displace some employees in the labour market, specifically jobs that can be easily automated. While the use of AI is reported to be the highest in the most obvious sectors (information & communication, and professional, scientific, and technical), the types of things AI is being used for are “improving business operations” and “providing a product or service to customers”. One could argue that these requirements are applicable to all sectors. Indeed, amongst all businesses who use AI regardless of sector, 59% reported using it to improve business operations.1 While we remain optimistic that the productivity improvements are real, we are also cautious and for two reasons. One, much of the productivity “improvement” relies on the administrative PAYE data, which excludes the self-employed, a rapidly expanding cohort (if one believes the LFS). As we don’t have a reliable estimate of how much this increase is, it is hard to be sure. Two, the productivity improvements – at present – do not appear to be coinciding with a noticeable growth in GDP. Put it differently, they are arising due to a slowdown in hiring. Without a market correction, long-term labour-market displacement can lead to structurally higher unemployment, pushing up on U*. The long-term path for interest rates, however, will depend on the positive productivity forces pushing down on inflation on the one hand, against the rising unemployment pressures pushing up on inflation in the opposite direction.
