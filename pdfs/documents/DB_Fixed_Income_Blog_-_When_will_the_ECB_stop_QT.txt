# Fixed Income Blog - When will the ECB stop QT

Rates Fixed Income Blog Date 19 October 2026

Higher yields have revived questions about whether the ECB will stop QT. This conflates balance sheet implementation, the monetary policy stance and fragmentation concerns. The current framework and money-market signals suggest that point remains some way off, while recent rhetoric continues to point to the policy rate as the main line of defence. When will the ECB stop QT? This has been increasingly asked in recent weeks prompted by the rise in yields globally, and more recently, the widening in OAT spreads. This conflates balance sheet implementation, the monetary policy stance and fragmentation concerns. Under the demand-driven framework outlined in 2024, the launch of the structural portfolio will occur only once the balance sheet starts to grow and after bank demand for the OMOs has risen substantially. The sequencing of events is particularly relevant. The implementation of the framework is separate from the monetary policy stance, which is set by the level of the policy rate. An earlier pause would therefore require a separate rationale. Right now, the balance sheet and excess liquidity continue to decline as runoff is not being replaced by usage of the tender operations. Over time, the balance sheet will stabilise and eventually start to grow again. However, the composition of the ECB balance sheet will shift towards OMOs (borrowed reserves) and ultimately include a structural tender and a structural portfolio. An important distinction between the structural portfolio and historical purchase programmes is that the APP/PEPP were designed t o change the stance of monetary policy, while the structural portfolio exists to implement the existing stance. This difference is likely to entail a different composition. This has not been outlined yet, but so far ECB speakers and speeches point towards a shorter maturity, tilted towards government bonds. There have also been questions as to whether a new structural tender may be launched early to support EGB spreads. While we think it unlikely for now, the terms will be important. ECB rhetoric has suggested that it is likely to be variable rate, restricted quantity (unlike the MRO/LTRO), so as not to provide an advantage versus market pricing. If so, it is not a re-run of the 3Y LTRO. No decisions have been made with respect to the design of either the tenders or the portfolio, nor has the ECB communicated the thresholds needed to launch these. It will be a case of learning by doing. With the current framework in mind, money markets suggest we are not there yet. We have argued that liquidity is less ample than the headline excess liquidity, and adjusted liquidity metrics suggest there is still scope for funding conditions to tighten more or faster than currently priced. This is also likely to imply more volatility in money market rates. Notably a recent article from the Bank of Finland reiterates that it is important for the ECB to tolerate this, otherwise “we risk short-circuiting the very framework we have chosen”. Market conditions are likely to be more relevant than specific spread levels, as indicators to monitor (see the STIR Chartbook for more details). As our chartbook highlights, spreads have normalised towards the DFR, and usage of the current ECB operations is minimal though creeping higher. The normalisation process has gone from the removal of collateral scarcity and is now moving towards scarcity of reserves. In summary, current money market conditions do not point to an imminent launch of a structural tender or the portfolio. That leaves a different question of whether the ECB could interrupt the process because higher yields are tightening financial conditions. One argument is that stopping/pausing QT reduces the likelihood of adding a policy-induced contribution to global factors that are raising yields. Here too the framework provides guidance. The framework implements the stance but doesn’t interfere with it. The stance itself is set by the policy rate (deposit facility rate). Indeed, the most recent account notes that the repricing of long-end yields “could have implications for appropriate policy rates in the future”. It does not say it has implications for the path of portfolio run-off. Given the gradual and well communicated pace of run-off, a pause risks causing more confusion around how and when to restart. In terms of the market reaction so far, t he macro FCI peaked on the 29 th of September. The sovereign spread component has risen but is offset by lower nominal yields. As highlighted here, terminal rate pricing has also declined, indicating that the market is already doing some of the adjustment via monetary policy expectations. In addition, our free float framework suggests term premium is more global than local, and merely pausing or stopping QT would have a limited effect on yields. The other angle to questions around stopping QT is whether this can be used to address fragmentation risks – namely to focus reinvestments on a single country. Using PEPP would involve a two-step decision – to pause run-offs, and then to refocus reinvestments on a single sovereign. It is debatable whether re-launching a tool created in response to the pandemic w ould stand up to scrutiny. It would also raise questions around hierarchy of the different instruments (not to mention political interference). The BoE’s recent decision on QT has been used as an example where other market signals have mattered. It adjusted the sales programme to end long-end sales, having already skewed them away from longs the year before. As the BoE notes, this demonstrates a sensitivity to market conditions. However, it has not stopped QT. Indeed, it simultaneously committed to a multi - year programme albeit at a slower pace than in previous years. While there was a clear outperformance on the day, the macro impact has not lasted. GBP 10s30s TP and the market model residual (versus 5s10s TP and vol) have recovered since and 30y swap spreads are largely unchanged (though have outperformed vs 10y). It has had a more favourable relative value impact on specific bonds and the ultras. This speaks to the uncertainty around the impact of stopping QT, given we consider the end of long-end sales a more permanent decision than one to pause QT in the Eurozone. To conclude: under the current framework, the ECB remains a long way from launching a structural bond portfolio based on money market signals. Recent rhetoric also highlights that the policy rate remains the main line of defence against higher yields tightening financial conditions more than warranted.
