# Fed Notes - How high might they go

Economics Fed Notes Date 18 September 2026

- We discuss Chair Warsh’s framing of the tightening cycle and what it might imply about how high rates might go this cycle. To do this, we show the Fed’s forecasts can be understood as following a Taylor rule with a higher r-star estimate.
- As we reiterated on Wednesday, the signals from the September FOMC meeting reinforced our baseline expectation that the Fed is likely to deliver 50bps more of tightening, leaving the fed funds rate at 4.375% by the end of Q1 2027 (see “Removing a “dose of accommodation” helps the inflation go down”). The simple analysis in this piece suggests that, based only on reasonable scenarios for r-star, there is plausibly 40bps of upside risk to that expectation, consistent with the just over 100bps of total tightening this cycle that is currently priced. How high could the Fed go? Coming into the September FOMC meeting, a critical question was how Chair Warsh would frame the tightening cycle that lies ahead. We posited at least three possibilities. First, he could have argued that the hike was for risk management reasons. In this framing, the Fed’s modal economic outlook was mostly unchanged, but with upside risks to inflation intensifying and the labor market stable it made sense to take out insurance against stickier inflation. Second, Warsh could have characterized the rate increase as part of a process to undo the 75bps of rate cuts delivered last year, which have left the Fed overinsured against downside risks to the labor market (see “Overinsured?”). Third, he could have argued that rate increases were intended to return policy to a sufficiently restrictive stance against a backdrop of a rising neutral rate. These options were neither mutually exclusive nor exhaustive. The first two options were more dovish framings in our view, as they would provide a concrete sense of the anticipated terminal rate from the tightening cycle. Risk management hikes suggest at most three hikes. Unwinding last year’s cuts frames the current hiking cycle as topping out at 75bps of rate increases. The third possibility was the most hawkish from a market perspective. It would leave expectations for the terminal rate open-ended, to be determined by the degree of tightening in financial conditions ultimately required to return inflation to target with sufficient speed. Warsh’s press conference mostly leaned on the third description. As we detailed in our recap note (see “Removing a “dose of accommodation” helps the inflation go down”), he described the September decision as removing “a dose of Matthew Luzzetti, Ph.D. accommodation.” He also noted that the notion that financial conditions are not restrictive was now “widely shared across the Committee.” Though he was reluctant to explicitly describe it as such, the implication seemed to be that the neutral rate is on the rise – a conclusion evident in the longer-run dots of the SEP as well – and that policy had become unintentionally overly accommodative. With this open-ended framing, what is a reasonable expectation for how high the fed funds rate might go? To begin this assessment, we first observe that the Fed’s updated median SEP projections can be fit nearly perfectly using a Taylor rule with an assumed r-star of 1.5%. The implied fed funds rate path using a Taylor rule and contemporaneous projections for core PCE from the SEP is 4.25%, 3.9%, and 3.5%, for 2027, 2028, and 2029, respectively.1 That profile compares to the 4.1-4.2%, 3.7-3.9% and 3.5- 3.6% ranges implied by the medians and means from the dot plot over this same horizon. As such, this parameterization provides a simple, yet accurate description of the Committee’s reaction function embedded in the median projections. The most prominent source of upside relative to this expectation is a further upward reassessment of r-star. Our own dashboard has placed the real neutral rate in a 1.5 -1.75% range for the past three years, a key factor in our consistent argument over this period that neutral is above the Fed’s projections and private-sector forecasts. If real neutral is 25bps higher than the 1.5% that is currently implicit in the SEP projections – an entirely plausible assumption based on our analysis – the fed funds rate implied by the SEP economic projections and Taylor rule reaction function rises to 4.5% in 2027, 4.1% in 2028, and 3.75% in 2029. 1 In this analysis we make the simplifying assumption that with the unemployment rate projected to be close to NAIRU throughout the forecast horizon (4.1% versus 4.2%) it does not feature materially in the modal assessment of appropriate policy. Figure 2: DB r-star dashboard continues to show avg. r-star 1.5-1.75% Source: FRB, FRBNY, FRB Richmond, FRB Cleveland, FRB San Francisco, Bloomberg Finance LP, Haver Analytics, Deutsche Bank Research However, we have also noted that there could be upside risks to even our above-consensus view on r-star (see “A case for 10s at 5”). Given that current market pricing is centered closer to a 4% nominal neutral rate (2% real) and that model-based estimates of r-star tend to be revised higher over time, we think there is a reasonable case to make that the real neutral rate could be 1.75 -2.25%. If, consistent with this, r-star is close to 2%, the implied nominal fed funds rates from the Taylor rule under the Fed’s median core PCE inflation forecasts would be 4.75%, 4.375%, and 4% for 2027 through 2029. As we reiterated on Wednesday, the signals from the September FOMC meeting reinforced our baseline expectation that the Fed is likely to deliver 50bps more of rate hikes, with 25bp increases in December and next March. This would leave the fed funds rate at 4.375% by the end of Q1 2027. The simple analysis in this piece suggests that, based only on reasonable scenarios for r-star, there is plausibly 40bps of upside risk to that expectation, consistent with the just over 100bps of total tightening this cycle that is currently priced.
