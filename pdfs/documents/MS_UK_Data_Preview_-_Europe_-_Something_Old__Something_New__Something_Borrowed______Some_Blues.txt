We see a 2 index point drop in the headline PMIs. We take stock of the most recent fiscal newsflow, which looks bearish to us. A blast from the past: February inflation, we think, just about rounds down to 2.9%Y, with core also ticking down to 3.0%Y. We see upside risk to our headline inflation forecast as our figures are very close to rounding up, but we are relatively confident that February will bring a solid decline in underlying services inflation. We see this as relevant information on corporate pricing power – or the lack thereof – going into another energy shock. We see RPI inflation at 3.73%Y, broadly in line with market fixing. The print, in a nutshell, should see a drop in services inflation countered by base effects-related rise in core goods; a stronger food print is met with a drop in fuel prices. This drop, we note, reflects pre-Hormuz closure dynamics. What are we watching out for | Non-core: The ONS will implement changes to their food price sampling practices as of this print, which is likely to be a source of some uncertainty. Price surveying will be replaced by scanner data, which is aimed at better capturing the consumer experience. However, with frequent discounting in the food retail sector, there could be monthly volatility. In any case, our bias is to see a firmer monthly print vs. a soft January reading, and we peg food inflation at 0.5%M and 3.9%Y. We see wide two-sided risks around this estimate. We also account for alcohol duty uprating in February (we estimate a 1.1%M change in the Alcohol & Tobacco category). Core | This must be the print: In recent years, January has been a month of discounting in the catering sector. As discounts end in February, large restaurant & pub chains use the normalisation in prices as an opportunity to also pass through their intended annual price hikes. UK's largest pub chain, for example, hikes its prices following its January promotions. On our tracking, their average price hike last year was ~4-5%, whereas this year, we see it as at ~2%. These base effects, in tandem with some reversal of the unusual surge in live music prices in January, help push underlying services inflation to sub-4%Y, on our forecasts (MSe: 3.8%Y). Core goods-wise, last year brought some delayed sales, which we don't see as likely to be repeated. With that, base effects help push core goods inflation modestly higher. 2, 3, 4...? PMIs & retail sales | First read of the Hormuz aftermath: The PMI survey is normally conducted in the middle of the month. Its March iteration, thus, ought to capture the peak commodity market pressures. Our model points to a 1 index point decline in the headline Services and Composite indices. We amplify this drop to 2 index points, for two reasons. First, we see an outsized sentiment hit. Second, we remain focused on how much of the PMI moves is driven by one sector - Software & IT Services - which came in at 64.6 in February. This is not to say that we don't think the UK businesses are investing and automating - they are. However, when one sector has such outsized importance in the aggregate data, headline indices can swing on the back of very concentrated sentiment changes. We will also monitor prices charged indices, where we assume we will see a rise in the manufacturing prices charged, which are a big beta to energy prices. Retail sales-wise, given the very weak BRC print as well as the scale to which the January print was boosted by a sharp rise in "other specialized stores" volumes sold, we pencil in a 2%M drop. What next: As the dust settles on the BoE decision, our focus turns to fiscal policy. PSNB came in higher than we had expected this morning, but cash requirements seem on course to meet the OBR's full-year forecasts, we think. The print thus has little implication for any DMO remit revision come April. Elsewhere, we are focused on any news about the Treasury contemplating "fiscal rule changes" or "suspension" amid the rise in energy bills, as we suspect this could cause adverse gilt market reaction. The government has thus far offered support of only £53 million for rural households that use heating oil. We are surprised that a fuel duty cut has not been discussed more prominently yet, given pump prices appear on course to rise by at least 10% in March, and by 20% in March and April cumulatively. For now, the government has focused on the planned rise in fuel duty in September - highlighting the backloaded nature of this planned increase. From limited and targeted, to 3% of GDP? As we outlined in this note, we think some support with utility bills is also likely should elevated natural gas prices persist, although we view this as more probable in the winter. We are, however, concerned about ratcheting up of fiscal support - as was the case in 2022, when fiscal payments also started in a "limited and targeted" fashion, and ended up totalling at 3% of GDP. We also worry that targeting households on Universal Credit simply means that households on the cusp of receiving such payments might reduce their working hours in order to avoid missing out on such funds. We are also surprised that there has been no conversation thus far on how to buffer the rise in corporate operational costs due to the increase in energy prices. This, after all, is the quickest way to ensure more limited "second-round effects". Same policy mix = same outcomes: In the end, we think the only effect of a potential net fiscal stimulus - i.e. unfunded fiscal payments - is more monetary restrictiveness. If this policy mix were to materialise, the government would be M Idea Morgan Stanley Research 3 boosting inflation, while the BoE would be dampening growth, as was the case between 2022 and 2025. A net neutral fiscal intervention specifically aimed at reducing inflation - temporary direct tax hikes aimed at funding temporary indirect tax cuts and caps in utility bills - would, in turn, ensure lowered measured inflation, would not be adding demand into a global energy supply crunch, and would leave more room for the BoE to act in a growth-supportive manner. This would leave us more constructive on UK fiscal, growth and inflation. However, we don't see any signs of a policy intervention being designed in such a fashion as of now. At this point, we see the downside risks to UK growth and fiscal outlook as at their most severe since early 2023, amplified by a central bank that seems to have guided the market towards a hike as early as April. While our base case is for a hold this year, we can see a path whereby the BoE attempts some front-loaded tightening in financial conditions, only to have to carry out a series of hikes with headline inflation rising in the summer. This is to say, for any comfort around our base case of no near-term hikes, barring the resolution of supply issues related to the Strait of Hormuz, we would want to see a clearer pushback against market pricing by Bailey, Breeden and Ramsden. We are also focused on any commentary by Chancellor Reeves in the coming days and weeks.
