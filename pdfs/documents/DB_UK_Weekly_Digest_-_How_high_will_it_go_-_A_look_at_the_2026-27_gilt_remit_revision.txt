# UK Weekly Digest - How high will it go - A look at the 2026-27 gilt remit revision

Economics UK Weekly Digest Date 16 October 2026

As we inch closer to the 28th of October, investors will be keeping a close eye on another fiscal announcement on Budget Day: the 2026/27 gilt remit revision. By all accounts, cash borrowing will almost certainly be higher. In this Weekly, we set out our expectations for the government's central government net cash requirement (CGNCR) in the current fiscal year. We also examine how the Debt Management Office's gilt remit could change as a result. How is cash borrowing evolving in 2026/27? Five months into the fiscal year, cash borrowing (i.e. CGNCR) is tracking nearly GBP 3bn ahead of the OBR's forecast. Figure 1: Cash borrowing receipts are running a little ahead of expectations Figure 2: CGNCR gap sitting at GBP 3bn following the August public finances report The good news? It's not weaker receipts that are pushing borrowing higher. In fact, outturn data show HMRC cash receipts running nearly GBP 3bn ahead of the OBR's projections, as the economy continues to grow faster than the OBR anticipated for 2026. What's lifting receipts? PAYE receipts alone are running GBP 3.1bn above the OBR's April-August projections. NIC receipts are also tracking slightly higher, coming in GBP 1.5bn above the fiscal watchdog's forecast. Corporation tax cash receipts are also ex ceeding the OBR's projections by around GBP 1.4bn. Together, these gains have more than offset weaker VAT and self-assessment receipts. What's the bad news then? Spending has exceeded projections. While interest costs (on a cash basis) have come in a little below the OBR's forecast, higher departmental spending and recently announced policy measures have likely contributed to the overshoot. Net social benefit payments have also risen, as evidenced by the detailed central government spending data on an accruals basis. What's changing? Four factors impacting CGNCR for 2026/27 To assess where CGNCR could go this fiscal year, we look at four different factors. Underlying spending increase. First, we estimate the seasonally adjusted "persistent" element of the current CGNCR overshoot. This gives us a sense of how much of the current overspend is likely to persist through the remainder of the fiscal year. We do this by stripping out policy measures announced since the Spring Statement that are already in place. On our calculations, around GBP 2bn of the current GBP 3bn CGNCR overshoot reflects underlying trends, driven mainly by higher departmental and investment spending, alongside stronger growth in net social benefit payments. Extrapolating this across the remainder of the fiscal year would imply around GBP 2bn of additional cash borrowing. Policy announcements. Since the Spring Statement, eight key policy measures have been announced, including a temporary fuel duty extension, the HGV VED holiday, an increase in Mileage Allowance rates, food tariff suspensions, the Great British Summer Savings scheme, a Chemicals and Ceramics Fund, and the unfunded portion of the Defence Investment Plan. In addition, we expect several further measures to be announced in the Budget that would come into effect this fiscal year, including a second fuel duty extension, an increase i the Warm Homes Discount, a potential VAT cut on gas bills, and some form of business energy support. We convert accrued and planned spending into cash spending for the current fiscal year to estimate the impact on CGNCR. In total, we estimate that policy support will add GBP 4.4bn to borrowing this fiscal year. Market conditions. There are two channels at play here. Higher interest rates will lead to higher borrowing, even in cash terms. This will come through higher financing costs on T-bills, NS&I liabilities and new gilt issuance relative to what the OBR assumed in the Spring Statement. We estimate this will add around GBP 0.7bn to cash borrowing over the remaining seven months. From a macro perspective, however, higher energy prices should boost VAT receipts and Energy Profits Levy revenues. Stronger inflation also implies faster nominal GDP growth. Based on current market conditions, we estimate these effects will reduce cash bo rrowing by around GBP 1.3bn. Net-net, we estimate market conditions will lower CGNCR by around GBP 0.6bn. QT. The BoE's latest QT decision will have important implications for cash borrowing, particularly this fiscal year. For one, the size of the APF will be larger than the OBR expected, with no sales taking place between October 2026 and March 2027. This increases the carry cost of the APF. We estimate the additional interest cost at around GBP 1.8bn. The offset, however, comes through lower valuation losses. In the Spring Statement, the OBR estimated valuation costs of just under GBP 10bn in 2026/27. Given the OBR's assumption of GBP 32bn of active sales beyond the October 2025-September 2026 QT year, the latest decision implies around GBP 16bn fewer gilts sold in Q4-26 and Q1-27. Applying a blended loss rate (40/40/20 across short, medium and long maturities), we estimate valuation costs will be around GBP 2.4bn lower. Where does this leave CGNCR for 2026/27? Putting this together, we estimate the net effect would increase cash borrowing by around GBP 6.5bn this fiscal year. This would take CGNCR from an unrevised GBP 137.2bn to GBP 143.7bn. Figure 3: How high do we think CGNCR can go? Source: Deutsche Bank, OBR How will the gilt remit change on 28 October? How should investors think about the remit? An upward revision to the gilt remit seems inevitable to us. But gilts need not absorb the entire increase in cash borrowing. The DMO has another funding tool at its disposal: T-bills. With the T-bill consultation now concluded, and the DMO expected to increase T-bill issuance as part of future funding plans, we think a modest upgrade is likely at the Autumn Budget. This would represent a small stepping stone towards a larger contribution from bills within the 2027/28 funding remit. How far can the DMO push T-bills? With GBP 5bn already allocated towards funding the 2026/27 borrowing requirement, we see a plausible range of GBP 1 - 3bn in additional T-bill issuance being announced on 28 October. This would also help offset the slowdown in NS&I inflows seen so far this year (we currently expect net NS&I financing to reach GBP 11 -14bn by the end of the fiscal year, although recent increases in savings rates should support stronger take-up). Figure 4: NS&I savings tracking well below target so far Figure 5: T-bill changes likely to be marginal, we think, for this fiscal year What does this mean for gilts? We assume NS&I financing expectations are revised only marginally lower, towards the top end of our range (GBP 14bn), reflecting higher savings rates. Indeed, HMT has rarely revised its NS&I assumptions during a fiscal year. The only change in the last seven years came in the previous Autumn Budget, when HMT increased its NS&I target by GBP 1bn. We also assume T-bills issuance will increase by GBP 2bn, the midpoint of our estimated range. Where would this leave the 2026/27 gilt remit revision? Based on our assumptions, we expect projected gilt sales to increase by a relatively modest GBP 5.5bn this fiscal year, taking total expected gilt sales for 2026/27 to GBP 251.8bn. Figure 6: Expectations for the gilt remit Source: Deutsche Bank, DMO The Week Ahead: Inflation, Labour Market, Public Finances, Retail Sales, Surveys, BoE Speak It's a packed week for the UK. We will get the latest (September) inflation, public finance and retail sales releases. We will also get the August labour market data to tie into the recent August GDP release. Elsewhere, keep a close eye on surveys: we will get the latest PMI, GfK consumer confidence, and CBI releases. And lastly, we will hear from Governor Bailey who will be taking part in a fireside chat – just as the MPC enters its blackout period. Figure 7: Key events in the week ahead Source: Deutsche Bank Research, Bloomberg Finance LP Inflation: After headline CPI matched expectations in August, coming in at 3.1% y-o-y, we expect another jump higher in September. Indeed, energy prices continue to put upward pressure on the cost of living. And food prices also look to be turning a corner. Where do we see September inflation? We expect inflation to push to its highest rate all year, with headline CPI rising to 3.49% y-o-y, with core CPI pushing to 2.74% y-o-y. Services CPI, we expect, will also rise, picking up pace to 3.71% y-o-y on stronger base effects. We see headline RPI jumping to 3.93% y-o-y. Labour Market: There will be plenty to w atch in the labour market release as usual. On the quantities side of the labour market, we will be watching for a few things. One, the jobless rate. Survey data have looked more positive than not recently. The latest KPMG/REC survey showed permanent job placements stabilise at 50.5 in August. Potential redundancy data also dipped over summer, pointing to further signs of stabilisation. Vacancy data have edged lower but have been largely range-bound. The composite PMI employment index jumped nearly 1pp to 48.9 – marking its highest reading in 23 months. And our DB Employment Tracker also pushed higher (albeit at negative levels). What do our modelled nowcasts tell us? Our modelled nowcasts point to a sideways move in the jobless rate. Cohort data from the LFS also suggest very little movement in the single rate measures. Altogether, we expect the jobless rate to stay put at 4.9% for a fifth consecutive month. Elsewhere, we will be k eeping a close eye on the HMRC payroll data. Our modelled estimates (based on survey data) have been tracking more positively than recent outturns. And we expect large revisions to eventually come through. For now, however, we expect the streak of negative payroll changes to continue with a slightly softer 7.5k fall in payrolled employees. On vacancies, we see no change to the monthly data, with total vacancies inching up to 704k. Our high-frequency trackers have pointed to some stabilisation too on this front, with Indeed Job Postings data even edging higher over August and September. Turning to pay growth, we will be closely watching for revisions. The last couple of reports h ave come with some slight upward revisions to monthly data. We expect AWE Private Regular Pay growth to edge up marginally to 3% (3m/YoY) – up a tenth from July. We also expect AWE Regular Pay to push higher to 3.7% (3m/YoY) – led in part by stronger public sector pay growth. Retail Sales: Retail spending has been far more resilient than we've expected for the last few months. Despite some softening in survey data, retail sales volumes have held up better than forecasted. What to expect for September? Survey indicators have continued to soften. The latest CBI Retail Sales Tracker, for example, slipped to -55 (from –48 in August). Orders placed with suppliers fell at their fastest pace since the survey began in 1983, and online sales dropped at their quickest rate in nearly three years. Footfall also took a step down, falling 2.9% y-o-y after a 1.7% drop in August. GfK's headline confidence index edged up to -13, but the Major Purchase Index slipped heading into the Golden Quarter. Spending data were more mixed. The BRC - KPMG monitor showed total sales picking up to 1.3% y-o-y from 0.7%. Non-food sales were back in positive territory, though only just, at 0.3%. But with shop price inflation at 1.4%, that leaves volumes broadly flat on the year. Barclays' retail spending was steady at 1.1% y-o-y. Where does that leave us? Our nowcast points to a 0.2% m-o-m fall in retail sales volumes (excluding fuel). Continued geopolitical uncertainty combined with budget uncertainty, we think, will also weigh on spending plans. Public Finances: As we move closer to Chancellor Healey's inaugural Budget, we will be keeping a close eye on the September public finances report. The OBR will have likely finalised its own projections by now (with some early estimates included for the September public finances). September tends to be an important month as it incorporates two key pieces of information: first, initial local authority spending and financing outturns for England; and second, 'final' central government spending estimates. Interest costs will remain a bugbear for the UK too, and on our forecasts are estimated to push towards GBP 11bn. Where do we see borrowing? For PSNB, we expect borrowing to rise to GBP 24.8bn. On cash borrowing, we see CGNCR at GBP 25.2bn. Surveys: There will be plenty of surveys to watch in the week ahead. Chief among them will be the flash PMIs for October. We see the headline services PMI slipping to 51.3. On manufacturing, we expect the headline index to dip to 51.7. Elsewhere, we will be keeping a keen eye on the CBI surveys. We see selling prices up to +18. We expect total order books to drop back down to -20. And we see business optimism slipping a touch to -40. One final survey to watch will be the GfK consumer confidence tracker. We see a slight drop off in sentiment over October as Budget uncertainty hangs over consumer confidence and energy prices continue to push higher. W ith the spectre of high inflation weighing on household confidence, we see a slight drop off to -14. BoE Speak: We will hear from MPC Member Greene and Governor Bailey before the MPC enters its blackout period. Bailey has recently pointed to upside risks from a persistent energy shock and second-round effects. In a data-heavy week that includes the September inflation print, we will be watching for his reaction to the data and any comments on the upcoming Budget.
