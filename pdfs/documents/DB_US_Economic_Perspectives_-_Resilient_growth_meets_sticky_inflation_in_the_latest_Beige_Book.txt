August Beige Book: Resilient growth meets sticky inflation We have previously leveraged our proprietary LLM tools to analyze the Fed’s Beige Book, assessing economic sentiment across key macroeconomic variables: growth, the labor market, inflation, and perceived recession probabilities (see From qualitative to quantitative: decoding Beige Book with AI). The derived sentiment scores were able to capture economic dynamics over the past decade. In this piece we continued this exercise and update our analysis for the latest Beige Book from August 2026. As a reminder, the score is based on a scale of 0 to 10, a score of 0 is associated with a lower assessment of conditions, and a score of 10 is associated with higher assessment. A score of 5 is neutral (see Appendix for full prompts). Note that for the last topic on recession, a score of 0 would suggest that the recession is not expected in next 12 months, while a score of 10 would suggest that the recession is nearly certain in next 12 months. Our data spans from Ja nuary 2015 to the latest Beige Book in August 2026. The latest Beige Book continued to reflect a stable labor market, a healthy growth backdrop, and elevated inflationary pressures similar to those observed in July (see Figure 1-4). Nationally, the monthly labor market score dropped slightly from 6 to a neutral level of 5, as fewer districts reported modest or stronger job gains compared to July (3 districts versus 5). In contrast to the July report, which indicated limited evidence of artificial intelligence causing job displacement, the August update noted both positive and negative effects of AI on labor demand. Matthew Luzzetti, Ph.D. Nevertheless, the underlying labor market remained stable, with the quarterly sentiment score rising by 0.5 to 5.5 in the third quarter (Figure 5). Meanwhile, our quarterly inflation sentiment score ticked down slightly by 0.5 to 7 (Figure 6). This marginal decline reflects a narrowing breadth of strong price increases in August, with fewer districts reporting moderate or robust price growth (9 districts versus 11). Despite this moderation, the Beige Book continued to highlight elevated input price pressures, which were particularly pronounced in the manufacturing and construction sectors. These pressures were primarily attributed to higher energy, tra nsportation, and raw material costs, alongside some tariff-related impacts. Notably, businesses reported significant cost pressures arising from healthcare and insurance, with some retail firms noting a limited ability to pass these rising costs onto price-sensitive consumers. Similar to the labor market trends, the quarterly growth sentiment score rose marginally to 5.75, even as the monthly score fell by 0.5 to 5.5. Ten districts reported growth in the slight-to-moderate range, compared to eleven in July (Figure 7). Both the July and A ugust reports highlighted consumer caution and weak agricultural conditions, which stood in contrast to robust manufacturing activity driven by defense orders and data center construction. While the August report described the economic outlook as generally positive, overall sentiment remained mixed due to uncertainties surrounding energy prices, policy, and international conflict. This cautious tone was also reflected in our monthly recession score, which increased to 3 from 1 in July. Our quarterly recessi on score came in at 2 for Q3, consistent with our estimated recession probability that’s near historically normal levels around 10% (see A post mortem on recession probability models). Ultimately, the latest scores for the August Beige Book period continue to signal a resilient growth outlook, a stable labor market, and persistent inflation. These findings align with recent Federal Reserve communications, which indicate that the outlook for a majority of officials has not changed significantly during the intermeeting period. However, the burden of proof is increasingly on upcoming data to provide sufficient confidence in disinflation to forestall an interest rate hike in two weeks. Our baseline expectations remain unchanged: we project two 25-basis-point rate hikes this year —in September and December —which will bring the federal funds rate to 4.1%, followed by an extended hold through 2027.
