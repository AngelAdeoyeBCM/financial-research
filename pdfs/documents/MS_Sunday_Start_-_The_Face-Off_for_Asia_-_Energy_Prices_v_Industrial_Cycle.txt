# Sunday Start - The Face-Off for Asia - Energy Prices v Industrial Cycle

M Global Idea Sunday Start | What's Next in Global Macro

Geopolitical tensions pose significant challenges to Asia through higher energy prices and natural gas constraints that could disrupt production and exports. But if tensions ease and oil prices start to retrace, we think the industrial cycle in Asia will roar back to life. What happens to production in Asia will be a harbinger for the global economy. Asia is the region most exposed to higher oil and gas prices. Its oil and gas trade balance sits at -2.1% of GDP, above -1.5% for the euro area, while the US is not a net importer of oil and gas. With past oil price spikes, analysis has focused on how well economies can weather higher oil prices. This time around, we must also consider potential supply shortages. Policy makers across the region are using a mix of subsidies, tax adjustments, and other forms of price smoothing to cushion the blow to households and corporates. The pass-through from international oil prices to domestic fuel prices is ~35%. Work-from-home, rationing of select products, and policy guidance to encourage fuel saving have also been deployed. Most Asian economies can use coal to replace the electricity-generating capacity lost from supply constraints to natural gas. These mitigating strategies face challenges should energy prices stay higher for longer. If Brent oil prices in aggregate rise and remain around US$120/bbl with natural gas around US$30/mm BTU, Asia's oil and gas burden would rise to 6.5% of GDP – the historical peaks where growth downside intensifies and demand destruction begins (physical cargoes are being traded at much higher prices than what most investors are used to tracking). Our rule of thumb is that each sustained US$10 increase in oil prices reduces Asia's GDP growth by ~20-30bp on a gross basis, or ~10-15bp after mitigating measures and fiscal offsets. The burden is uneven across the region: Thailand, Korea, Taiwan, India, and Japan are more exposed while China is relatively better placed. Fortunately, inflation in most of Asia is relatively benign. That gives central banks some room to maneuver before macro stability risks are stretched to the point where they must hike. If oil prices stay at US$110/bbl for three months but move back to US$80/bbl, we don't expect central banks in Asia to raise rates except the BOJ, for which we forecast a hike before geopolitical tensions began. If oil stays that level for more than three months, we think central banks will hike once inflation breaches their target or exceeds the upper end of the target range. The central banks in Philippines, Indonesia, Korea, India, and Taiwan are more likely to move, in M Global Idea 2 that order. In a more adverse scenario where oil remains above US$120/bbl, i.e., closer to demand destruction levels, we believe the central banks' assessment would shift decisively from upside inflation risks to downside growth risks, especially since that scenario would imply bigger supply disruptions. Looking further out, we see a silver lining. Before tensions began, Asia was operating from a position of cyclical strength. PMIs had risen to a four-and-a-half-year high, industrial production was firming, and export growth and the capex cycle (proxied by capital goods imports) were strong. And beyond cyclical improvement, three structural forces were likely to support the next phase of Asia's industrial upswing: higher AI-related investment, increased defense outlays, and more spending tied to the energy transition. With recent developments, defense spending has likely moved up on the policy agenda, while the focus in energy has shifted from security of supply to strategic resilience. If geopolitical tensions in the Middle East begin to ease or energy prices move back into a more benign range, the drivers to the industrial cycle should re-emerge, and Asia is well placed to benefit. THURSDAY, APRIL 09 US Personal Income and Spending: We expect personal income rose 0.3% m/m in February. Labor compensation slightly stronger, up 0.4% m/m, while rental and asset income are drags. Nominal disposable income is similarly up 0.3%; in January, the BLS incorporated the new tax changes from the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, which resulted in a boost in disposable income. That new tax rate should be maintained throughout the remainder of the year; the m/ m gain in disposable income does not repeat but also does not reverse. With high inflation, real disposable personal income is down 0.2% m/m. Real personal spending rises 0.1% m/m in February, with goods down 0.1% and services up 0.3%. Higher prices weigh on real goods spending, even before the oil shock. The saving rate falls from 4.5% in Jan to 4.3% in Feb.
