Ruben Segura-Cayuela BoE review: Hawkish hold, increasingly nervous Hawkish hold The Bank of England (BoE) kept the Bank Rate unchanged at 3.75% with a 6-3 vote as expected. It highlighted little evidence of material second round effects so far, along with soft labour market conditions and tightening in financial conditions weighing on inflation. But there was a decisive hawkish shift compared to July, as we expected, in light of recent energy price moves. We interpret today’s decision as signalling that the BoE looks closer to moving towards tightening, if the recent rise in energy prices is sustained and barring big downside data surprises. Overall, the BoE is getting increasingly nervous about sitting out this shock entirely. Rising upside inflation and second round effects risks The BoE noted that risks to inflation were titled further to the upside compared with July and there is possibility of a less stark trade-off between growth and inflation. The MPC judged the shock as looking protracted with most members placing more weight on energy prices staying higher for longer in the absence of a lasting de-escalation. Near term energy prices are close to the BoE’s adverse scenario, though less persistent. While the BoE has seen little evidence so far of material second round effects, it noted that risks of material second round effects have increased since July and it was possible that indirect effects from energy on inflation had just been delayed rather than diminished. There was also increasing mention of risks of inflation exceeding thresholds where attentiveness to inflation has typically increased, especially in the run up to 2027 wage setting round (which previous comments from BoE has suggested is close to 4%). More importantly the BoE highlighted that “Given the lags with which second-round effects appeared, it was not appropriate to wait too long for evidence of such effects before responding with policy”. At the same time, the MPC raised its near-term inflation forecasts to 3.75% in 2026 Q4 and slightly above 4% in 2027 Q1 based on energy futures as of 14 Sept. It also noted that activity has been slightly stronger than expected and that there are signs of stabilization in slack, implying the possibility of a less stark trade-off between growth and inflation. Lombardelli, Bailey, Ramsden look closer to a hike The paragraphs of swing voters moved more hawkish and they look closer to a hike. Bailey gave a signal that if the conflict persists and the risk of second-round effects emerging increases, it is likely that policy may have to tighten. He noted that with risks to energy and food more to the upside, second round effects could materialize more strongly, even though they haven’t so far. Lombardelli noted that “the case for a hike is building, the longer the conflict lasts.” Ramsden said “if upside pressures continue to build there could be a case for increasing bank rate”. Breeden noted that “if risks to the outlook for second-round effects crystallise, it becomes increasingly appropriate for Bank Rate to respond.” Dhingra and Taylor seemed less concerned about risks of second round effects growing and seemed further away from hiking relative to the others. But the MPC signalled that market pricing could be excessive At the same time the MPC also implicitly pushed back slightly on the excessive market pricing of close to four hikes. While the minutes acknowledged that market participants' mean Bank Rate expectations have moved up in the near term, it noted that the upward slope in the market curve, to a greater extent, reflected risk premia. Bailey also highlighted that they didn’t discuss the prospect of raising rates four times. Hike as soon as Nov. is looking increasingly likely Our view is that the BoE’s guidance today signals that an insurance hike as soon as November could be warranted, if recent moves in energy prices persist until then and barring big downside data surprises. Our call is for no hikes this year, but recent energy moves, if they persist, could make a hike in November our base case. We are working through our energy price assumptions and keep our call of unchanged rates this year under review. We will fine tune our forecasts and BoE call in the next few days to incorporate our commodities team upside revision to gas forecasts and recent oil moves (which are higher than levels included in our last forecast update and looking increasingly likely to stay there). This could imply a bit less growth, more inflation and BoE increasingly looking less likely to hold out on hiking in this energy shock. But we view market pricing as hawkish But we would view any tightening as insurance/risk management, rather than driven by materially broadening price pressures. Any tightening is unlikely to be the start of a prolonged hiking cycle. So we do view market pricing of close to four hikes as excessive. We don’t think that we are in the adverse scenario of the BoE yet because that scenario assumes much greater persistence in energy prices in future years and materially higher second round effects (peak of 1pp). We don’t expect second round effects to be as material or price rises to be significantly broad based, given the limited evidence so far, a soft labour market backdrop and restrictive rates. Even Huw Pill (who has voted for a hike since April) has noted that the BoE could lift rates without kickstarting a prolonged and aggressive series of increases. So we don’t think rates will reach the peak of 4.75% (unless things materially worsen), which the optimal policy path under the adverse scenario points to and which markets are pricing. QT decision adds more predictability to the QT process The MPC published a multi-year plan for its QT decision. It decided to reduce the stock of UK gilts held for monetary policy purposes to zero through a multi-year plan, such that the remaining stock is unwound at an annual average pace of £46bn by Sept. 2034, through annual sales of £20bn alongside maturing gilts. They have also set a high hurdle to future changes to the unwind path (such as severe gilt market stress or if Bank Rate is insufficient to meet the inflation target). Overall, the BoE has doubled down on making QT operate in the background, rather than an active policy tool. At the same time, pausing of active sales for potentially the next six months and selling bonds directly to the government were taken as a positive signal for the gilt market ahead of the Autumn Budget. Rates: reassuringly hawkish GBP front-end rates ended Thursday around 5bp lower on average across MPC-dated Sonia contracts relative to Wednesday's close, with much of the move driven by the BoE's hawkish hold. November MPC-dated Sonia closed with 21bp of Bank Rate tightening priced in, and a market pricing in almost four Bank Rate hikes by mid-2027 (Exhibit 1). That remains significantly more hawkish than our view that, even if the BoE were to hike in November because energy prices do not subside, it would not embark on a prolonged hiking cycle. The Bank's overhaul of the QT sales process was the big market mover on the day, with 30y Gilts ending the day 12bp lower outright relative to Wednesday’s close, outperforming Sonia by a few bp. The likely absence of “active” Gilt sales for the next six months and a perception that this new approach would be more effective in limiting the impact of QT on Gilt term premia supported the day's price action (Exhibit 2). to take the controls We and the market consensus had been expecting a £50bn QT envelope for the new ‘QT year’ beginning next month, comprising approximately £30bn passive (redemptions) and £20bn active sales. Well, we did get the £20bn of active sales but they are on pause, potentially until next April, because the Bank has overhauled its approach to active sales completely. The pause in active sales – potentially for as long as six months – will possibly align the QT and fiscal years because from now on the plan is to conduct £20bn/year of active sales until 2034 directly to the government, rather than to the market. With the active amount being fixed, the total annual QT amount will vary each year according to the size of maturing holdings, averaging £46bn/year. This is the way RBNZ, one of the few other central banks to pursue active sales, has done them, and is the way we have recommended they be done in the UK since before QT began in either the UK or NZ (see: ‘“Active QT” looms in UK; BoE should hand over keys and let DMO valet park the APF’, 4 February 2022). We mentioned this in passing (as a possibility, not an expectation) in yesterday’s Liquid Insight Liquid Insight: Can the BoE out-hawk the market? 16 September 2026: ‘Time to reexamine the New Zealand solution? If the Bank feels that it must cut the QE stock at a faster pace than passive run-off allows, as noted previously, we think it could sell Gilts directly to the DMO. New Zealand has done QT in this way.’ The advantages of the approach are operational – it’s better to have one arm of the state selling Gilts rather than two, and responsibility for the structure of the state’s debt profile should rest with the Debt Management Office (as its name suggests). And it is potentially very beneficial from the point of view of managing the evolution of the debt/GDP ratio, given the crystalised losses incurred on active sales. For a full discussion of what we see as the benefits of this approach, see: ‘Quantitative Entanglement - minimising the fiscal consequences of QT in the UK’, 8 Oct 2024’. The Bank will not be selling Gilts maturing earlier than 2035 to the DMO. The reason for this, we would assume, is to provide certainty for the total QT envelope while it conducts active QT. Nor will it sell Gilts maturing beyond 2049, which will be retained to maturity. They, the Bank says, will be backing the stock of banknotes, rendering seigniorage more explicit to an extent. We suspect the fact that the very longest Gilts trade at the largest discount to their original purchase cost might also be a factor. The important thing to stress is that the net value of Gilts to be absorbed by the market from active sales will be the same. We will get an extra £20bn Gilt sales from the DMO rather than £20bn from the Bank. (Strictly speaking it would not have been £20bn from the Bank, it would have been perhaps £14bn in sale proceeds plus £6bn paid by the Treasury under the indemnity to cover crystalised losses). So the strong bull-flattening on the news represents the market expectation two things – a long respite before active sales restart and the view that the additional sales of Gilts from the DMO will be of a shorter maturity than they would have been had the Bank continued to conduct them. The “market price” red herring Transactions between the Bank and DMO will be done "at market prices". As they should be, we might say. This makes sense from a presentation point of view, but it is purely cosmetic. It is about being respectful to those that might worry about debt monetisation, fiscal capture, financial repression, etc, (choose your bogeyman). The price the DMO pays the Bank matters for each of those institutions own internal accounts, and to those of us that try to track the APF’s P&L. But it doesn’t matter economically, for the state (treating the Bank and Treasury as part of the public sector), or for the Gilt market If the BoE does £20bn of active QT in original cost terms in a year, the Treasury will pay the Bank £20bn. Some will be BoE Gilt sales proceeds and the balance will be swept up in the quarterly payments under the indemnity. How much of each doesn’t really matter. FX: 4 is not the magic number All things considered, the BoE made the best of an invidious situation given the amount of tightropes into the curve. We had flagged the risks to GBP given that the task was almost impossible. Nonetheless at the time of writing, BoE pricing is removing some of the tightening. Given the hawkish tone, we think 20bps priced in for November seems fair. In our view, whilst 4 hikes is excessive, no hikes is also looking less likely. Crucially, the correlation breakdown between GBP and the 10yr means that lower yields should not be a precursor to a weaker GBP. Direct Gilt sales from the BoE to the DMO has been taken well by the market and takes away some of the pressures from active QT. Net-net, we think the market is trying to establish an equilibrium level of the amount of tightening which as we flagged above is not 4 but equally not zero. In terms of risk management, the BoE has done as good a job as it could in the circumstances. For GBP, global rather than localised risks will continue to dominate for now. EUR/GBP has risen but our bias would be to fade as the ECB finds itself in precisely the same dilemma as the BoE and will have to confront this sooner rather than later. Overlay increasing focus on French budgetary risks, we are comfortable with our directionally lower EUR/GBP
