# Fed Notes -June FOMC preview - Warsh-ing their hands of guidance

Economics Fed Notes Date 12 June 2026 June FOMC preview:

- With the transition of Fed leadership, key features of the June FOMC meeting are more uncertain than normal. While initially changes might be more incremental than discontinuous, Chair Warsh has promised “regime change” at the Fed. His press conference wil l provide his first opportunity to detail his vision for what these changes might look like.
- We expect the Statement to upgrade the assessment of the labor market and drop the easing bias, in line with Warsh’s dislike of forward guidance. Beyond that, we anticipate few changes to the statement, though Warsh might want to undertake a more significant revamp of the document, for example by trimming or removing the forward-looking language in the fourth paragraph.
- The dot plot should show a noticeably hawkish shift, with the 2026 median moving to unchanged rates and a handful of officials likely to show an expectation for rate hikes this year. The long-run dot could continue to edge higher as well. Warsh might not submit dots, a possible indication of the eventual fate of what has become a key tool of quantitative guidance. Beyond the dots, we will be most interested in revisions to 2027 inflation forecasts, which will signal any shift in views about the persistence of inflation pressures -- a critical determinant of rate hikes.
- A change in style is likely to be most evident in Warsh’s press conference. Based on prior comments, Warsh is likely to avoid forward guidance and an overreliance on short-term data trends. With the Committee moving increasingly in a hawkish direction, we expect Warsh to tack towards the center, not argue for near-term rate cuts, and not take rate increases off the table. That said, he could look to counterbalance these signals with a broader narrative that envisions medium-term disinflationary pressures from AI and productivity. It is unclear if this messaging will be sufficient for a market that has anticipated a dovish bias from the new Fed chair. FOMC statement At the last FOMC meeting, the Committee kept its forward guidance intact despite three members dissenting to the language. In doing so, the Committee gave incoming Chair Warsh the opportunity to put his imprimatur on the meeting statement while at the same time signalling to the new Chair the direction the Committee was leaning. In this respect, the range of outcomes for the June meeting statement is larger than usual. Indeed, though it is not our base case, it Matthew Luzzetti, Ph.D. is possible that Warsh scraps the forward guidance language al together. We could also envision Warsh cutting the entire last paragraph, which arguably contains some redundancies. What is more difficult to project is what Warsh may wish to add to the meeting statement (if anything). With the above in mind, we see a couple of practical changes to the meeting statement assuming no major departure from the recent formulation. First, in the opening paragraph, the labor market appraisal is likely to be upgraded to “Job gains have increased, on average, and the unemployment rate has been little changed in recent months”. With nonfarm payroll gains averaging 188k over the last three months – the highest since Q1 2024 – the current description of “Job gains have remained low” is no longer accurate. More important will be changes to the guidance language. While it is possible Warsh could look to scrap the language entirely given his prior criticisms of the Fed’s over-reliance on forward guidance, we expect change to come more incrementally given that a rising chorus of the Committee wishes to signal the potential for monetary tightening amidst ongoing elevated inflation concerns. Hence, we see the Committee revising the guidance line to state “In considering any adjustments to the level of the federal funds rate, the Committee will carefully assess incoming data, the evolving outlook, and the balance of risks.” By removing “extent and timing of additional” the Committee can signal a clear neutral bias instead of leaning toward further easing as the current language suggests. Regarding the final paragraph of the statement, which has been a staple of every meeting statement since September 2020, it is possible Warsh may w ish to trim it or cut it entirely. That said, it is not our base case at this point given that Warsh is likely going to have more pressing concerns – namely, navigating monetary policy amidst a very challenging economic environment that has repeatedly thwarted the Fed from achieving its inflation goal. Summary of Economic Projections (SEP) We expect several revisions to the SEP – the most important being the median policy rate forecast. In the March SEP, seven officials had pencilled in one rate cut for 2026 while seven others expected to keep the policy rate steady this year. In light of recent developments and Fedspeak (see “US Economic Perspectives: DB AI hawk-dove update: More hawks eyes on inflation “and “Fed Watcher: Officials re-assess inflation risks, re-open hike debate “), we expect a general upward shift in the dots with 4-5 officials projecting a hike in 2026, thereby pushing the median up to 3.625%. We would not be surprised to see more officials projecting a hike than a cut in 2026 given that the most dovish forecaster, Stephen Miran, has now left the Board. We see the 25bps increase likely carrying over into 2027 and 2028 medians as well. In addition, we expect the median long-run rate to edge up further to 3.25%. Recall that several officials have mentioned the potential of a higher neutral rate given AI investment and building evidence that the Fed may not be restrictive (see “Overinsured?”). To be sure, it is entirely possible that Chair Warsh do es not submit dots at all for this SEP. It is also possible that Governor Bowman joins him. However, this could also potentially signal an imminent end to the SEP – a step which the majority of the Committee may not feel comfortable with taking at this point. Recall that Chair Powell had initiated a review of communications policy during last year’s policy framework review with a focus on how they could improve the SEP. The Committee was unable to reach a consensus and it was therefore left to Warsh to pick up the debate where Powell left off (should he choose to do so). The rise in the policy rate should be partly motivated by upward revisions to the median headline and core inflation forecasts. For headline PCE, we expect the median estimate to rise by 70bps to 3.4% for 2026 and 10bps (to 2.3%) for 2027, leaving 2028 unchanged at 2.0%. We expect 2026 core PCE to rise by 30bps to 3.0% and 2027 to edge up a tenth to 2.3% -- the latter is most important to watch as a more significant upward revision would be a stronger argument for higher interest rates. Similar to the headline PCE forecasts, policymakers are likely to still signal a return to 2.0% by 2028. Regarding the growth and labor market forecasts, we expect minimal revisions of the mark to market variety. Finally, we expect some shift in the distribution of risks around officials’ forecasts that would reinforce the hawkish developments in the median dots. In particular, risks to the labor market are likely to be more balanced. In short, the SEP should affirm that the Fed is firmly on hold in the near term with the focus being squarely on inflation at this point given a largely stable growth and employment outlook. Overall, we expect Chair Warsh will stick to a few of the key themes he has outlined about the Fed’s communications, including most recently at his nomination hearing in front of the Senate Banking Committee (see Fed Notes: Market’s first Warsh-ach test). Specifically, we expect Warsh to refrain from any form of forward guidance, as he has been consistently critical of that practice in recent years. As we noted, he may opt to not submit “dots” in the SEP, and we would expect him to downplay any signal from the dot plot even if he chooses to provide his views on the appropriate path for policy. So what will we hear from Warsh? We think he will take the opportunity to frame the discussions at the meeting. That could include that the Committee believes policy is well positioned at the moment and refrain from providing guidance about future policy actions. If pressed, we think he is unlikely to either suggest that the Committee is actively contemplating rate hikes or take that action off the table. While we suspect he will reflect a discomfort with inflation – an intensifying sentiment across the Committee best reflected by the recent hawkish pivot by Governor Waller – we also expect Warsh to balance this tone against a broader framing of the economic outlook. Recall that Warsh has criticized forward guidance, data dependence, and the Fed’s models, leaving a narrative-based approach as potentially most relevant. Within this, he could emphasize the importance of productivity growth and AI for providing a strong backdrop for supply-side growth that – over time – could prove to be disinflationary. Warsh is also likely to highlight some aspects of the inflation outlook that are less concerning, including softer measures of trend inflation, moderating wage growth, stable labor market slack, shelter disinflation, and the potential for tariff price pressures to abate over time. That said, we do not anticipate an overtly dovish message from Warsh – e.g., we do not expect him to lean excessively on measures like trimmed mean inflation – as doing so would risk a more significant rise in inflation expectations and bond yields in the belly and longer end of the curve. Notably absent from his nomination hearing was a focus on the labor market. While in previous years that side of the dual mandate ha s provided scope for the Fed to lower rates even in the face of elevated inflation, recent data have provided Fed officials with greater confidence that the labor market is at worst stabilizing. Indeed, recent evidence suggests that job gains have picked up noticeably, running well above common estimates of the breakeven rate. If this persists, upside risks to the labor market could well emerge – something we will be looking for in the risk distribution charts at the back of the SEP. We will be keen to see how much focus Warsh gives to the labor market and his assessment of current and prospective trends. We also hope to learn more about what “regime change” at the Fed might look like. One area Warsh has promised this outcome is with communications. He will likely be asked for his plans on press conference frequency, the dot plot and SEP, and broader commun ications practices by the central bank. There is a lot of uncertainty about how Warsh views these issues, as he has not yet presented concrete plans. Most likely, Warsh could launch a review of these practices with the outcomes to be presented over the coming quarters. However, he could also prefer to move less incrementally and aim for a quicker / more significant overhaul of communications practices. Our baseline is that Warsh will
1. continue to conduct a press conference at every meeting (doing so provides him the first opportunity to frame the meeting’s disc ussion),
2. keep the Summary of Economic Projections in some form, but
3. potentially eliminate the dot plot to be replaced by the central tendency or range of projections to deemphasize the precision and guidance evident in the interest rate projections. If asked about Fed independence, we expect Warsh will repeat some of the lines from his nomination hearing. For example, he could reiterate that he is “...committed to ensuring that the conduct of monetary policy remains strictly independent” and that “Fed i ndependence means everything to me.” On the specific question of his discussions or commitments to the President, Warsh could note again that Trump did not ask for a commitment — either specifically or generally — for lower rates, and if the President woul d have made such a request, Warsh would have declined to make that pledge.
