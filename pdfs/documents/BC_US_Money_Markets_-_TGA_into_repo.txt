# US Money Markets TGA into repo

At the QRA this week, TBAC discussed the feasibility of parking extra cash in the repo market to earn a return. While direct returns might be low, extra cash in repo could smooth funding markets. We have been recommending 1y1yf SOFR/FF wideners and continue to do so.

- At this week’s refunding meeting, Treasury asked TBAC to explore investing excess TGA cash in the repo market. The idea of running such a program has received increased attention as the TGA has risen to ever-higher levels, and as a possible way to allow the Fed to run a smaller balance sheet.
- Treasury’s objective looks to be earning excess return by investing that cash in repo, but the direct returns from running such a program are likely marginal, and dependent on the level of repo rates relative to the interest rate the Fed pays on reserve balances.
- One indirect consequence, however, would be suppressing repo pressures, as the Treasury may end up only deploying that cash when repo moves above IORB, smoothing out funding markets. That would support SOFR/FF wideners, which we have been recommending at the 1y1yf point (current -4.0bp, entry -5.5 bp).
- Smoother funding would indirectly support swap spreads, which helps with Treasury’s issuance costs further out the curve. This is another cost benefit to Treasury.
- As part of the QRA, Treasury also provided guidance on the TGA level over the near term, indicating it expects a cash balance close to $1tn at the end of July. Our prior forecast had the TGA closer to $925bn at that time, implying Treasury will need to issue additional bills in July to reach that higher target. TBAC exploring placing excess cash into repo markets At this week’s quarterly refunding meeting, Treasury asked the Treasury Borrowing Advisory Committee (TBAC) to explore the feasibility of investing excess cash held in the Treasury General Account (TGA) into the Treasury repo market when balances exceed Treasury’s minimum cash requirement. The issue has gained prominence as Treasury’s target TGA balance has moved steadily higher in recent years and as the TGA—now one of the Federal Reserve’s largest liabilities—has increasingly featured in discussions about potential ways to reduce the size of the Fed’s balance sheet. Treasury’s cash management policy requires holding enough cash on hand to meet roughly one week of obligations in the event of a loss of market access. In practice, this means holding sufficient balances to cover all gross maturing debt as well as net fiscal outflows. Over the past year, that policy has at times implied TGA balances in excess of $1tn. Treasury primarily builds the TGA through bill issuance, but because bill auction sizes are adjusted only gradually, issuance must often occur well in advance of anticipated cash needs. This can result in periods when TGA balances temporarily exceed the minimum implied by the cash management policy, leaving Treasury with excess cash. TBAC was therefore asked to explore whether this excess cash could be placed into the repo market, reflecting Treasury’s broader effort to manage the TGA more efficiently given its larger structural size. The growing focus on TGA management also reflects its role on the Federal Reserve’s balance sheet. Alongside currency in circulation and reserve balances, the TGA is one of the Fed’s largest liabilities. As policymakers and market participants continue to debate the appropriate size of the Fed’s balance sheet, improved TGA management is increasingly viewed as a potential avenue through which the Fed could operate with a smaller balance sheet, all else equal. Large and rapid swings in the TGA—particularly around tax payment dates—also introduce volatility into reserve balances, since increases in the TGA drain reserves from the banking system. This complicates reserve management for the Fed under the ample reserves framework. For example, this past April the TGA increased from roughly $700bn to over $1tn in the span of about a week. While no funding pressures emerged as reserves were drained, this dynamic was a concern for the Fed last December and one factor behind it initiating its RMP program at an elevated $40bn per month pace. Against that backdrop, several themes emerged from the TBAC discussion. The first is that the amount of excess TGA cash available to invest is subject to large swings. Some days Treasury might have hundreds of billions of excess cash, other days it might have none. Moving that excess cash in and out of repo means that repo markets would need to absorb potentially large and abrupt swings in Treasury cash flows. For this reason, TBAC noted that such an excess cash repo investment program would likely need to be capped. FIGURE 1. But that return is likely to be modest at best, and more so if the program is capped. Moving cash from the TGA into repo increases reserve balances one-for-one, and those reserves earn interest from the Fed (IORB). From a consolidated balance sheet perspective—Treasury plus the Fed— Treasury would earn the tri-party repo rate (TGCR) while the Fed pays IORB. Over the past several years, TGCR has generally traded below IORB, with only brief periods—most notably in Q4 2025—when repo rates exceeded IORB. Moreover, under the ample reserves framework, the Fed generally aims to keep money market rates close to IORB, implying that any sustained spread between TGCR and IORB is likely to be thin or negative. In other words, the returns would not only be small but only exist in certain repo environments, such as when reserves conditions tighten (for instance as the TGA rises). But this presents an opportunity for Treasury to deploy that excess cash into repo whenever repo rates move over IORB, counteracting the effects of a rising TGA. That would make the Treasury a notable marginal cash investor in potentially large size, likely helping suppress funding market pressures. Smooth funding markets likely benefit Treasury in other ways, such as supporting swap spreads, and in turn reducing the cost of issuance further out the curve. Such a program would support SOFR/FF wideners, which we have been recommending at the 1y1yf point (current -4.0bp, entry -5.5 bp) While not a stated goal in the Treasury discussions, we think another attraction of such a framework is that it could reduce the minimum required TGA balances if structured correctly, potentially allowing the Fed to operate with a somewhat smaller balance sheet. But if Treasury structures the program as only investing excess cash, it is unlikely to reduce the peak TGA levels driven by the cash management policy. For instance, in the QRA Treasury noted the policy indicates that it needs to have $1tn on hand at the end of July to meet its outflow needs, and when the TGA reaches that level their would be no excess to invest. As a result, simply investing “excess” cash above the minimum requirement would do little to support a structurally smaller Fed balance sheet. TBAC discussed an alternative framework in which Treasury would hold only three days of projected outflows in the TGA, with days four and five placed into the repo market. Outflows associated with days four and five can be sizable as well, particularly around coupon settlements, suggesting that this approach would also need to be capped to avoid large and abrupt swings in the amount the Treasury is investing. But importantly, designing the program this way would would give Treasury an available cash balance to deploy even when the required minimum is very high, meaning that Treasury can move into repo as the balance peaks and the drain on reserves is extreme. That would give it meaningful capacity to counteract the effects of a rising TGA on repo markets directly. In short, if the objective is to earn a return on the excess cash, that direct return is likely marginal and dependent on the repo environment. But structuring it this way could could have indirect benefits by supporting swap spreads and lead to lower issuance costs for the Treasury. And while not a stated goal of the program as outlined, it would be effective at reducing peak TGA balances and, in turn, alleviating some need for the Fed to run a larger balance sheet—the extent of which would depend on how the program is structured and the level of any caps. Bill Supply Update As part of this week’s refunding meeting, Treasury also provided guidance on bill issuance over the coming months. In the near term, Treasury noted it is likely to issue a cash management bill (CMB) in late May, which looks intended to manage bill supply around the mid-June corporate tax payment date. We think a roughly $50bn CMB issued at the end of May and maturing shortly after mid-June likely would be sufficient. Relative to our prior baseline, this would modestly increase bill issuance in May while reducing issuance in June, though it does not materially change net bill supply in either month. July, however, is more notable. We had already been penciling in roughly $170bn of net bill issuance to private investors in July. Treasury indicated that its cash management policy implies a TGA balance closer to $1tn at the end of that month, while our prior forecast had the TGA closer to $925bn.
