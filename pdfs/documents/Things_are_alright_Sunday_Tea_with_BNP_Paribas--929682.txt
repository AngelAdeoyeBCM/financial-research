# Things are alright Sunday Tea with BNP Paribas

Sunday Tea with BNPP: Things are alright

## OUR KEY VIEWS

- Despite a mixed payrolls report, the underlying US economy remains very strong. Q2 earnings have been exceptional while nominal Q3 growth could easily top 8%.
- We still expect the Fed to start a hiking cycle this year, although this week’s CPI print may play a key role in determining the timing of liftoff.
- We do not think the recent JPY intervention is sufficient to change the trend in USDJPY. Last Friday’s nonfarm payrolls print had something for everyone. The 3mma of payrolls dropped to +20k, but the unemployment rate has now declined by 0.1% in two straight prints. All else equal, this data suggests a very low breakeven rate, which may be related to the US administration’s recent work-authorization revocations. Soft earnings suggest that we haven’t yet hit an inflection point in labor slack where firms start bidding up to get people. However, if the labor market continues to tighten, this could begin to become a worry. Rather than getting caught up in details that may be revised again later, though, we suggest focusing on the big picture. Things are alright. We have been above-consensus on US economic growth since the start of the year, and the broad tenor of the economic data continues to suggest optimism. Indeed, with Q2 US earnings nearly finished, the overall results portray extraordinary strength in corporate America. Of almost 90% of firms that have reported, 76% beat sales expectations while 89% beat earnings estimates. As things stand, the YoY earnings growth rate for the S&P 500 will sit at +51.5%, the highest pace for the index since Q 2 2021. Given the strength of the results, consensus YoY EPS growth rate expectations for 2026 have risen by ~6.4 ppt since the end of June, from 33.5% to 39.8%. Meanwhile, nominal Q3 growth could easily top 8% annualized. With overall equity positioning still in neutral territory (BNPEPUS: 43/100), we remain constructive on US stocks. With corporate America roaring, the economy growing well above potential, the labor market tightening, inflation well above target, and inflation expectations at risk of unanchoring upwards, we continue to believe that the Fed will begin a tightening cycle this year. Since the start of June, we have penciled in three consecutive hikes in December, January, and March. But given the aforementioned strength of the economy, the risk is that these hikes get pulled forward. Fed Chair Warsh appears to be trying to nudge policymakers to formulate monetary policy according to a forward-looking and thematic forecast that changes relatively infrequently. As such, we think, all else equal, he would prefer to wait, both until we get past the midterms and until we get the result of his task forces. The issue is policymakers are already signaling specific disagreement with moving to a structural - outlook approach to monetary policy, instead signaling a commitment to the Powell-era first - differences framework. In that framework, decisions are guided by “the incoming data” and whether it supports “confidence” that the economy is stabilizing towards maximum employment and 2% inflation. If the incoming data do that, then policy would move towards neutral; if they do not, then it would move away from neutral. This approach to policy formulation is supported by previous research and does not rely on structural analysis of the economy. In an environment where the structural dynamics of the economy are hard to know (like today), a differences-rule approach to policy formulation does a decent job of achieving macroeconomic stabilization and is reasonably likely to avoid very bad outcomes (like runaway inflation or a deep recession). Thus, while Chair Warsh would like to pivot from data-point watching, he appears to be in the minority. Three different policymakers in July – Governors Waller, Cook and Jefferson – linked their near-term policy outlook to the “incoming data” (a phrase that appears in all three speeches). Waller notably clos ed his speech with that phrase. This suggests to us that policy will continue to be made primarily according to short-run realized data surprises, putting heavy emphasis on this week’s inflation number. Taking together the information learned from what we have heard from policymakers in recent weeks, it appears that the pivot point is around 0.25%m/m on core CPI, which, at the time of writing, is exactly the average consensus estimate. Numbers cooler than that may decrease the probability of a September liftoff, while a convincing 0.3% may make next month’s meeting the market’s base case. Calvin Tse Head of Americas Macro Strategy and US Economics The Fed continues to follow a data dependent framework Core CPI to drive September FOMC pricing Either way the die rolls, we continue to believe that long-end US yields are asymmetrically biased higher. Our framework for rate hikes this cycle is informed by the Fed’s late 1990s playbook. In 1998, it cut rates three times to pr event downside risks stemming from the collapse of LTCM. With the economy in good shape, and financial conditions loosening with the euphoria over the internet, the Fed took back all three cuts in 1999. 2026 reminds us a lot of 1999 and 2025 reminds us a lot of 1998. Consequently, when the Fed begins hiking, we think it will do three, likely consecutively. It is also informative how term premiums reacted in the late 1990s. T erm premiums began rising from the second cut in 1998. Even when the Fed hiked rates in 1999 to reverse the insurance cuts, term premiums kept rising through the first three hikes. Put differently, markets didn’t think upside inflation risks had receded just because the Fed began hiking. It wasn’t until the fourth hike that term premiums stabilized, and the fifth hike for them to fall. The July FOMC price action suggests rate hikes may be needed to be priced into the market to stabilize or lower term premiums again. The 1999 experience tells us that it may take more than just liftoff to regain inflation credibility. Combing these facts, we think the market will need to see at least three hikes priced in if we are to see a stabilization of term premiums and re-pricing of credibility in the long-end of the Treasury curve. This creates an asymmetry for a continued rise in yields, in our view. If the data remain strong, as we expect, and the Fed does not move, higher term premiums push long-end yields higher. Conversely, if the Fed does hike, we think rate expectations move up in an environment of stable term premiums, still pushing yields higher. The key risks to the view is that inflation cools rapidly or the economy rolls over – which is not our base case. In the FX space, the key question is whether recent intervention will change the trend in JPY. That is not our view. We see recent intervention as somewhat of a midway point between a unilateral and a multilateral intervention. In previous multilateral interventions, participating central banks issued press statements on the exact intervention dates to telegraph their joint support. This time around, the ECB did not do so, and media reports suggest it was only alerted to the intervention after it happened. Without overt ECB support, we think any Treasury interventions are likely to be limited. The Exchange Stabilization Fund (ESF) that the Treasury would draw from to intervene has a limited amount of EUR. And creative ways to get more EUR – including interve ning in the forward market or swapping SDRs for EUR with the NY Fed – would likely come up against roadblocks. Meanwhile, we remain skeptical that the US Treasury would want to outright intervene in the market by selling USDJPY. The fact that it did not do so in the last round of intervention is telling, in our view. The reason is simple: if domestic officials actively engage in policies to weaken the value of the reserve currency of the world, it could have a difficult-to-model and non-linear impact on the market. Such a move could trigger a re-evaluation of the USD’s reserve status, potentially resulting in large outflows from the US Treasury market and push up back-end US yields significantly: exactly what the Treasury is trying to avoid at present, in our view. Therefore, like prior episodes of JPY interventions, we do not view this US –Japan effort as a sustainable strategy to combat JPY weakness. Rather, we interpret it as an effort to buy time for fundamentals to shift in favor of a stronger JPY. Slowing the weakening trend in the JPY will require a shrinking of the US D-JPY interest rate differential. With that in mind, we recently revised our B ank of Japan policy rate forecasts and now expect a hike in September, followed by another in December. If realized, this should do more to stabilize the exchange rate than intervention. We still see US long-end yields moving higher Recent JPY intervention not a game changer
