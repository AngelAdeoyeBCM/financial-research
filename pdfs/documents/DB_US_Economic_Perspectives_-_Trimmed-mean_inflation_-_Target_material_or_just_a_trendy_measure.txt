# US Economic Perspectives - Trimmed-mean inflation - Target material or just a trendy measure

Economics US Economic Perspectives Date 27 April 2026

- One of the more notable exchanges in Kevin Warsh’s nomination hearing was that his preferred inflation gauges were “trimmed averages”. In this piece, we give background on such alternative gauges and explore if they are in fact superior to more traditional headline and core measures.
- We find that in a low and stable inflation environment, focusing on the trimmed-mean may have some advantages. However, it may systematically ignore inflation rates in the tails of the cross-sectional distribution that could be useful in identifying turning points and transitions to more volatile inflation regimes.
- As an example, trimmed-mean rose slower than headline or core post - covid, taking about six months longer to rise above 3% in 2021. Were the Fed to have solely focused on trimmed-mean then, this potentially could have led to a slower interest rate response. Introduction One of the more notable exchanges in Kevin Warsh’s nomination hearing (see “Market’s first Warsh-ach test”) was that his preferred inflation gauges were “trimmed averages” that “take out all of the tail risks, all of the one-off items” to get at the “generalized change in prices”. In this piece, we look at these alternative measures of inflation and compare them to the more traditional gauges such as headline or core. In particular, we focus on whether trimmed-mean or median inflation measures are indeed superior measures of trend inflation. The rationale for looking at these alternative inflation measures is similar to that for core. Excluding food and energy prices is justified, not because these prices are unimportant – quite the contrary – but because these items have their own idiosyncratic drivers and any large moves up or down are likely to be short-lived or “transitory”. As such, core inflation has often been found to be more informative than headline about trend inflation in certain regimes. Though as we will discuss below, that may not necessarily always be the case.1 Background on trimmed-mean measures Trimmed-mean and median inflation measures make core’s exclusion rationale more product-agnostic. The trimmed-mean measure ranks each granular 1 See for example Matthew Luzzetti, Ph.D. subcomponent of CPI or PCE by their monthly inflation rate and excludes items in the specified top and bottom tails of the monthly distribution – those items with large, potentially idiosyncratic spikes up or down – while the median measure focuses on the item with the monthly price change exactly in the center of the (weighted) distribution, essentially a more extreme trim than the trimmed-mean. Trimmed-mean and median statistics are produced by the Cleveland and Dallas Feds, usually released a couple hours after the BLS / BEA official CPI / PCE statistics. There are some notable differences in the coverage of the trimmed-mean CPI and PCE. For the CPI, the Cleveland Fed produces the trimmed-mean and removes the top and bottom 8% of the basket with the most extreme monthly moves. The Dallas Fed constructs the trimmed-mean PCE by taking out the 24% with the lowest inflation rates and the 31% with the highest inflation rates.2 Figures 1 and 2 show the monthly prints for core CPI and PCE versus their trimmed-mean and median alternatives. Generally, these measures have similar monthly prints, deviating only when there are particularly large increases in certain core components that get stripped out by trimmed-mean and median. For example, the large run up in used car prices in 2021 boosted core CPI and PCE but was omitted by the alternative inflation measures. Similarly, recent core PCE prints have been elevated by large increases in small goods categories like software and computer accessories that trimmed-mean and median are “looking through”. In year-over-year terms (Figures 3 and 4), median measures seems to run higher than core or trimmed-mean for both CPI and PCE, at least post-GFC and pre - covid. For the CPI, trimmed-mean was relatively in line with core, likel y because the trim amount is relatively small and is more likely to capture the volatile food and energy categories relative to other goods and services. In contrast, trimmed-mean PCE cuts more of the distribution. As such, it has been more likely to diverge from core and has tended to run at or slightly above core over this period. Are trimmed-mean measures better? Is there necessarily a clear reason to focus on trimmed-mean measures of inflation when setting monetary policy? In this section, we explore reasons why trimmed-mean may not be strictly preferable to headline or core. Trimmed-mean can lag when inflation regimes change One clear benefit to trimmed-mean is that it is easy to communicate why certain products are being excluded on a month-by-month basis. Inflation is measured imprecisely, so excluding some of the “noise” of large moves in smaller categories (which do not necessarily have to be food or energy categories) can provide a clearer picture of the trend. However, fundamental to this is the premise that the inflation prints out in the tails are in fact noise and thus not informative about the trend. Previous Fed research has found that trimmed-mean is “more useful in low inflation environments, when the underlying signal is weak relative to the noise in the data”, but may not be able to timely capture changes in the inflation regime that information in the tails may help identify. For example, if large increases in gas prices are indicative of future cost-push energy pressures or spikes in used car prices signal extreme supply-demand imbalances that are likely to persist, then t rimmed-mean and median inflation would systematically strip out that signal. As a result, these alternative measures would rise much slower than headline or core would. Indeed, this occurred post-covid, where the year-over-year rate for both headline and core PCE rose above 3.0% in April 2021, but it was not until roughly six-months later that trimmed-mean (November) and median (October) did so. Were the Fed to have focused solely on trimmed-mean measures of inflation, they would have likely responded slower to the inflation data than if they also incorporated some signal from headline and core. As such, strictly relying on these alternative measures of inflation could lead to worse monetary policy outcomes. Recent Dallas Fed research pointed out this potential shortcoming of trimmed - mean inflation noting that sharp increases in the skewness of the inflation distribution have sometimes preceded or coincided with inflation surges, for example in 1974 and 2021. As a result, trimmed-mean lagged core and headline PCE inflation. However, skewness also spiked in 2012, after which inflation remained subdued. Figure 5 shows the rolling root-mean-squared difference over the past three years of the specified measure from a 37-month centered-moving-average of headline inflation. Of the four measures shown, core and median PCE seem to have the closest distance to t his admittedly simple measure of trend inflation. There are some periods in which trimmed-mean also has a similar distance to trend; however, there also clear periods (e.g. 2021 as described above) in which trimmed-mean presents a less accurate portrayal of trend inflation. As Figure 5 indicates and trimmed-mean’s lagging behavior in 2021 exemplifies, while trimmed-mean may be better in certain regimes (likely those with low/stable inflation), it could underestimate (overestimate) inflation during a period when the cross-sectional distribution of inflation is persistently skewed towards the upper (lower) tail. In the current environment where higher tariffs and energy prices have the potential to generate cost-push pressures, trimmed-mean may underperform other metrics. Figure 5: Average distance to a simple measure of trend inflation Forecasting ability Related to the above section, trimmed-mean type inflation measures are often cited as having the ability to improve inflation forecasts. For example, Ball et al (2023) find that across 38 advanced and emerging economies median measures are less volatile than standard core, are more closely related to e conomic slack, and are more closely related to headline inflation. Similarly, Verbrugge and Zaman (2023) find that including trimmed-mean inflation measures can help forecasting inflation over medium- and longer-term horizons. They note, however, that it is not just the “trend information implicit in trimmed-mean estimators” that help to improve forecast accuracy, but by comparing trimmed-mean to headline or core, important information about t he skewness of the cross-sectional inflation distribution can be brought to bear. What is the correct inflation measure? If trimmed-mean inflation measures are not strictly super ior, then what measure should we focus on? Unfortunately, there is not a simple answer. As is the case with all macroeconomic data, extracting the signal from the noise and identifying turning points is not straightforward. Moreover, different inflation measures may be more or less informative about the trend in different regimes. Similar to how we look at data on the labor market or r-star, we prefer a dashboard approach for monitoring trend inflation, including alternative measures discussed in this note, others not mentioned (e.g. the New York Fed’s multivariate core trend measure which uses a dynamic factor model to isolate the common trend across PCE components), as well as several model-based estimates of trend. Following work by Fed staff, we regularly update our dashboard of trend inflation measures (a recent example is this piece or within our monthly inflation chartbook). Currently, almost all of the measures in our dashboard point to trend inflation being closer to 3% than the Fed’s 2% target. The only exception is trimmed-mean PCE (at 2.33% in February). However, past behavior suggests that trimmed-mean PCE significantly lower than core may not persist. While they may better capture trend inflation in low and stable inflation environments, more traditional headline or core measures may better capture turning points and transitions to more volatile regimes. Warsh’s preference for trimmed-mean aside, it was clear from his hearing that he wants to change how the Fed thinks and communicates about inflation. Indeed, he expressed his plan to direct researchers to “use our new understanding and new data sources to see what’s really the inflation rate in the economy.” He alluded to a new data project in mind, a joint public and private sector endeavor including the BLS, to undertake “a survey of a billion prices”. One potential revision to the Fed’s communications could be taking something more akin to our dashboard approach to inflation by looping a broader set of measures into official communications, in cluding trimmed-mean and median, similar to what some other central banks have done (e.g. the Bank of Canada and the Reserve Bank of Australia). Another pote ntial evolution that Warsh’s comments hinted at could be the eventual adoption of a target range around 2% at some point. In his comments, he noted that “what we need to do is focus [on the number to] the left of the decimal point, not to the right of the decimal point.” This would likely be framed as an acknowledgement that inflation is too volatile to micromanage down to the tenth and could be operationalized by presenting a range of different inflation estimates.
