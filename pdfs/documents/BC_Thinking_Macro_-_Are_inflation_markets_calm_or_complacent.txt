# Thinking Macro Are inflation markets calm or complacent

Inflation markets, and by implication broader US fixed income, are priced remarkably close to levels consistent with the Fed’s 2% inflation target despite several potential upside drivers. Bottom-up and top-down frameworks have different implications for whether this is prudent, or not. As we enter 2026, US fixed income markets appear to be notably underpricing upside inflation risks and assuming that wage pressure will not re-emerge. US CPI swap rates sit remarkably close to levels consistent with the Fed’s 2% inflation target, providing the FOMC space to continue cutting rates in early 2026. 1y CPI swaps plunged sharply going into the end of the year; they were down by more than 120bp since the end of August and 40bp just in the past month, and are now at 2.2%. TIPS-implied breakevens tell a similar story. Thinking through what is going on here, it is helpful to start by emphasizing that one of the key themes this year for US fixed income, and by extension broader financial markets, may well be the tension between the diverging outlook for inflation depending on whether one looks at the world through a top-down or bottom-up lens. Many potential inflationary drivers from a top-down lens... From a top-down perspective, which is how most traditional macroeconomists view the world, the combination of projected above-trend growth, an ongoing AI-driven capex boom, stimulative effects from the OBBBA, resilient payroll income, a negative labor supply shock due to the combination of aging demographics and a sharp slowdown in immigration, rising commodity prices (gold, silver, copper set new record highs over the holidays), and the wealth effect from equity markets near all-time highs, all point to elevated inflation pressure in the short to medium term. Add to this the monetary policy tailwinds of multiple rate cuts to end 2025, a return to balance sheet expansion and a more dovish Fed chair to come later this year. The totality is compelling. And yet, despite that backdrop, the US inflation trajectory priced into fixed income markets is remarkably sanguine. Markets are aware of all of the above, so what’s going on? Wither Okun’s law? Thing brings us to one of the other big macro questions for 2026: whether Okun’s law will reassert itself. Recall that Okun’s law (as is frequently the case, these macro “laws” are more “guidelines”) states that when growth is above trend, unemployment should fall. This is intuitive as when an economy is “running hot”, it normally craves workers and the labor market tightens. To give an order of magnitude of how this manifests in the data, were growth to come in around 3%, and trend is, hypothetically, 2%, Okun’s law says that the unemployment rate should decline by 0.5pp (using the traditional rule of thumb). Instead, the US unemployment rate was up 0.5pp in 2025 through November. That gap alone represents more than one and a half million jobs. The 2025 experience shows that with robust productivity growth, solid growth can be sustained even with a loosening labor market. At some level, this is consistent with a positive technological/supply shock (more output, less price pressure) resulting from AI. And, if this is indeed the case, we could see more of the same in 2026, which seems to be what markets are pricing. But the risk is that markets have a recency bias and are naively assuming the same dynamics persist this year. Indeed, a major risk in the coming quarters is that Okun’s law begins to reassert itself – labor markets tighten and wage growth accelerates. It is hard to get a wage-price spiral without wage growth, but once that happens, a lot more inflationary trajectories open up. ...but a bottom-up perspective is more nuanced Given this set up, why is 1y1y inflation priced to near perfection versus the Fed’s 2% target? Here’s where the bottom-up story comes in since, from this lens, index construction matters. Pre-COVID, one way to frame core inflation dynamics in the US was that goods were in slight deflation, shelter ran above target and core services ex-shelter adjusted as needed to move overall core near target. Moving forward, this is no longer obviously correct, which is another “core” (pun intended) theme of 2026. Specifically, shelter (which composes more than 40% of core CPI) cannot necessarily be relied upon to make up for weak goods inflation. CPI rents and OER inflation have already slowed to, or below, the pre-COVID trend, and private metrics imply the potential for additional slowing to come. The Zillow rent index, which grew around 4% y/y from 2012 through 2019, ran closer to 3% from mid-2023 to mid-2025, and has decelerated further in recent months. Measuring the precise timing and passthrough into CPI rents is difficult, but the direction of travel is intuitive. To take the logic one step further, if shelter inflation runs below the pre-COVID trend, this means that “supercore” inflation (a key sub component closely monitored by FOMC officials composed of services which are especially labor market sensitive) likely must sustainably run higher in order to offset. While very possible, to be sure, we sense market skepticism on this dimension given increasing evidence of labor market slack through 2025. Of course, were Okun’s law to reappear in 2026, the market narrative changes, and it is this risk that appears underpriced. Finally, it would be remiss not to flag one non-core inflation category that represents a key upside risk to headline inflation: electricity. AI-related power demand may be massive, growing, and pushing up residential electricity prices, but from a bottom-up perspective, electricity is only 2.5% of the CPI basket. So even if prices rose 10% more than expected (which would be dramatic), that’s worth 25bp for headline CPI. Not nothing, of course, but scale matters.
