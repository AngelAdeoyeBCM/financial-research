# US Economic Perspectives - Cliffs notes - 2026 outlook

Economics US Economic Perspectives Date 5 January 2026

Summary: After a tumultuous year, we see the US economy on more solid footing in 2026. With some headwinds fading (e.g., trade policy uncertainty) and tailwinds strengthening (e.g., tax cut boost to household income), we expect growth to pick up, helping to stabilize the labor market and allow the unemployment rate to decline marginally. Lower tariff rates should help disinflation, which along with a dovish shift in Fed leadership, will lead the Fed to cut rates below 3.5% by year - end. Despite this constructive ba seline outlook, risks abound. Recession probabilities are somewhat elevated, given the tenuous nature of the labor market, and AI presents two-sided risks to the outlook – the potential to weaken employment further while further boosting productivity gains. Policy assumptions: We expect that the average effective tariff rate could fall by several percentage points in 2026. Reasons include: further targeted tariff relief as the Trump administration looks to support affordability and a reasonable probability that the Supreme Court will limit the Trump administration’s ability to raise tariffs through IEEPA powers. While other avenues will be pursued (e.g., temporary usage of Section 122 and / or Sections 232 and 301), they are unlikely to fully replicate the tariffs announced this year. Lower tariff revenues will widen the federal budget deficit, which we now anticipate should rise to ~6.7% as a share of GDP. After surprising to the upside in recent quarters, we see several tailwinds, including: a mechanical rebound from the government shutdown, easy financial conditions supported by Fed rate cuts, fiscal support (incl. larger tax refunds), and dissipation of the trade uncertainty shock. Lower oil prices would add to the tailwinds if sustained. However, points of caution include: weak hiring trends, some signs of fragility for lower income Matthew Luzzetti, Ph.D. households, lackluster capex outside of AI and the potential for layoffs to pick up more persistently, perhaps in part due to AI. Looking further out, our outlook is unchanged with growth near potential in 2027 (2.1% ann/ann and Q4/Q4). Figure 3: DB growth forecast revised higher in 2026 (% q/q annualized) Figure 4: And is generally above alternative forecasts in 2026 (% yoy) Labor market: After cooling gradually in 2025, we expect the labor market will stabilize and show signs of a modest retightening over the course of the year. The unemployment rate should dip to 4.4% by year end. Payroll gains slowed sharply in recent quarters but have shown tentative signs of stabilizing at low levels. This slowdown reflects both weaker demand and immigration-constrained labor supply, which has pushed the breakeven monthly payrolls figure towards ~50k jobs / month. We expect demand and hiring to firms omewhat alongside growth. But, in the near term at least, risks remain that the “curious” equilibrium of low hiring and firing breaks with layoffs picking up in a more sinister way. Figure 5: Breakeven monthly payroll gains likely around 50k Figure 6: DB unemployment rate forecast close to alternatives Source: BLS, BLS, Inflation: Overall, we continue to expect disinflationary forces going forward, with shelter maintaining its downtrend and core goods reversing the bump from tariffs in H2. However, unwinding the downside distortions in the recent inflation data stemming from the government shutdown will keep our core PCE profile mostly flat between 2025 and 2026 at 2.6-2.7%. Risks are balanced. On the downside, if IEEPA powers are struck down and the Trump administration does not pursue other tariff avenues, that could spell more downward pressure from goods prices. Shelter and softer energy prices could also drag broader inflationary pressures lower. On the upside, a tightening labor market could lift wages and keep super core inflation sticky. Further out, we no longer expect inflation to come back all the way to 2%, as a more dovish reaction function from th e Fed keeps inflation expectations somewhat elevated and prohibits the last mile of disinflation – core PCE projected to be 2.3% in 2027 and 2.2% in 2028 (both Q4/Q4). Figure 7: Disinflation progress to return in core goods (in H2), continue in shelter, and remain elusive in super core Figure 8: DB core PCE inflation forecast now somewhat lower than alternatives in near term, above 2% beyond Fed: While we continue to view nominal neutral as ~3.5%, we have nonetheless added one rate cut (tentatively set for September) to our Fed path through 2026. That puts the fed funds rate in the 3.25 -3.5% range at year end, slightly accommodative in our framework. This additional cut is helped by lower inflation trends and is also motivated by the slightly more dovish reaction function that could be adopted by the Committee following a change in leadership, though the extent of the dovish pivot will be constrained by Committee dynamics. Powell’s replacement remains uncertain but should be announced in January. The top candidates remain Kevin Hassett, Kevin Warsh, and Chris Waller, but we continue to see Treasury Secretary Bessent as a strong candidate. The Fed has stopped QT and has reinitiated reserve management purchases (RMPs). These RMPs will be focused on T-bills and frontloaded to rebuild a reserve buffer before expected volatility in reserves emerges around known dates in 2026. Downside risks include a pickup in layoffs, which would undermine consumer spending. Upside risks involve AI replicating the 1990s productivity boom, with plausible estimates of a 0.5 -0.7pp annual boost to growth. Policy risks include a more severe impingement on Fed independence, or the return of market concerns around the fiscal trajectory, which could be triggered by less tariff revenues or a distribution of those revenues to provide targeted fiscal relief.
