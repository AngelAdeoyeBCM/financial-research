# Euro Area—Why Is Services Inflation So Sticky

Services inflation in the Euro area has been stickier than expected, with the year-over-year rate standing at 3.2% in January—nearly unchanged from last May. This remains above the 2-3% range historically consistent with 2% core inflation, given historical core goods inflation of 0-1%. The three most plausible explanations for this are

1. a robust labour market,
2. services prices still catching up to relatively higher goods prices, and
3. wage growth running hotter than usual. These mechanisms are challenging to disentangle, as they operate simultaneously and interact with one another. However, the Euro area offers the unique advantage of cross-country comparisons, enabling better identification of each driver. We therefore use a quarterly panel of the 10 largest economies since 2000 to regress services inflation on these three drivers and other control variables. Our results suggest that all factors—labour market tightness, the relative price of services to goods, and wage growth—have contributed to boosting services inflation in recent years. The recent moderation was largely attributable to diminishing catch-up effects as the services-to-goods price ratio normalised. In contrast, a weak labour market and relatively lower goods prices acted as headwinds during the 2010s. Our model suggests that services inflation remains more elevated than normal due to remaining services price catchup (worth 0.4pp), resilient labour markets (0.5pp) and firm wage growth (0.3pp). Our model points to gradual services disinflation ahead. Further normalisation in the services-to-goods price ratio and moderating wage growth should subtract 0.5pp from services inflation over the coming year, allowing it to fall back within the 2-3% target-consistent range. A robust labour market should prevent an undershoot. In a downside scenario where unemployment rises by 2pp (e.g., due to AI-induced job losses), services inflation would fall close to the bottom of its target-consistent range. In an upside scenario where energy prices spike 70% (e.g., under a Strait of Hormuz closure), services inflation would move sideways, remaining above 3%. Euro Area—Why Is Services Inflation So Sticky? Services inflation in the Euro area has been stickier than expected. The year-over-year rate stood at 3.2% in January, nearly unchanged from last May. This remains above the 2-3% range historically consistent with 2% core inflation, given core goods inflation of 0-1% (Exhibit 1). We see three plausible explanations for why services inflation has stayed high. First, the labour market remains robust. The unemployment rate stands close to estimates of the structural rate, and our broader measure of labour market tightness—which incorporates job openings and other indicators—suggests conditions are even firmer (Exhibit 2, left). Labour market tightness matters for services inflation because the services sector is highly labour-intensive. This is the core mechanism of the Phillips curve: when labour markets tighten, workers bargain for higher wages, and firms pass these costs through to prices. Second, services prices have been catching up to goods prices. The services-to-goods price ratio typically trends upwards—a pattern that is particularly stable when excluding volatile energy and food prices (Exhibit 2, right). But the surge in goods inflation in 2021-22 caused large deviations from this trend, which services prices have since had to recoup. The intuition is that absolute price levels have little economic meaning; what matters is the ratio of prices to one another. There is no reason, for instance, for a toaster to become more expensive than a haircut unless their relative demand or input costs have changed. Compensation per employee—the broadest measure of pay pressures in the economy—stood at 4% year-over-year in 2025Q3, above the 2.5-3% range we estimate to be consistent with 2% inflation. Importantly, wages are a separate driver of services inflation because pay pressures do not only reflect labour market tightness. Workers also bargained for higher wages because elevated goods prices had raised their cost of living. This wage catch-up mechanism is distinct from the Phillips curve channel described above, but similarly led firms in the services sector to pass higher labour costs through to consumer prices. Disentangling these mechanisms empirically is challenging, as they operate simultaneously and interact with one another. However, the Euro area is unique among developed markets in offering the opportunity to study variation in services inflation and its drivers across countries. This approach also helps circumvent two limitations of Euro area data: its relatively short history and measurement inaccuracies. It is one of our favourite empirical strategies in the Euro area, and we have previously used similar frameworks to study the drivers of wage growth and the household saving rate. We therefore employ a quarterly panel of the 10 largest Euro area economies from 2000 to 2025. Our baseline specification regresses quarter-over-quarter services inflation on a lag of itself, a synthetic measure of inflation expectations, our measure of labour market tightness, the detrended services-to-goods price ratio, and year-over-year compensation per employee.1 We lag the price ratio and wage growth by one quarter and include country fixed effects. We find that services inflation accelerates after an increase in inflation expectations, labour market tightness, and wage growth, and after a decrease in the services-to-goods price ratio. We can use our estimates to calculate the historical contribution of each driver to the deviation of area-wide services inflation from its sample average (Exhibit 4). This shows that a weak labour market, low inflation expectations, and the lower relative price of goods weighed on services inflation during the 2010s. However, the post-pandemic reopening and surge in energy prices reversed these dynamics in the early 2020s, with all factors—including wage growth—shifting to boost services inflation. Our estimates indicate that the recent moderation was largely attributable to diminishing catch-up effects with the normalisation of the services-to-goods price ratio. This would bring services inflation back to the 2-3% range that has historically aligned with 2% core inflation. At the same time, we expect the labour market to remain robust (with no increase in the unemployment rate) and prevent services inflation from falling below its target-consistent range. The model-implied path is also close to our baseline forecast and the ECB staff projections (Exhibit 5, right). Finally, we use our model to study two alternative scenarios (Exhibit 6). First, we construct a downside scenario in which the unemployment rate rises by 2pp over the next couple for years (for instance, due to AI-induced job losses), leading to a slowdown in wage growth. We estimate that services inflation would fall to 2% in such a case, close to the bottom of its target-consistent range.
