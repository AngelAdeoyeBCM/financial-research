# US Daily: Kevin Warsh on Interest Rate Policy, Balance Sheet Policy, and Financial Regulation

1 February 2026 | 4:12PM EST President Trump announced that he will nominate former Fed Governor Kevin Warsh to be the next Chair of the Federal Reserve. Warsh will likely be able to win the votes needed for confirmation once the Department of Justice’s investigation into Chair Powell is resolved. Financial markets reacted to the appointment with a small rise in long-term bond yields, a rebound in the US dollar, and a drop in precious metals prices. Warsh called for lowering the fed funds rate last year, in part because he expects both the Trump administration’s deregulatory policies and productivity gains from artificial intelligence to be disinflationary. He has frequently argued that the Fed should pair a reduction in the funds rate with a reduction in the size of its balance sheet. Warsh has been a long-time critic of the Fed’s asset purchase programs and large balance sheet. His views on balance sheet policy have caught investors’ attention because they differ from the views of current policymakers. We would not expect a major reduction in the size of the Fed’s balance sheet if Warsh is confirmed as Chair because there is strong support within the Fed for the current ample reserves framework for implementing monetary policy. But a more limited reduction is possible if regulatory changes reduce banks’ demand for reserves at the Fed. Warsh has also been critical of the Fed’s approach to financial regulation. He argues that it imposes excessive compliance costs on banks and disadvantages small and medium-sized banks, and he has said that regulators should be more open to consolidation by smaller banks. Warsh was previously a Fed governor from 2006-2011 and is currently a partner at Duquesne Family Office, a Visiting Fellow at the Hoover Institution, and a lecturer at the Stanford Graduate School of Business. Warsh will require Senate confirmation to become Chair. Senator Tillis, a member of the Senate Banking Committee, has reiterated that he will oppose the confirmation of any Fed nominee until the Department of Justice’s investigation into Chair Powell is resolved. Opposition from one Republican member of the committee is enough to stall the nomination if no Democrats on the committee support it. If the investigation is resolved, Warsh would likely be able to win the votes needed for confirmation. Financial markets reacted to the appointment with a small rise in long-term bond yields, a rebound in the US dollar, and a drop in precious metals prices. The move in bond yields likely reflected Warsh’s views on the Fed’s balance sheet, discussed below. We discuss Warsh’s recent views on the economic outlook, interest rate policy, the Fed’s balance sheet, and financial regulation below. Economic outlook Warsh has made a few comments on the evolving balance of risks facing the US economy over the last two years. In the fall of 2024, he opposed interest rate cuts because he worried that the FOMC risked declaring victory over inflation prematurely. In 2025, he argued that the Trump administration’s deregulatory policies and potential spending cuts would be disinflationary enough to outweigh any one-time effect of tariffs on prices, which the Fed could look through anyway. More recently, he said that the US economy “could be at the front end of a productivity boom” driven by artificial intelligence (AI). As a result, he said, “We are probably in the early innings of a structural decline in prices. AI is going to make almost everything cost less.” Because he thinks that “AI will be a significant disinflationary force,” he has argued that the Fed should not keep interest rates high solely in response to strong GDP growth. Instead, he said, the FOMC should imitate the approach taken under the leadership of former Chairman Alan Greenspan in the 1990s, when strong GDP growth was powered by strong productivity growth that was “structurally disinflationary.” Interest rate policy Warsh’s less concerned take on the inflation picture might position him on the dovish side of the FOMC’s current policy debate. Warsh has not made many comments recently on the state of the labor market and whether the recent softening provided a strong case for the Fed’s latest risk management cuts. Many of Warsh’s recent comments on interest rate policy have been tied to his views on balance sheet policy, discussed below. He argues that the Fed should lower interest rates while also shrinking its balance sheet so that the two policies have offsetting effects on financial conditions, limiting the impact on inflation. He argues that under the current policy mix, “Wall Street is doing great, but Main Street is not doing as well,” and that cutting interest rates will “support households and small and medium-size businesses.” The Fed’s balance sheet Warsh’s views likely differ the most from those of current Fed officials on the topic of balance sheet policy. He opposed the Fed’s QE2 program and resigned from the Fed after it was announced. Since then, he has been a frequent critic of the Fed’s balance sheet, noting that it remains far larger than it was before the 2008 financial crisis, and he continued to call for shrinking it over the last year. Warsh has offered several arguments for reducing the balance sheet. First, that the Fed has too large of a role in financial markets and should aim to stay out of asset markets outside of crises. Second, that past asset purchase programs caused a “misallocation of capital” by “redirecting capital from the real domestic economy to financial assets” and have “been a material part of the inequality story.” Third, that the larger balance sheet contributes to higher inflation. And fourth, that the Fed’s asset holdings have “subsidized” the government’s financing costs, enabling fiscal policymakers to run larger deficits. Most Fed policymakers and members of the Fed staff would likely take a different view on these points. They see the growth of the balance sheet relative to the size of the economy as a necessary consequence of faster growth in demand for the Fed’s liabilities and the switch to an ample reserves framework for implementing monetary policy. The new framework has broad support within the Federal Reserve, and Chair Powell gave a speech just a few months ago to explain its advantages. As a result, we would not expect a major reduction in the size of the Fed’s balance sheet that would require reverting to the old framework for implementing monetary policy if Warsh is confirmed as Chair. However, steps to reduce regulatory requirements on banks could reduce their demand for reserves at the Fed, which could allow the Fed to maintain a somewhat smaller balance sheet under the current framework. Most such changes, such as changes to the Liquidity Coverage Ratio or the Net Stable Funding Ratio, would require support from the majority of the Board of Governors and in some cases from other federal banking agencies. But changes to the softer and more informal guidance that bank supervisors have sometimes provided about how banks satisfy high-quality liquid assets (HQLA) requirements, with reserves or other assets, could occur without a formal vote. Financial regulation approach to financial regulation. He argued recently that the current approach imposes excessive compliance costs on banks and has “systematically disadvantaged small and medium-sized banks.” In his view, small and medium-sized banks face too much regulatory uncertainty about whether their mergers will be approved, and regulators should instead “say we’re open for business, for consolidation among small and medium-sized banks.” negotiations on bank regulatory and supervisory standards with its global counterparts within the Basel framework. He recently said that “Fed leaders have tried to bind US banks to a complicated, vaunted set of rules in the name of global regulatory convergence,” but argued that “the Basel endgame isn’t America’s endgame.” He called for “a new, reformed American regulatory regime” that would “make the US the best place for the world’s banks to do business.” Late last year, Warsh praised the “good early work” undertaken recently by Vice Chair for Supervision Michelle Bowman in “crafting a new regulatory framework.” & Co.
