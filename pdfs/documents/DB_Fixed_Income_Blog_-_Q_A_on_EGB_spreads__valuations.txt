# Fixed Income Blog - Q&A on EGB spreads' valuations

Rates Fixed Income Blog Date 18 September 2026

Given the recent big moves seen in EGB spreads, we refresh our valuation models and present the results in a Q&A format. EGB spreads continued to underperform vs other risky assets, driven mainly by the weakness in OATs, and through contagion to OLOs as well. Oil and ECB hikes impact BTP spreads more than other countries’ spreads. The move in OATs & OLOs has been predominantly idiosyncratic and can’t be attributed to other risky assets, oil or ECB hikes. The residual of 10y OATs vs our twin-deficit model has jumped from a +5bp to +20bp range over the past 1.5 year to +36bp at the moment. More than the absolute level of this discount level, the speed of the move is what stands out. The 2027 budget negotiations gain even more importance now, given the market nervousness around OATs. Any signs that RN might be willing to allow the budget to pass without the usual volatility could help reverse the tone in OATs. How have EGB spreads performed YTD relative to other risky assets? First, we need to select the “other risky assets”. For this analysis, we focus on VIX, DAX and SX5E equity indices, and iTraxx Main and Crossover credit indices. We run a PCA to isolate the PC1 out of these 5 risky assets. Then we estimate the beta of the average 10yr EGB spread (vs Bund) vs PC1 in 2025 and we treat 2026 as the out-of-sample period. This analysis is based on first differences rather than levels. Finally, we simply calculate the over or under performance of the average 10yr EGB spread vis -à-vis the beta-weighted PC1 evolution this year. The evolution of this cumulative residual is shown in Figure 1. At the moment, EGB spreads are at their cheapest YTD level relative to other risky assets. This cheapening move started in mid-May and has been quite persistent since then. However, it’s worth mentioning that EGB spreads started the year with a strong outperformance relative to other risky assets, fuelled by the OATs’ rally upon the 2026 budget agreement in January and the resilience of EGB spreads during the AI-driven risk-off move in February. Hence, the cheapening move since the beginning of the Iran conflict started from rich EGB spread’s valuations. We believe this cheapening is justified by the higher sensitivity of EGB spreads to the rise in o il and the corresponding pricing of ECB rate hikes. Higher outright yields weigh more on EGB spreads, especially the highly indebted countries, than on equities and credit spreads. Combining that with the relative richness of EGB spreads at the start of the Iran conflict explains the subsequent cheapening. We repeat the same framework as above, by replacing the average 10yr EGB spread with each individual country spread. While BTPs have been the main underperformers for most of 2026, OATs surpassed them during this month’s relentless cheapening. In fact, the bulk of BTP underperformance took place at the beginning of the Iran conflict in March. OATs, on the other hand, have been under a non-stop cheapening move since the summer. OLOs have been tracking OAT underperformance with a lower beta and complete the puzzle of the three main underperforming EGBs YTD. The two main outperformers have been Irish & RAGBs. Since these residuals are out of sample and vs the PC1 of other risky assets, they don’t necessarily add up to zero. Their average is what we presented earlier above. Hence, these residuals capture both the relative performance of each country spread vs other EGB spreads and the relative performance of EGB spreads vs other risky assets in general. Figure 3: Over/under performance of each country spread has each EGB spread performed relatively to other countries? Instead of using PC1 from other risky assets like we did earlier, now we use the average EGB spread as the only explanatory variable. Hence, this is an intrinsic valuation exercise with the sum of residuals adding up to zero, i.e. for every outperformer there has to be an underperformer. The benefit of zero-sum residuals outweighs the cost of the fact that each individual country spread (Y - side variable) is also impacting the average EGB spread (X-side variable). After all, this impact is small due to averaging across 9 different country spreads. It’s no surprise that the chart below looks very similar to the chart above since the average EGB spread is currently only 2bp cheap relative to other risky assets as shown in Figure 1. Nonetheless, the split between the three main underperforming EGB spreads, i.e. OATs, BTPs & OLOs, vs the rest is a bit clearer in Figure 4 due to the zero-sum element of this analysis. It’s also interesting that the BTP residual has been range-bound since March, different from Figure 3 – a sign that the recent cheapening is part of the broader EGB spread weakness and not an idiosyncratic cheapening move like for OATs & OLOs. Figure 4: Over/under performance of each country spread vs avg EGB spread sensitive have EGB spreads been to oil? The sensitivity to oil prices varies significantly across EGB spreads. If we isolate the period since the beginning of the Iran conflict in March and estimate the beta of each EGB spreads to oil prices (using first differences), we obtain the results shown in the chart below. Italy is by far the most sensitive, followed by France, Belgium, Portugal and Spain. In theory, this sensitivity should correlate well with the Energy Imports Dependency (provided by Eurostat). However, this doesn’t appear to be the case, with France being the main outlier, i.e. low energy imports dependency but high beta to oil. Instead, these betas to oil seem to correlate much better with debt/GDP ratios. This implies that the market isn’t pricing the economic impact of higher oil prices to each Eurozone economy but the impact from the resulting higher yields to debt sustainability & fiscal dynamics. This is further reinforced by the fact that the betas across countries to oil are matching the betas to ESTR pricing for Z7, i.e. market is pricing the impact of oil on EGB spreads mainly through the higher ECB rates channel. You can find more details about our twin-deficit framework here. When the regime around OATs changed in mid-2024, we relied on our twin-deficit model in order to come up with a fair value for OATs. The idea back then was that the so-called “privilege exorbitant” that OATs enjoyed until that point should disappear. Since then, we’ve moved a long way though, and OATs are now trading at a significant discount to their fair value implied by the twin-deficit model. This could be attributed to the upcoming election and political uncertainty. Of course, this could also imply that the market expects a further deterioration in France’s twin deficit. This discount hovered between +5bp to +20bp from late 2024 until July 2026 and we considered that a “fair” level given the uncertainty. Over the past month though, it has seen a very fast repricing higher, exceeding the jump seen in mid- 2024 following the parliamentary election result. This discount is now estimated at 36bp, the highest it has ever been for OATs. Attaching a fair value to political uncertainty, i.e. how much of a discount is fair for OATs, can’t be modelled, especially in the absence of precise political manifestos of the various candidates or a clear view about second-round run-off. In the absence of “new” information, especially on the political front, the speed and magnitude of this repricing looks overdone, possibly affected by the broader repricing and big moves seen in rates markets rather than having to do with a French catalyst.
