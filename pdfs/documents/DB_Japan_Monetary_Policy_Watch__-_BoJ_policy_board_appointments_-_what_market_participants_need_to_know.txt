# Japan Monetary Policy Watch - BoJ policy board appointments - what market participants need to know

17 February 2026 Deutsche Bank Group Research Japan Japan Economics Japan Monetary Policy Watch Date

Introduction: why these BoJ appointments matter On February 25, the nomination for a successor to BoJ policy board member Asahi Noguchi, whose term expires on March 31, is expected to be presented to the Diet. It has also been suggested that a successor for Member Junko Nakagawa, whose term ends on June 29, may be nominated simultaneously. These appointments are more significant than usual for market participants from two main perspectives. First, they serve as a key test of the Takaichi administration's economic policy stance. Before taking office, Prime Minister Takaichi was perceived as a "reflationist" who favored aggressive fiscal policy and continued monetary easing. Since becoming prime minister, however, she appears to have shifted toward a more pragmatic policy approach that emphasizes dialogue with the market. As the first BoJ board appointments under her administration, this process will be a critical piece of evidence for the market to judge the government's true stance on economic policy, particularly monetary policy. Second, the appointments are an important indicator for the future dynamics of Diet operations. The ruling coalition currently holds a supermajority of over two-thirds in the House of Representatives (Lower House), allowing its policy agenda to be strongly reflected in legislation and the budget. However, appointments requiring Diet consent, including those for the BoJ policy board, need approval from both houses. Since the ruling coalition lacks a majority in the Upper House, these appointments offer a rare opportunity for the opposition to exert its influence on government policy. The opposition's response—whether it respects the public mandate given to the large ruling party and cooperates, or adopts a more confrontational posture—will significantly shape the political dynamics ahead. Lessons from past rejections While we believe the likelihood of the BoJ policy board nomination being rejected by the Upper House—where the ruling party is in the minority—is low, the possibility cannot be entirely ruled out. Past cases where the Diet rejected BoJ nominations provide valuable insights for analyzing the current situation. The 2008 governor and deputy governor appointments: a divided Diet leads to a leadership vacuum In 2008, the appointments of successors to Governor Toshihiko Fukui and Deputy Governors Toshiro Muto and Kazumasa Iwata, whose terms were expiring, faced Kentaro Koyama, Ph.D. Deutsche Securities Inc. The Diet at the time was in a "divided" state, where the ruling LDP-Komeito coalition held a majority in the Lower House but lacked a majority in the Upper House. The government initially nominated Toshiro Muto, then a BoJ Deputy Governor and a former Ministry of Finance (MOF) Vice Minister, for the governorship. However, the DPJ strongly opposed this, citing a violation of "the principle of separating fiscal and monetary policy." The DPJ clarified its stance against a former MOF official becoming the BoJ governor, viewing it as a threat to the central bank's independence. As a result, Muto's nomination was rejected by the Upper House. A subsequent nominee, Koji Tanami (a former MOF Vice Minister and then head of the Japan Bank for International Cooperation), was also rejected for similar reasons. Finally, the government proposed promoting Masaaki Shirakawa, who was then a deputy governor, to the position of governor, which eventually secured the DPJ's consent. Kiyohiko Nishimura, an academic economist, and Hirohide Yamaguchi, a former BoJ official, were appointed as deputy governors. This series of disruptions led to an unprecedented situation where the BoJ governorship was vacant for about three weeks after Governor Fukui's term ended. This is the direct cause of the staggered term-end dates for the governor and deputy governors that persist to this day. The 2012 policy board member appointment: a conflict over the monetary easing stance In April 2012, the DPJ administration under Prime Minister Yoshihiko Noda nominated the private-sector economist Ryutaro Kono to succeed policy board member Seiji Nakamura, whose term was expiring. However, the nomination was rejected by the Upper House, where the ruling party lacked a majority at the time. The main reason for the opposition from the LDP, then an opposition party, was that Kono was perceived as being cautious about aggressive monetary easing. At that time, escaping deflation was recognized as an urgent task for the Japanese economy, and the LDP was demanding more drastic monetary easing from the BoJ. Kono's appointment was judged to be inconsistent with this policy direction. As a result, the successor was not decided, leading to a temporary vacancy on the policy board before other private-sector economists were eventually approved by the Diet to fill the roles. Transparency in appointments and Diet management: the evolution of the "prior reporting rule" In the past, there was an informal Diet agreement known as the "prior reporting rule" that governed BoJ appointments.

- Establishment of the rule and its background: In 2007, against the backdrop of a divided Diet, an informal agreement was established under the leadership of Takeo Nishioka of the DPJ, then chairman of the Upper House Committee on Rules and Administration. This rule, known as the "prior reporting rule," stipulated that "if a personnel appointment is reported in the media before its official presentation by the government, the nomination will, in principle, not be accepted." This was born from strong concerns that the government could undermine Diet deliberations by leaking nominations to the media and treating them as a fait accompli.
- The rule's negative effects and ironic outcome: While meaningful in its Deutsche Securities Inc. Page 3 respect for Diet deliberations, this rule made prior, behind-the-scenes coordination between the ruling and opposition parties extremely difficult. It is pointed out as one of the factors that complicated the BoJ governor appointment process in 2008, as parties were overly conscious of the rule. Ironically, the rule also backfired on the DPJ itself when it became the ruling party in 2012. For the successor to board member Hidetoshi Kamezaki, whose term expired in April of that year, the nominee's name was reported in advance by some media outlets. The DPJ administration, adhering to the rule, was forced to forgo presenting the nomination to the Diet, leading to a temporary vacancy on the board.
- Abolition of the rule and current practice: Due to these negative consequences, the prior reporting rule was officially abolished in February 2013 with the agreement of both ruling and opposition parties, as it was seen to significantly hinder the appointment coordination process. Under the current practice, a nomination remains valid even if it is reported in advance. Instead, the government is required to ensure strict information management and to investigate and report any leaks to the Diet's steering committee1. For this reason, it is now rare for appointments to be reported before their official presentation to the Diet, and we expect to wait for the official announcement on February 25 for the current appointments as well. Appointment trends The six members of the BoJ's policy board (excluding the governor and deputy governors) tend to be categorized by their professional backgrounds into three groups: business/industry, finance (with one member typically from a megabank), and academia. Furthermore, to ensure diversity in policy decisions, it is customary for at least one woman to be on the board. The current composition of the board is: three members from the financial industry (Junko Nakagawa, Hajime Takata, and Naoki Tamura), two from academia (Asahi Noguchi and Junko Koeda), and one from business/industry (Kazuyuki Masu) (Figure 1). The two female members are Ms. Nakagawa and Ms. Koeda. In last year's appointments, the succession of Kazuyuki Masu, from an industry background, to replace Toyoaki Nakamura, also from industry, was seen as a straightforward choice. On the other hand, the appointment of Junko Koeda from academia to succeed Seiji Adachi from the financial industry was considered a double surprise, as it involved a change in both professional category and gender (from male to female). Implications of the appointments Based on the above, the following points can be inferred about the upcoming appointments.
- The hurdle for Diet approval is not high: With the abolition of the "prior reporting rule," it is likely that a certain degree of coordination between the ruling and opposition parties has already taken place by the time the nomination is presented. As long as a candidate is not an extreme "hawk" (favoring monetary tightening) or "dove" (favoring monetary easing) and possesses a high level of expertise and practical experience in monetary policy, their approval is highly probable. The smooth confirmation of members Koeda and Masu in 2025, even with the ruling party in a minority position, supports this view.
- Differences from past rejection cases: When nominations were rejected in 2008 and 2012, the ruling party had suffered defeats in recent national elections and its political foundation was shaky. This time, the ruling party won a landslide victory in the last election, and its power base is relatively stable. Therefore, it is natural to think that the political hurdle for the opposition in the Upper House to reject a nomination is higher than it was in the past.
- Profile of potential successors: Following convention, the successor to Member Noguchi, who comes from academia, is likely to be chosen from a similar background, such as a university professor. Meanwhile, the successor to Member Nakagawa would typically be expected to be a woman from the financial industry. However, with Ms. Koeda already on the board, the "female slot" is considered filled. Furthermore, with three members currently from the financial industry, it is quite possible that, for the sake of balance, a candidate could be chosen from outside the financial sector (for example, from industry or academia). If the nomination for Ms. Nakagawa's successor is presented at the same time, this choice has the potential to be the bigger surprise. Furthermore, considering that the opposition parties currently tend to favor more aggressive fiscal and monetary policies, it is relatively more likely that the nominee will be a "dove" rather than a "hawk."
