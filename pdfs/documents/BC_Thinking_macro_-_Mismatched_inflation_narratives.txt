# Thinking Macro Mismatched inflation narratives

This report builds on the analysis from our recent note "Energy in the age of fragmentation, " in which we discuss whether an expected secular mismatch between energy supply and demand could generate sustained upside risk to inflation pressure, offsetting medium-term disinflation risks from AI. We recently published The global energy race: Energy in the age of fragmentation, a comprehensive cross-asset report examining the outlook for the global energy sector. This report examines its implications for inflation. The inflation dynamic The mismatch between energy supply and demand creates a dynamic that directionally translates into upward pressure on three core factors for inflation risk: baseline expectations, price volatility, and asymmetric risks. As noted in the report referenced above, energy demand is rising faster than supply at the same time as geopolitical fragmentation is increasing the premium on energy security, creating a complex terrain for global investors to navigate. Key question: does the upside inflation risk from the energy supply/demand imbalance overwhelm other disinflationary forces? This creates a tension in the market narrative. For several months/quarters now, the inflation discourse has gravitated toward the idea that AI is near-term inflationary but medium-term disinflationary (for instance, see A Claudy inflation outlook for our thinking on the topic back in March, and Inflation has a memory problem, where we explore one facet of AI-related inflation). But what if this is wrong, or at a minimum, overly simplistic? While we still strongly sympathize with that framework on a basic level, a more nuanced take would need to acknowledge that the path to any eventual productivity dividend may itself be energy and commodity intensive. The focus of this note is therefore on a growing upside risk that may be underappreciated in inflation pricing: energy. Indeed, one theme from the cross-asset report is that the energy supply/demand imbalance should support commodity prices and implies that inflationary pressure may originate from prices of raw materials, rather than from labor market-related wage increases. This, in turn, suggests that an over-reliance on Phillips-curve-style logic could lead to an understating of upside inflation risks. Source: Barclays Research Baseline expectations Turning to the different channels by which aggregate inflation could be pushed higher by the secular growth in energy and electricity demand, the natural first path is via modal/baseline expectations. In essence, excess energy demand should translate into sustained upward pressure on energy commodities (for more details on this, please see the Commodities in Focus section of the recent cross-asset report). This is an essential factor for assessing inflation risk, as many economists/investors rely on commodity futures curves to project market-implied forward raw commodity prices and, by extension, inflation. If our thesis is correct, and the supply/demand imbalance leads to sustained upward pressure on prices of raw materials, this should translate into higher commodities futures prices and, thus, higher baseline inflation forecasts. In addition, it is not only the level of the curve, but also the shape that informs inflation risk. For instance, if the entire commodity curve shifts higher, but the curve does not steepen, then in essence the price action implies a one-off price-level shock, rather than a sustained inflation process. With many major commodity curves in backwardation, the first-order implication is actually forward energy deflation, though our sense is that the signal may be complicated by a combination of futures markets underpricing upside commodity price risk, hedging needs, and risk premia. That is, there is a very real risk that the futures curves are underpricing upside risk. More broadly, a persistent energy deficit would have implications beyond the direct contribution of energy prices to headline inflation. One of the defining features of the post-GFC period was abundant and relatively inexpensive energy, which encouraged investors and policymakers to treat energy shocks as largely transitory. An AI-driven increase in energy demand could challenge that assumption. If markets begin to view energy scarcity as a multi-year structural phenomenon, rather than a cyclical fluctuation, the result may be a higher inflation risk premium embedded in nominal yields, inflation swaps, and breakevens. There is also a convexity dynamic to watch in the inflation indices via the weight in the basket. While it is a bit wonky, we can use the US inflation indices as an example; higher energy prices tend to correlate with a higher weight for energy in the CPI and PCE baskets (Figure 2). If energy prices are rising, not only could there be upward pressure on the energy subcomponents themselves, but the importance of energy for the overall basket would be increasing, leading to a more significant upside impulse, all else equal. This is especially true for CPI and, thus, inflation market pricing in TIPS breakevens and CPI swaps, but the narrative is equally relevant for other markets (UK RPI, EUR HICPx, etc). FIGURE 2. Intuitively, this makes sense and would likely correlate more with upside risks, further underscoring the inherent asymmetry. Higher expected energy volatility should naturally translate into increased inflation uncertainty and upward pressure on inflation risk premium/breakevens. One way that we can already see this happening is by looking at the CBOE's OVX index (ie, something akin to the VIX for crude oil, Figure 3). The year-to-date average in 2026 is near the highest on record, with the only comparable year being the peak of the COVID pandemic in 2020. FIGURE 3. Crude Oil ETF Volatility Index Annual average. Source: CBOE, Macrobond, Barclays Research Asymmetric risks Finally, more elevated volatility by itself does not necessarily introduce upside risk if price shocks are evenly balanced to the upside and downside. However, if risks are asymmetric and skewed to the upside, that would be the case, and indeed that is the world in which we generally see ourselves. A key focus here is geopolitical fault lines. Two of the biggest price shocks of the past decade have corresponded with a major geopolitical development (Russia invading Ukraine in 2022, the US/Iran war in 2026), and in a more fractured geopolitical environment, these kinds of events may become more frequent. Since they are more correlated with upside risks than downside, this asymmetry should naturally raise perceived inflation risk and, thus, market pricing. Of course, different geographies face different geopolitical risks/responses to higher energy prices, but we think the high-level underlying logic generally valid. For investors, the relevant point is that a structurally tighter energy system leaves less spare capacity to absorb the next shock. Put together, we see the above dynamics as supporting the argument that inflation risk premium may be underpriced. To be sure, some risks remain to the downside (AI-related disinflation risks remain real via other channels, and the ever-possible world of a broader economic slowdown), and there are meaningful additional upside risks (fiscal profligacy, de-globalization, etc.), but no discussion is complete without considering what may prove to be a secular mismatch between energy supply and demand.
