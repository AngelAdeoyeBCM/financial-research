# CPI of the storm Sunday Tea with BNP Paribas

Sunday Tea with BNPP: CPI of the storm

## OUR KEY VIEWS

- August’s CPI/PPI reports will drive the marke t’s expectations of September’s FOMC decision. While we still see high uncertainty, w e bring forward our existing December hike to September on account of a range of factors, including cyclical strength evident in payrolls.
- We have revised our ECB call and now see rate hikes in September and December due to the persistence of the energy shock and the resilience of the eurozone economy.
- Our base case is that the GPIF will not shift the midpoint of its strategic asset allocation target in favor of domestic assets, but a gradual move towards the upper bound of the permissible range is plausible. This may be meaningful for JGB markets, but not so much for FX. A confluence of bearish headwinds, including but not limited to renewed skepticism regarding Fed credibility, rising energy prices, and stronger-than-expected economies, has triggered a sharp repricing across global bond markets throughout the summer. Yields have adjusted higher – though notably from unjustifiably rich levels at the end of June. We maintain that the medium-term path of least resistance remains higher for yields; nevertheless, given the magnitude of the recent adjustment, we would not recommend establishing fresh short exposure at current levels. While the focus remains on structural considerations, a pickup in the macro calendar over the next couple of weeks should also see a renewed emphasis on event risks. In FX, for instance, implied vol curves indicate that upcoming events (ECB, CPI, FOMC) command a high event weight relative to other days, though this is off a low vol base. We think that breakevens still appear too low in some pairs compared to average realized performance in recent weeks, suggesting that a long gamma bias may be attractive. Payrolls on Friday were unambiguously strong, with solid job creation across sectors and a steady unemployment rate. There are signs of cyclical strength, underscoring our longstanding view that the unemployment rate should remain on a downtrend. We look for a 4.0% unemployment rate by year end, below both consensus and FOMC estimates. The combination of bond market instability, persistent inflation, and most recently cyclical strength evident in payrolls leaves us thinking that the FOMC likely starts in September rather than December (revised path: September, December, January). However, there is much uncertainty, and a weak CPI print could derail a September hike. The debate on inflation momentum appears unique to the US and a divergence against global peers who of late have sounded increasingly concerned on upside risks to the price level. The Bank of Canada last week, for example, ‘outhawked ’ market expectations by focus ing on inflationary pressures from rising energy prices and an escalating tariff war. This week, we anticipate that the E uropean Central Bank will again deliver a well-priced hike, with the Governing Council’s risk distribution also turning more hawkish. We have shifted our ECB call to now have another hike in December, for a total of 75bp of hikes since the US–Iran conflict began. A large part of the ECB’s reaction function is driven by the energy shock. Brent crude and TTF recently broke through USD95/bbl and EUR72/MWh, respectively, with the latter at its highest level since late 2022. Uncertainty is high, but there appears little sign of an imminent resolution. And with little improvement in fundamentals (due to refinery outages in the Middle East, peak demand from harvesting season, and Ukrainian drone strikes on Russian refineries), ICE gasoil cracks have risen considerably. This disconnect between oil prices and broader energy markets (such as gas and oil derived products) implies that the ECB’s often-cited ‘synthetic energy price index’ likely understates the extent of energy price pressures. We reconstructed a similar index but using product prices and find that this it is at the highest level since the conflict began. And despite the duration of the conflict, the eurozone economy has been much more resilient than expected, actually showing signs of momentum. This assuages concerns of a downturn, and the demand backdrop is one that is conducive to second-round effects on inflation building. Sources: ECB, Macrobond, BNP Paribas Sources: Bloomberg, Macrobond, BNP Paribas Upside concerns on inflation are also apparent in Japan. Along with energy price driven pressures impacting global prices, AI and wage pressures from a tight labor market should put inflation above 3% this year and accelerating through mid -2027 (excluding the impacts of tax cuts and subsidies). The B ank of Japan already saw a shift in monetary policy in July, emphasizing that the risk has transitioned now towards preventing underlying CPI from rising too high above target. As a result, the BoJ’s September meeting has been well priced for a hike, and hawkish commentary from the BoJ’s Takata last week has seen the market shift to reflect a quarterly pace of hikes continuing through to March, in line with our base case. This helps to ease concerns that the BoJ is behind the curve, but on its own does not suggest that there should be a durable move lower in USDJPY given that relative rate differentials remain wide. So what explains the drop in USDJPY last week, the third largest one day drawdown this year? The market seems to be increasing speculation around the GPIF increasing its domestic asset allocation after an unusually scheduled board meeting on 21 August. The JPY has been structurally weakening over the last decade, in part driven by foreign investment, and thus headlines around a possible shift raise the question if the tide may be turning. Should this occur, we estimate a flow of USD70bn, which is meaningful for the JGB market but not so much for the currency market. We still see USDJPY remaining supported but acknowledge that recent price action has gone against our existing bearish JPY trades and have accordingly moved to the sidelines on long AUDJPY after taking profit for a small gain. While we are cautious on global duration, there are some secular positive stories in EM where we remain bullish and in Europe the standout example is Hungary. Last week, Bloomberg headlines suggested that the National Bank of Hungary is working to lower its inflation target, which will continue to help flatten the curve over time and reduce the term premium in the back end. Hence, we like to keep our 5y receivers, and 10y HGB longs against bunds. Finally, in Latam politics will be in the economic driving seat. The focus is on Brazil, where we are entering the final stretch before the first-round of the presidential election (4 Oct). We expect the BRL to remain attractive following the election outcome given its attractive carry and would thus prefer to express views via FX. There is a positive asymmetry for local rates as well, albeit more nuanced than FX. A Lula victory would likely constrain the BCB’s ability to ease due to fiscal concerns, leading to a cautious, data-dependent easing cycle. Conversely, a Bolsonaro victory could trigger a significant rally. Because the potential upside from a Bolsonaro w in (-200/300bp) outweighs the potential downside from a Lula win (+100bp, likely retracing over time), spikes in DI rates may present attractive entry points for receiver positions.
