# US Economic Perspectives - Is the Fed R-star restrictive

Economics US Economic Perspectives Date 9 April 2026

(*)estrictive? Updating DB’s r-star dashboard At the March FOMC meeting, the median SEP projection for the longer-run federal funds rate edged higher by 10bps to 3.1%. With the upgrade, the longer-run dot rose to its highest level since 2016. Consistent with the overall dot plot, which showed a range of 2.6 -3.9% for the longer-run nominal fed funds rate, Chair Powell described the current monetary policy stance as “in that range where it’s somewhere around the borderline between restrictive and not.” He has variously described policy as in the upper end of the range of neutral or as mildly / modestly / moderately restrictive. How does this assessment compare to alternative estimates of neutral? We update our r-star dashboard to make this assessment. Our dashboard -- which is a suite of thirteen indicators for the real neutral fed funds rate (r-star) based on macro/Fed models (six), term structure models (three), and market pricing (four) – shows that r-star has edged higher over the past year. The latest average reading across the estimates for Q4 2025 is 1.6%. Unsurprisingly, the range of estimates is wide, from 0.9% on the low end, represented by HLW, to 2.8% on the high end, represented by 5y5y TIPS adjusted for a typical spread between CPI and PCE. Based on this dashboard, the current nominal fed funds rate of 3.6% would be exactly in line with the average neutral estimate if inflation were at 2% -- i.e. average r-star of 1.6% plus 2% inflation is equivalent to the current nominal fed funds rate of 3.6%. However, with inflation still well above 2% and projected to remain above target for some time, the current fed funds rate is likely below the neutral setting implied by the average r-star estimate in our dashboard. In this sense, while it may be reasonable to describe policy as in the vicinity of neutral, it may be more accurate to classify policy as somewhat accommodative rather than modestly or moderately restrictive. This alternative assessment of the policy stance would be consistent with the desire of “some” Fed officials to adopt a more two-sided description of the policy outlook, one that included the potential to raise rates if inflation stayed elevated for longer. Yes, both economic and financial conditions are behaving like monetary policy is not noticeably restrictive. At present, core PCE inflation is 1 percentage point above target, and has demonstrated no evidence of moving closer to target over the past 18 months. Clearly some of this is due to one-time price increases from tariffs, but inflation is likely running above target even excluding these effects (e.g., Powell attributes 0.5-75pp of the current overshoot to tariffs). On the other side of the dual mandate, the unemployment rate is currently very close to the Fed’s assessment of NAIRU (4.3% versus 4.2%) and has been mostly stable since last summer. Given the inherent uncertainty in estimating NAIRU, it is reasonable to describe the labor market as close to target. Together, above-target inflation and at-target labor market conditions would be broadly consistent with policy not being restrictive. Accommodative financial conditions are also consistent with the conclusion that r-star is likely higher than what is embedded in the Fed’s dot plot. Indeed, while the Fed staff’s FCI has generally tracked a measure of the Fed’s policy stance (real 5y yield minus HLW r-star) historically, financial conditions have been consistently looser in recent years than implied by this relationship. Squaring the disconnect would be the conclusion that r-star is higher than estimated by HLW and that policy is not as restrictive as believed. Both charts present an intuitive depiction of the idea that the current monetary policy stance is more likely to be somewhat accommodative than noticeably restrictive.
