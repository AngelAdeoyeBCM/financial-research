# EM rates: Pay 5y INR as growth picks up

Chandresh Jain, EM Asia FX and Rates Strategist | MAR | Joey Li, Markets Strategist, APAC | BNP Paribas Singapore Branch. Sources: Reserve Bank of India (RBI), Macrobond, BNP Paribas Fig. 1: Credit growth has started to recover across sectors We favour paying INR rates, as we believe India's economic growth has bottomed-out and recent deals with the EU and the US lower the downside risks to growth. Additionally, the gap between bond supply and demand appears wide this year. Economic growth improving: Several macroeconomic indicators suggest that economic growth has bottomed out towards the end of 2025. Credit growth increased across sectors in recent months (Figure 1). Industrial production, a measure of real output, also surprised to the upside in the last two months of 2025 (6.7% y/y in November versus 2.9% consensus; 7.8% y/y in December versus 5.9% consensus). Other higher-frequency indicators, such as automobile sales and volume of air travel, which proxy for urban demand, also improved towards the end of 2025 (Figure 2). Trade deals: India has been actively pursuing bilateral trade agreements with its partners. The recent EU–India trade deal reduces or eliminates tariffs on most of the Indian goods. The agreement benefits labour-intensive sectors (such as textiles, leather, chemicals, gems and jewellery), comprising more than USD33bn of annual exports to the EU and subjected to import duty between 4% and 26%. Post-deal, this duty will drop to zero. Similarly, the recent US–India trade deal reduces US tariffs on Indian goods to 18% from 50%.

A hold at the Reserve Bank of India's meeting this week should further strengthen the market's conviction that the RBI is done with its cutting cycle. INR rates have rallied on the back of the recent announcement of a US-India trade deal, giving us a good entry level to pay. Trade: 6.16% & roll: -2bp/month. We prefer to pay INR rates, as we believe economic growth has bottomed out and recent deals with Europe and US removes the downside risk to the economy. Additionally, the gap between bond supply and demand appears big this year. Economic growth has bottomed out: Several macroeconomic indicators suggest that economic growth has indeed bottomed out towards the end of 2025, such as credit growth, which saw broad based increases across various sectors (Figure 1). Industrial production, which measures real output, has also surprised significantly to the upside the last two months (Nov: 6.7% y/y vs consensus 2.9%, Dec 7.8% y/y vs consensus 5.9%), signaling a rebounding economy. Other higher frequency indicators such as automobile sales and volume of air travel, which proxy for urban demand, have also seen an uptick towards the end of 2025 (Figure 2). Europe trade deal: Furthermore, India has been actively pursuing bilateral trade agreements with its partners, recently concluding a significant deal with the EU that reduces or eliminates tariffs on most of the Indian goods. This benefits labour-intensive sectors (like textiles, leather, chemicals, gems & jewellery etc), comprising more than USD33b of annual exports to the EU and currently subjected to import duty between 4% to 26%, which will now move to zero. Fig. 1: Credit growth has started recover across sectors E M – YE S FO IE W We think the deals further alleviate downside risks to growth over the medium term. They should boost India's export growth, which has been struggling due to hefty US tariffs. Budget FY2026–27 projects high bond supply: India announced a larger-than-expected budget for FY2026– 27, with record expenditures on infrastructure projects (INR12.2trn, about a 9% y/y increase) and to support manufacturing and defense spending. Further, gross government borrowing is planned to increase to a higher-than-expected INR17.2trn (roughly 18% increase y/y), compared with the anticipated INR16–16.5trn range. The estimated short-term borrowings have also increased by INR1.3trn in this budget, adding upward pressure on short-term yields. In recent years, state government issuances have been on the rise (Figure 3). In this budget, the tax share for states has been maintained at 41% for 2026–31, despite requests from several states to increase it to 50%. Without a substantial increase in tax revenues, states will probably continue to face an increased issuance burden, contributing to a heavy bond supply this year. Overall, we estimate the gross bond supply for FY2026– 27 for the central government and states combined will exceed INR31trn this year (Figure 3). Demand for bonds is weak: India's insurance industry growth has slowed down to around 6% y/y from double-digit growth in the last few years, due to weak economic momentum and regulatory changes. India's Employees' Provident Fund Organisation is also decreasing its bond holdings and increasing its investments in equities to boost the overall return of the fund, thereby reducing the demand for bonds from this industry. Meanwhile, the demand from banks has also fallen due to a pickup in credit growth and less demand from the held-to-maturity (HTM) book. Previously, banks were permitted to shift investments to and from the HTM book once a year at the beginning of the accounting year. However, such transfers are now tightly regulated and restricted to exceptional circumstances, which has further reduced demand for bonds from banks. Finally, foreign investors remain on the sidelines, as the rate-cutting cycle is probably over. Combined with high taxes and elevated FX-hedging costs, this leaves minimal potential returns for them. Risk of hawkish RBI meeting: At the previous RBI meeting, the central bank cut policy rate by 25bp and maintained a neutral stance. Most economists expect the central bank to hold rates steady at the policy meeting on Friday. We think that the RBI could state that economic growth data has improved, the deals with Europe and the US will also help growth, and that the recent budget will bolster infrastructure-led growth in India, sending a potentially hawkish message to the market. Therefore, we believe that a hold decision could lead investors to conclude that the RBI is done with its easing cycle, and INR rates could subsequently sell off. Recent rally in rates: The announcement of the US– India trade deal, which reduced tariffs on Indian exports to 18% from 50%, has cooled selling pressure on the INR. This led to a bull flattening in INR rates, as markets took this as a signal that the RBI will no longer have to intervene as much in the currency markets, thereby easing the tightness in INR liquidity. We think this offers an attractive entry to pay INR rates. Trade idea: 6.17% and roll: –2bp/month. Risk: The key risk to this trade idea would be further open-market operations from the RBI, which are less likely. As the RBI does not need to intervene as much in the FX market after the reversal in USDINR, the need for OMOs will also likely decrease, in our view. FX: We have initiated a long INR trade idea, as we see scope for USDINR to break below 90 in the coming months with economic sentiment improving, encouraging portfolio inflows. Union budget FY26/27 projects high bond supply: The recent Union Budget for FY26/27 was larger than market expectations, with record-high expenditures on infrastructure projects planned (INR12.2trn, a ~9% increase y/y) and targeted at boosting manufacturing and defense spending. Furthermore, gross government borrowing is planned to increase to a higher-than-expected INR17.2trn (~18% increase y/y), versus the anticipated INR16-16.5trn. The gross bond supply for FY26/27 for the center and states will cross INR31trn this year, in our view. Demand for bonds is weak: The demand side of the bonds is already weak this year. India's insurance industry growth has slowed down to around 6% year-over-year from double-digit numbers in the last few years, due to weak economic momentum and regulatory changes. India's Employees' Provident Fund Organisation (EPFO) is also decreasing its bond holdings and increasing its investments in equities to boost the overall return of the fund. This has reduced the demand for bonds from this industry. The demand from banks has also reduced for bonds due to a pickup in credit growth and less demand for the Held to Maturity (HTM) book. However, such transfers are now tightly regulated and restricted to exceptional circumstances. This has reduced the demand for bonds from banks as well. Foreign investors are also remaining on the sidelines, as the cutting cycle is probably over, and FX hedging costs are high, along with taxes, giving minimal potential returns for them. Risk of hawkish RBI meeting: At the previous RBI meeting, the central bank cut policy rates by 25 basis points and maintained a neutral stance. Looking ahead to the upcoming RBI meeting this Friday, most market economists expect the central bank to hold rates steady. In our opinion, the RBI could say that economic growth data has improved, the deals with Europe and the US will help growth even more, and the recent union budget will push infrastructure-led growth in India. Therefore, the message from the RBI could be hawkish. We believe that a hold decision could lead investors to conclude that the RBI is done with its cutting cycle, and rates could subsequently sell off. Recent rally in rates: The announcement of the US- India trade deal, which reduced tariffs on Indian exports from 50% down to 18%, has alleviated selling pressure in the Rupee. This led to a bull flattening in INR rates, as markets saw this as a signal that the RBI will no longer have to intervene as much in the currency markets, which reduces the tightness in INR liquidity. We think that this is providing an attractive entry to pay INR rates with reasons mentioned earlier. Trade: 6.16% & roll: -2bp/month. Risk: The key risk to this trade would be further Open Market Operation (OMO) announcements from the RBI, which we view as less likely. As the RBI does not need to intervene as much in the FX market now after the reversal in USDINR, the need for OMOs will also decrease. FX: On FX side, we are running long INR trade as we see scope for USDINR to break below 90 in the coming months as economic sentiment improves, encouraging portfolio inflows.
