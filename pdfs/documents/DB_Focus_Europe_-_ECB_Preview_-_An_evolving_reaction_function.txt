# Focus Europe - ECB Preview - An evolving reaction function

Economics Focus Europe Date 23 October 2026

We expect the ECB to leave policy rates unchanged at 2.50% on 29 October. This is consistent with a “measured” response to the energy shock, limited pass - through and tighter financial conditions since the September hike. Our baseline remains a final hike t o 2.75% in December, with some risk of a further hike to 3% in 2027. Energy prices are rising above the ECB’s adverse scenario, the economy remains broadly resilient and a mildly restrictive stance feels appropriate. The market-implied terminal rate still feels excessive. It declined from c.3.50% to c.3.20% as the ECB sharpened its communications: President Lagarde reiterated the “measured” response, emphasised embedded energy inflation as the policy target rather than energy inflation itself, and argued that tighter financial conditions can crowd out rate hikes. Financial conditions are doing part of the ECB’s work. In this note we consider the policy-tool debate. The ECB faces two main challenges: inflation and France. We conclude that

1. policy rates remain the primary tool for adjusting the monetary stance;
2. tighter financial conditions may limit rate increases in the coming months; and
3. while inflation risks remain skewed upwards, the optimal combination is likely to be rate hikes —moderated by tighter financial conditions —and TPI, if needed, to stabilise France. This is preferable to slowing or stopping QT to address tighter financial conditions and restarting flexible PEPP reinvestments to stabilise France. The hurdle to changing QT plans is high, especially amid an inflation threat. The focus on 29 October will be communications. Parts of the policy statement and press conference could sound hawkish, particularly with energy prices rising above the September ‘adverse’ scenario. However, we expect a more balanced overall tone that leaves market pricing broadly unchanged. The key message will be the changing weights within the ECB reaction function. At the start of the energy shock, the ‘inflation outlook and risks’ dominated. The ECB can now orient its decision more towards ‘underlying inflation’ —given no evidence of indirect or second-round effects—and ‘transmission’: monetary policy tightened early, and higher global yields and tighter financial conditions, in part driven by France, are reinforcing the policy tightening. Other things discussed in this preview inclu de: what Lagarde could say about France; the conditions for using TPI; whether minimum reserve requirements will rise; the level of neutral rates; what policy rules say about the appropriate level of rates; and whether Lagarde could announce an early exit from the ECB at this meeting. The gradual approach to this hiking cycle – hikes in June, September and likely December – is consistent with
1. President L agarde’s claim that the ECB is delivering a “measured” response to the inflation threat,
2. the lack of evidence yet of indirect and second-round energy inflation effects, and
3. the tightening of financial conditions since the September hike. A landing zone of 2.75 -3.00% is consistent with a mildly restrictive stance on monetary policy. This stance is appropriate given
1. the energy inflation shock is larger and more persistent than expected – energy prices are starting to rise above the ECB’s September ‘adverse’ scenario and we now see HICP averaging 3.1% in 2026 and 3.3% in 2027 –
2. the economy remains broadly resilient in the face of the energy shock. The October PMI was weaker than expected with the composite output index falling from 53.1 to 51. 9 (52.7 expected). However, this masks some divergence. France was weak at 48.4 while Germany was more robust at 52.8 and the rest of the zone is implied at 53.0. The risk is skewed towards the ECB hiking to 3% The fact that energy prices are starting to rise above the ECB’s September ‘adverse’ scenario suggests that 2.75% might not be the peak. That said, the risk of a further hike is contained by the lack of evidence of energy price pass-through as well as the tightening of financial conditions. We calculate that the tightening in the MF-FCI in 2026 is equivalent to one quarter point rate hike. In a recent speech ECB Chief Economist Philip Lane described the tightening of financial conditions as “material”. What is needed to justify a further hike to 3% in March 2027:
1. Iran energy disruptions continue,
2. growth remains robust despite tighter financial conditions (the softer October PMI might signal that financial conditions are starting to bite, at least in France), and – in particular –
3. clear evidence of second-round wage effects. This is not our baseline. We expect Iran normalisation, weakening growth tailwinds as the positive credit and fiscal impulses fade and no strong second-round effects given weak competitiveness and the threat from AI. Bear in mind too that gas prices should be falling as the winter comes to an end. What policy rules say Using the DB forecasts for growth and inflation, the median signal from our policy rules framework is consistent with a peak rate of 2.75%. The ECB’s September ‘baseline’ forecast is consistent with 3.00% rates and cuts before end -2027. The ‘adverse’ scenario also implies rates at 3.00% but remaining there into 2028. The ‘severe’ scenario implies 4% rates or higher – although the absence of feedback effects in the policy rules framework means the further the implied rate is from the ECB’s baseline assumption, the more likely it is to be overestimating the needed tightening of policy. Market pricing at 3.25% still seems excessive The market-implied terminal rate has declined from 3.50% to 3.25%. This repricing occurred around the time of the spike in OAT spreads and following three key messages from Lagarde’s speech to the European Parliament on 28 September:
1. a “measured” response is appropriate to keep inflation in check,
2. the ECB does not target energy, it targets the risk of energy becoming embedded into inflation and there is no evidence of this, and
3. long-term interest rates had risen notably, which will slow growth and reduce the risk of energy inflation becoming embedded. Figure 5: French OAT yields are continuing to rise quickly Figure 6: The levels are still low in comparisons to the member states who suffered crises 10-15 years ago We see policy rates above 3% as consistent with an outright restrictive stance. This would only make sense if there was clear evidence of second-round wage inflation or a much stronger economic recovery that sustains higher core inflation. We don’t see either – and the tightening of financial conditions makes them less likely. The o ther potential driver of rates above 3% is a higher neutral rate (see below for more). Are energy prices rising above the ‘adverse’ scenario consistent with rates above 3%? Not necessarily. First, energy pass-through effects are arguably weaker than the ECB staff have been assuming. Second, financial conditions have tightened. Both mean that higher energy inflation might not mean higher non-energy inflation. Hence the weakening correlation between energy prices and the market-implied terminal rate. Figure 7: Still no compelling evidence of indirect inflation in our high energy intensive index... Figure 8: ...or in our inflation shock momentum (ISM) indicators The policy tool debate: interest rates vs. balance sheet We have been getting lots of questions not just about the path for policy rates but also about TPI and whether the ECB could slow or stop QT. We are also getting questions about the ECB’s new operational framework and when to expect the new structural liqu idity operation and structural bond portfolio to be announced and whether these could play a role in cooling current market conditions. However, it is important not to blur stance and implementation. In terms of monetary stance (and transmission of monetary policy), the ECB faces two main challenges: inflation and France. Inflation requires a tightening of financial conditions. This can be achieved either through an endogenous tightening of the monetary stance or an exogenous increase in market yields. At some point, France might need some idiosyncratic market intervention via the TPI – this will depend on the degree of unwarranted and disorderly market conditions – or TPI will be needed to quell contagion. The conditions under which we could imagine the ECB deploying TPI are: first and foremost, the market overshooting fundamentals; second, a rapidly moving (dysfunctional or disorderly) market; and three, spillover into or amplification of a shock through the domestic financial system. In one sense, the hurdle for intervening with TPI is high. The TPI can only address unwarranted or non-fundamental risk premia. France has weak fiscal fundamentals and ultimately the exit from this episode of stress requires investor confidence in a credible path back to fiscal sustainability. That needs the 2027 Budget to pass without significant slippage and RN’s journey towards fiscal orthodoxy to continue. Neither can be blown off course by social unrest. With the election still six months away, the elevated political risk premia are going to linger. If something needs to happen, maybe French banks’ low exposure to OATs could act as an initial buffer to absorb market pressure. At the same time, the ECB will be watching the speed at which the market is moving. The TPI was created to address unwarranted and disorderly market conditions. A market that is moving too quickly and is becoming dysfunctional could lower the hurdle for intervention. The ECB won’t tolerate irrational pricing, one-way markets, liquidity spirals or flight to quality dynamics. The ECB wants to maintain the disciplining role of the market, but has no interest in the market breaking. If the ECB thought markets were reaching this point, maybe the first response before deploying TPI would be a carefully crafted verbal intervention. Figure 9: The energy inflation shock is larger and more persistent than expected Figure 10: Transport fuels are contributing as much to energy inflation as in 2022 Source: Eurostat, Deutsche Bank Research There is no inconsistency in the ECB hiking policy rates to address inflation and deploying TPI to stabilise France at the same time. The TPI was created in 2022 to allow the ECB to hike policy rates without destabilising sovereign markets. If TPI cannot be deployed – because the risk premia are country - specific/fundamental/warranted – the risk premia could tighten financial conditions to the extent that there is less need for tighter monetary policy to cool the inflation problem. However, the more narrowly concentrated the market pressures are on France, the less effective the tightening of financial conditions will be at addressing a general inflation problem. In other words, when facing an inflation threat, the ECB might still need to tighten the monetary policy stance alongside TPI (note: the BOE response to the LDI crisis in 2022 shows that a central bank can intervene for financial stability purposes while still hiking and running off the balance sheet.). Our sense is the hurdle to changing the QT plans is high, especially when there is an inflation threat. Slowing or stopped QT eases the monetary policy stance. The easiest way to do this would be for the ECB to hike less than priced – in the ECB’s policy tool hierarchy, policy rates have primacy. This is in effect what the ECB said in the September Accounts and what President Lagarde said to the European Parliament on 28 September: higher global yields can crowd out the need to tighten policy rates. Given that policy rates are 2.50% and the market is pricing three further hikes to 3.25%, there is room for the market to price out rate hikes before QT becomes relevant. In short, the rise in global yields is less a problem and more part of the solution, given the inflation threat. Financial conditions were starting to tighten quickly in September, but the pressures have peaked. According to the ECB’s MF-FCI, the peak tightening of financial conditions was on 29 September, before the big spike in OAT yields. After Lagarde’s comments at the European Parliament, the market priced out an ECB hike and capped the tightening in financial conditions despite the increase in French risk premia. This tells us two things. First, the ECB is happy to use higher yields/tighter financial conditions to lean against inflation. Second, the situation would need to change markedly for the ECB to think about slowing or stopping QT. Figure 11: The ECB’s MF-FCI financial conditions gauge has spiked upwards in recent months... Figure 12: ...but peaked on 29-Sep and has trended lower since despite rising OAT yields Talking about the new structural liquidity operation or structural bond portfolio risks confounding the implementation of monetary policy with the appropriate stance on monetary policy. Liquidity conditions are tightening, but not to the extent that the ECB needs to accelerate plans for a new structural liquidity operation – which in a ny case would come before the structural bond portfolio. For more, see the recent note by Soniya Sadeesh from DB Fixed Income Strategy. The bottom line is,
1. the policy rates are still the primary tool for the ECB to adjust the monetary stance,
2. tighter financial conditions might crowd out the extent to which the ECB raises policy rates in the coming months,
3. as long as inflation risks are skewed to the upside the optimal policy combination is likely to be rate hikes (moderated by the degree of tightening of financial conditions) and TPI if needed to stabilise France rather than slowing or stopping QT to address tighter financial conditions and re-engaging flexible PEPP reinvestments to stabilise France. Source: Deutsche Bank Research Other things to watch for in October What Lagarde says about France Things can change quickly and the ECB will be closely monitoring the situation day by day. But right now we would expect President Lagarde to say as little as possible. Lagarde could say that
1. sound public finances are the most important step towards reducing risk premia,
2. the ECB is attentive to markets (through the transmission element of its reaction functio n), and
3. the ECB has tools to deal with unwarranted and disorderly markets if needed (TPI). Minimum reserves In recent months the expectation has been that the ECB would consider raising the minimum reserve requirement from 1% to 2% in the autumn. Technically, this would imply a larger volume of unremunerated reserves. The rationale is policy efficiency: the ECB may conclude that it can achieve the same monetary stance at a lower cost. Had market conditions remained benign, raising reserve requirements would be easier. It could be presented as a normalisation step en route to the new operational framework and pre-announced to give the market time to adjust. However, it is not obvious that the ECB will raise reserve requirements at this time. Market conditions have become less benign and the risk of unintended consequences has increased. Neutral rates Our assessment of the inflation outlook suggests that a mildly restrictive stance on monetary policy would be appropriate. Hence our call for a hike to 2.75% in December, with risk of a further hike to 3% in early 2027. This view assumes that 2.5% is the upper end of the range of nominal neutra l rates, as argued by ECB Chief Economist Philip Lane. There are reasons why neutral rates could be higher, such as the size of the German fiscal easing and the current global competition for capital to fund AI investment. But there is nothing to suggest the ECB is buying into these arguments yet. If they do, the path for monetary policy could be different. One way to explain market terminal rate rising to 3.25-3.50% in recent months is that the upper end of neutral is 3.00% rather than 2.50%. Lagarde’s exit President Lagarde has left open the possibility of leaving the ECB a few months before her term ends in October 2027. We recently wrote that if Lagarde intends to play a role in the French Presidential election campaign – not necessarily as a candidate, but as an influential figure arguing for Europe – then a new ECB President would need to be in place for the March 2027 Governing Council meeting. If the decision-making process to determine her successor followed the standard procedures, Lagarde would need to announce her intention to leave by the October Governing Council meeting to ensure sufficient time to get the successor in place. The EU may be willing to accommodate a faster decision-making process. This was hinted at recently by German Chancellor Merz. Ultimately, the issue is not whether Lagarde leaves early, it’s whether Lagarde’s successor is chosen early. With Isabel Schnabel stepping down to join the IMF, there is an opportunity over the next few months for the European Council (heads of state and government) to determine the composition of the three key roles on the ECB Executive Board – President, Chief Economist, Head of Markets – for the next 8 years. This could lock in the type of monetary policymaking the current European Council wants to preserve before the various European elections over the next year potentially challenge the status quo. For more, see our recent note. Communications – re-weighting the reaction function There is potential for the October policy statement and press conference to feel more hawkish. Energy prices are above the September ‘adverse’ scenario, dbDIG inflation expectations are rising again, etc. However, we think the tone will feel more balanced and keep market pricing relatively unchanged. Beyond the usual data dependent, meeting by meeting, no pre-commitment message, we expect the communications to focus on the changing w eights within the ECB reaction function. In the initial stages of the energy price shock the ‘inflation outlook and risks’ dominated. 6 -7 months after the start of the shock the ECB can afford to adjust and orient the monetary policy decision more towards ‘underlying inflation’ (the absence of evidence of indirect and second-round effects) and ‘transmission’ (the fact that monetary policy tightened, tightened early and the tightening is being reinforced by higher global yields/tighter financial conditions).
