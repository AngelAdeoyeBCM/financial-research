# Focus Europe - Italy - Assessing the Significance of the Upcoming Constitutional Referendum

16 February 2026 Deutsche Bank Research Europe Italy Economics Focus Europe Date

On 22 and 23 March, Italy heads to the polls for a constitutional referendum on a proposed justice system reform sponsored by the current government. Opinion polls show the "No" vote catching up and a close call being in prospect. Much will depend on turnout. There is risk to government stability in holding a referendum, most recently seen in the fall of the Renzi government in 2016. A "No" vote in 2026 would represent a political defeat for Prime Minister Meloni and her government, but it probably would not precipitate the government's resignation or snap elections. Both the 2016 and the upcoming referenda are constitutional, and both were supported by their respective ruling coalitions. However, a significant political distinction exists: Mr. Renzi explicitly linked the survival of his government to the outcome of the 2016 referendum, whereas Ms. Meloni has adopted a more pragmatic approach, deliberately distancing her government from the result. Although the reform is part of her political manifesto ahead of the 2022 general elections, by distancing herself from the result of the vote Ms Meloni is saying she wants to be judged on the overall performance of her government and not just on one part of her programme. The market has adopted a positive stance on Italy of late. Factors such as fiscal consolidation, adherence to EU rules, the judicious use of NGEU conditional loans and grants, and a positive Net International Investment Position (NIIP) have collectively contributed to increased market confidence in Italy. An election is due in Autumn 2027. Polls say Meloni's government is on course for reelection, but there is uncertainty. If the opposition forms an effective alliance, it might compete with the current government — although divergent views on key policies suggest a lack of cohesion at the moment. However, even in the case of an early snap election after a referendum defeat, a new government, even with a different majority, might not deviate from the recent prudent fiscal path, at least in the near term. While we maintain a relatively positive short-term outlook, we harbor slightly more concerns for the coming years, particularly from 2028 onwards. The final tranche of NGEU funds is scheduled for disbursement at the end of this year and is earmarked for spending in 2027. Thereafter, the cyclical boost provided by NGEU will conclude. From that point, we will need to assess whether the NGEU reforms have successfully created structural changes capable of boosting the economy from the supply side. Should the economy slow, political pressures to ease fiscal policy could mount, despite limited room for maneuver. With the average cost February 2026 Page 2 Deutsche Bank AG debt increasing and growth prospects easing, a lack of ongoing fiscal consolidation could lead to a deterioration of public finances. Building confidence in public finances takes time. Losing confidence can happen more rapidly. In this note we detail the specifics of the referendum and analyse its potential consequences for the government and the current legislative term. While there are good chances that the legislature will run its full course until Autumn 2027, the prospect of early general elections being called in Spring 2027 exists, potentially coinciding with crucial local elections in major cities like Milan and Rome. What is the referendum about The justice reform put forward by Justice Minister Nordio introduces several modifications to the Italian Constitution, necessitating a special legislative procedure which eventually ended up in a constitutional referendum (more details on the procedure in the footnote).1 Crucially, there is no quorum for this referendum. The reform will be adopted if the number of votes in support of it exceeds the number of votes against it. The vote will be held on 22 and 23 March. The key elements of the reform are the following:

- Separation of career paths for judges and public prosecutors: Currently, judges and prosecutors in Italy are part of the same professional body and can switch between roles. The reform aims to introduce a definitive separation of these career paths, meaning individuals would choose either to be a judge or a prosecutor from the outset and would not be able to switch.
- Restructuring of the High Council of the Judiciary (Consiglio Superiore della Magistrature or CSM using the Italian terminology): The reform proposes to split the current CSM, the self-governing body of the judiciary, into two distinct councils: one for judges and one for prosecutors.
- Changes to the selection of members for the CSM: The reform suggests a system of selection by sortition (lottery) rather than traditional election for members of these councils.
- Establishment of a High Disciplinary Court: This new court would be responsible for overseeing disciplinary proceedings for both judges and prosecutors. Arguments For and Against the Justice Reform Arguments in Favor ("YES" Vote): Those in favour contend that the separation of careers for judges and public prosecutors from the outset is crucial for eliminating perceived conflicts of interest and bolstering the impartiality of both roles. Furthermore, they argue that selecting members of the High Council of the Judiciary (CSM) by sortition (lottery) rather than election aims to mitigate political influence, internal power struggles, and factionalism within the judiciary's self-governing body. Finally, the establishment of a High Disciplinary Court is seen as a vital mechanism for ensuring greater accountability within the judiciary2. - Futuro Nazionale, the party recently formed by Mr Vannacci (the former Vice President of the League, who departed the party in early February), also favours the reform.
- Among the opposition, two centrist parties, Azione (led by Mr. Calenda) and Europa+, are also in favour of the reform. Opposition to the Reform ("NO" Vote)5 The primary opposition party, the Democratic Party (PD), advocates for a "NO" vote, although some internal differences of opinion exist.6.

- Alleanza Verdi e Sinistra (AVS), a left-wing alliance, opposes the reform. Open Stance:
- The centrist party "Italia Viva," led by former Prime Minister Matteo Renzi, has not provided any official indication to its voters regarding their preferred stance. However, since the official start of the electoral campaign in mid-January, the "NO" campaign appears to be gaining momentum, substantially narrowing the gap. Consequences of a Referendum Defeat While a "No" vote would undoubtedly represent a defeat for Prime Minister Meloni and her coalition—an outcome that could lead to some debates/analysis within the coalition —several arguments suggest it would not inevitably trigger a political crisis or snap elections.
1. Risk of Backfire: A defeat in the referendum could ignite a political debate within the current coalition, potentially leading to tough confrontations among its constituent parties. 2. Challenges in Triggering Snap Elections: It is important to remember that the process leading to early elections is more complex than in many other jurisdictions. Only the President of the Republic has the authority to dissolve Parliament, and the departure of a single party from the majority does not automatically necessitate snap elections. Instead, a new government could potentially be formed by a different coalition capable of securing a parliamentary majority. For instance, in the summer of 2019, Mr. Salvini withdrew from the Five Star Movement/League government with the intention of triggering snap elections, counting on his party's then-high popularity, which could have led to his appointment as Prime Minister. However, a new coalition government comprising the Democratic Party (PD) and the Five Star Movement was subsequently formed without recourse to new elections.
3. Meloni's Stance: Holding a referendum inherently carries risks to government stability, as most recently demonstrated by the fall of the Renzi government in Italy in 2016. Both the 2016 and the upcoming referenda are constitutional, and both were supported by the respective ruling coalitions. However, a significant political difference exists: while Mr. Renzi explicitly linked the survival of his government to the outcome of the 2016 referendum, Ms. Meloni has adopted a more pragmatic approach, deliberately distancing her government from its result. This reform, though an element of her 2022 political manifesto9, is not considered by her to be the sole determinant by which her government's entire performance should be judged.10
4. Strategic Electoral Timing: The current legislative term is nearing its end, with elections constitutionally mandated by Autumn 2027. The incumbent government is in a favourable position to present a budget ahead of the 2027 elections. Italy's anticipated early exit from the Excessive Deficit Procedure (EDP) will create some, albeit limited, fiscal manoeuvring room. While budget tightening will likely continue, it will likely be less stringent than under the EDP. This context allows the ruling coalition to potentially leverage the budget to appeal to its electorate, building momentum for elections in 202711. Prospect of Early General Elections Despite the natural end of the current legislature being Autumn 2027, a window of opportunity for snap elections in Spring 2027 exists for at least two reasons:
- Budgetary Advantage: Elections held in the second half of the year are often problematic, as they leave insufficient time for a newly elected government to adequately prepare the following year's budget, potentially 9 For more please go here 10 See il Corriere della Sera here 11 For a view on fiscal position and economic outlook of Italy please see "Italy: Unpacking the positive market stance" DB on 16 October 2025 and " Italy: Keep prudent and carry on" in our 2026 outlook DB on 5 December 2025 resulting in a continuation of the previous year's fiscal plan. Advancing elections by a few months could provide the necessary timeframe.
- Coinciding Local Elections: Spring 2027 will see several crucial local elections, including in major cities like Milan and Rome. Both are currently governed by the center-left, and incumbents, particularly in local contests, often benefit from their position. Positive outcomes for the center-left in these local elections could generate favorable momentum for them ahead of general elections a few months later (Autumn 2027). Strategically, the centre right might try to hold the local and general elections together. Political leaders are reportedly discussing this possibility, though its likelihood remains unclear12. Market Implications and Policy Stability Markets generally dislike uncertainty, so snap elections could introduce volatility in the debt market. However, we are likely far from a scenario where a newly elected government would adopt policies threatening the ongoing improvement in Italian public finances and leading to negative spreads against the rest of the Euro Area. Although two of the three current coalition parties previously held a tougher stance against Europe, their approach has shifted significantly since taking office. This change is likely influenced by the new EU institutional framework, which combines rules with incentives. The EU's Next Generation EU (NGEU) program, offering concessional grants and loans, has made Italy a primary beneficiary. Adhering to European Commission recommendations, including NGEU implementation and EU fiscal rules, is a necessary (though not sufficient) condition for Italy's eligibility for the ECB's Transmission Protection Instrument (TPI). Consequently, Italy has every incentive to comply with these rules. Fiscal consolidation, NGEU implementation, political stability, and a positive NIIP are crucial factors underpinning market confidence and recent rating agency upgrades (Figure 2 and Figure 3). 12 For more see la Repubblica here 8 Deutsche Bank AG Figure 2: BTP-Bund spreads at very low level Source: Deutsche Bank, Macrobond Figure 3: Rating agencies have adopted a positive stance on Italy Source: Deutsche Bank, Credit agencies A new government, even with a different majority, would likely continue this trajectory, potentially favoring different priorities, but a significant deviation leading to open confrontation with Europe and the rejection of EU fiscal rules seems improbable. Firstly, any alternative government would almost certainly involve a coalition with the pro-European Democratic Party (PD), which would limit any substantial departure from a pro-European path. Secondly, markets would likely punish any deviation, as observed in 2018 during the Five Star/League government. Government stability could affect market sentiment on Italy. The current coalition has demonstrated relative stability, despite the recent defection of Mr. Vannacci (for more see below). For the opposition to defeat the current majority, they would need to form a broad alliance of parties with diverse opinions, risking potential collapse. Political stability is paramount for implementing reforms and investments; without it, GDP growth prospects could diminish. More risks probably from 2028 onwards. While the market has demonstrated positive sentiment towards Italy thus far, and we foresee no particular short-term risks (especially with Italy anticipated to exit the Excessive Deficit Procedure (EDP) this year, a year ahead of schedule), we are more concerned about the coming years, particularly from 2028 onwards. Italy has significantly benefited from a combination of factors, including the new EU framework with Next Generation EU (NGEU) concessional grants and loans, and the European Central Bank's (ECB) Transmission Protection Instrument (TPI). Italy has played its part by respecting fiscal rules and diligently implementing its resilience plan. Consequently, market confidence has increased, and its Net International Investment Position (NIIP) has turned positive. The final tranches of NGEU funds are scheduled for disbursement in the second half of this year and are set to be spent by 2027. This implies that for the remainder of this year and next, Italy can still rely on a cyclical boost from NGEU; however, this will cease from 2028 onwards. By then, if the reforms embedded within the NGEU have not yielded their intended benefits, the economy's growth rate will likely decelerate. With interest rates increasing, the "i-g" gap (the difference between the average cost of debt and the economic growth rate) will become less supportive of debt dynamics, with "i-g" turning positive (Figure 5). This means that, all else being equal, the debt ratio would increase, necessitating additional fiscal consolidation. This would also need to account for the rising cost of an ageing population (Figure 6). Yet, with lower growth, pressures on the government to ease its fiscal stance may intensify, potentially placing upward pressure on Italian public finances. In such a scenario, the market might become less benign towards Italy. Elected as a Member of the European Parliament (as a League candidate) in July 2024 and appointed Deputy Secretary of the League in May 2025, Mr. Vannacci departed the party in early February 2026 to found his own party, Nazione Futura, diverging from the League's official line. Currently, Nazione Futura has only a few Members of Parliament. Its alignment with the current majority remains ambiguous. Despite leaving a government party and initially opposing the government's stance on ongoing support for Ukraine, Nazione Futura ultimately voted confidence in the decree for additional Ukrainian aid13 — the government used a confidence vote for the decree on Ukraine as a strategy to gauge the robustness of the majority. Pollsters have begun assessing the new party's popularity, crediting it with approximately 3% of the popular vote. This support appears to come primarily from the League and Fratelli d'Italia, which have experienced a marginal decline (around 1 percentage point according to some immediate post-defection polls, with slightly less impact subsequently).
