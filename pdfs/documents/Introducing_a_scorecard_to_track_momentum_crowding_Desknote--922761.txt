# Introducing a scorecard to track momentum crowding Desknote

## DEEP DIVE KEY MESSAGES

Momentum has been one of the best-performing equity factors over recent years – but the more crowded a trade becomes, the more violent the unwind. Here we present a four-indicator scorecard for the US momentum factor. It combines price dynamics, options market signals, fundamental valuation and trend analysis, each capturing a different stage of the crowding cycle. Residual return co-movement and implied skew, the positioning-based indicators, are below alert thresholds, suggesting the momentum trade is not currently crowded. However, fundamental indicators tell a different story. While the valuation spread between momentum winners and losers is still below its crowding threshold, it is getting closer to it. The deviation form trend is just above its threshold, flagging a crowded environment. Overall, momentum remains an attractive trade for now, though caution is warranted – the tools below will help you monitor crowding and identify optimal exit signals. 22/04/2026 1 Chloe Duban, Cross-Asset & Macro Quant Strategist | BNP Paribas London Branch. At its core, crowding in financial markets occurs when too many investors hold similar positions for similar reasons. In the context of equity factors, it manifests as an excess accumulation of capital in stocks that score highly on a particular characteristic. The mechanism is self-reinforcing: as a factor attracts inflows, its constituent stocks are bid upward, performance improves, further inflows follow and the pattern resumes. The trade becomes consensus, and consensus trades are fragile. The risk is not merely theoretical. When positioning gets sufficiently crowded, even a modest shift in sentiment can trigger a disorderly unwind. The August 2007 “quant crisis” remains the canonical episode, but the momentum crashes of early 2009 and late 2018 share the same fingerprints, as does 2021’s post-Covid reflation trade. Momentum is structurally prone to crowding. It is a purely price-based and self-referential signal. This makes momentum structurally negatively skewed (Figure 2). Gains are steady and gradual, but its crash episodes are disproportionately severe. This paper attempts to develop and test a set of crowding indicators drawing on cross-sectional price dynamics, options market information, fundamental valuation signals and trend analysis. The goal is to provide a structured framework for assessing when the risk/reward of holding momentum tilts unfavourably and, conversely, when a less crowded momentum factor presents a compelling entry point. Price-based and implied skew indicators are at low percentiles, suggesting momentum is not acutely crowded today. The risk is not immediate but latent. If momentum draws renewed inflows, the high starting valuation means the crowding cycle could intensify faster and from a higher base than history suggests. When all four signals are elevated, the framework produces its highest-conviction crowding alert. When they diverge, as today, the scorecard invites a nuanced view: acknowledge residual fundamental risk while recognizing that the immediate positioning threat has receded. Overall, using this four-indicator scorecard lets you spot early tremors of a crowded momentum trade, decide whether to stay the course, and know when to step away before the unwind becomes violent.

- Watch the crowd: A rise in residual co-movement or implied skew signals investors moving in lockstep.
- Read the price: When the valuation spread widens, the long leg is priced beyond its fundamentals.
- Check the trend: A large deviation from the factor’s long-run path may indicate a new regime or inflated crowding.
- Combine the signals: Only when all four indicators align should you treat crowding risk as acute; divergent readings call for a measured, nuanced view. Shared ownership creates shared price dynamics – and that co-movement, once stripped of common risk factors, is precisely what we seek to capture. Construction Methodology The construction proceeds in two steps. For each stock in our US momentum basket (BNPFMOM1 Index), we run a time-series regression of daily returns against the S&P 500 and four Dow Jones equity style factors: momentum, value, quality and low beta. These five regressors absorb the bulk of systematic return variation, namely market beta and factor exposures. What remains – the residual – is in theory idiosyncratic to each stock. We then compute the average pairwise correlation of these residuals across all holdings in the basket. This cross-sectional average becomes our crowding score, percentile-ranked against its own history to produce a 0–100 reading (Figure 3). The link to crowding is intuitive. Under normal conditions, idiosyncratic residuals should be largely uncorrelated – by construction, there is no common factor driving them. But when a large and homogeneous investor base owns the same stocks, selling pressure or momentum-chasing affects all holdings simultaneously and in the same direction. The result is a rise in residual co-movement that cannot be explained by market or factor dynamics alone. This is the crowding signal. In spirit, this approach is close to Baltas (2019), who similarly exploited residual return correlations to detect positioning congestion in futures markets. Decile Analysis To assess whether the indicator carries genuine forward-looking content, we ran a decile analysis sorting historical observations of the price-based crowdedness score against subsequent three-month momentum long/short returns, with a three-month lag to reflect implementation realism (Figure 4). Key Takeaways The results were striking. Across the first eight deciles, average three-month momentum returns were positive and relatively stable, i.e. moderate crowding does not impair factor performance. The picture changes sharply in the top decile. The intuition is simple: when a trade is crowded, investors express growing conviction in its continuation through the options market. In turn, that directional consensus leaves a detectable imprint on the implied volatility surface. Construction Methodology For each stock in our US long/short momentum basket (BNPFMOM1 Index), we decompose the implied skewness of the return distribution into two distinct components. The upside skew is constructed as the spread between the three-month implied volatility at a 120% strike and the at-the-money (ATM) implied volatility, computed separately for the long and short legs of the factor and then weighted accordingly. A high reading signals pricing of strong continuation on the long leg in options, i.e. investors are expressing growing conviction that momentum winners will keep winning. The downside skew mirrors this logic: it compares the three-month implied volatility at an 80% strike to ATM, again across both legs. Elevated downside skew on the short leg indicates market pricing of continued deterioration of momentum losers, reinforcing the directional consensus on that side of the book. The two components are then summed into a single composite implied skewness score, which is percentile-ranked over its history. The options market is in many ways a more forward-looking crowding gauge than price-based measures. Residual correlations reflect what has already happened in positioning while implied skewness signals what options traders expect to happen. When crowding is building, that expectation tends to manifest in the volatility surface before it shows up in realised returns. The two indicators are therefore complementary. Decile Analysis The decile analysis for the implied skewness indicator (Figure 6) uses a one-month lag, reflecting the more forward-looking nature of options pricing. The pattern that emerges is directionally consistent with the price-based results, but the signal structure differs in an important way. The premise is straightforward: if crowding creates excess capital in winners at losers’ expense, it should leave a detectable imprint not only in prices and volatility surfaces but also in the relative valuation multiples of the long and short legs. Construction Methodology We construct it as follows. For each stock, we retrieve three valuation multiples – price-to-earnings (PE), price- to-sales (PS), and price-to-book (PB). We compute a weighted aggregate valuation for the long leg using positive exposures, and a similar measure for the short leg using absolute negative exposures. The spread is the log-ratio of short-to-long valuation, capturing how expensive the long leg trades relative to the short leg. We z-score this spread against its expanding history, interpreting the indicator relative to its own past distribution and avoiding look-ahead bias. The final composite valuation spread is the simple average of the three z-scores – PE, PS and PB – giving a more robust reading than any single multiple. A high z-score indicates the long leg trades at a historically elevated premium relative to the short leg, beyond normal cross-sectional valuation dispersion. The link to crowding is intuitive and grounded in literature. Momentum baskets are long recent winners and short recent losers. Normally, winners trade at a moderate premium – a valuation gap reflecting genuine earnings momentum and compensated by forward returns. When the trade becomes crowded, persistent inflows push winners’ valuations higher, detached from fundamental improvement. The spread widens not because the longs are better businesses, but because too much capital chases the same stocks. This dynamic precedes momentum crashes: the long leg becomes expensive, the short leg cheap, and potential energy for a sharp mean-reversion builds. This indicator is the only one anchored in fundamentals, not market microstructure or options pricing. Decile Analysis Using a six-month lag, the decile analysis matches the slow-moving nature of fundamental valuation signals. The results are compelling and, crucially, easier to interpret than at shorter horizons. In the lower six deciles, returns are broadly positive, showing that only the tail of the valuation distribution carries the warning signal. Valuation spread Key Takeaways Combined with the residual-correlation and implied-skew indicators, the valuation spread completes a three-dimensional crowding framework: one signal based on price co-movement, one on options-market expectations, and one on fundamental overvaluation. Each captures a distinct facet of the same phenomenon, and their joint elevation provides the strongest crowding alert our framework can generate. The intuition is straightforward: when a factor becomes crowded, capital inflows push its price above its fundamental trajectory, generating a detectable departure from trend. Construction Methodology We compute this deviation using a rolling approach. At each date, we fit a linear trend over the preceding three years of the BNPFMOM1 Index and define the crowding score as the percentage deviation of the current price from this rolling trend. The score is then percentile-ranked over its history to produce a 0-100 reading. Decile Analysis The decile analysis (see Figure 9) reveals a clear and monotonic relationship between the deviation score and subsequent one-month momentum returns. Across the lower deciles, average returns are positive, reaching approximately +1% to +1.4% in the least crowded bucket. As the score rises, returns decline steadily and turn negative from around the 60th percentile onward. Key Takeaways This pattern is consistent with the price-based and implied skew indicators and confirms that an elevated deviation from trend is a meaningful signal of crowding risk. Currently, the indicator stands at the 61 percentile, signalling that momentum is trading above its 3-year trend — consistent with a moderately crowded environment.
