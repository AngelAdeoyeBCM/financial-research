# Sunday Tea with BNPP: Hawkish leanings

. 22/03/2026 1

RKE.

## KEY MESSAGES

- The risk of a prolonged conflict in the Middle East is growing, suggesting that energy prices can stay higher for longer.
- Central banks globally are more concerned about the conflict’s impact on inflation than on growth.
- Markets have begun to price in rate hikes by several key central banks. We would not stand in the way of it. The Middle East conflict continues to escalate. Critical energy assets are now in the crosshairs, with an attack on Qatar’s LNG facilities last week inflicting damage that may take years to repair. This implies a structural supply risk to energy on top of the current disruptions. Unless de-escalation happens soon, we see a possible deterioration toward an outcome that could see energy prices higher for longer and potentially even exceed our worst-case outcome of oil at $130/bbl. Supply-driven energy shocks pose opposing risks for most central banks by putting upward pressure on inflation and downward pressure on growth. While there were question marks heading into last week over which of these central bankers would prioritize, communication from a swath of policy meetings was clear: they see inflation as the more important battle (Figure 1). The reasoning is simple. While the textbook response would be to “look through” the inflationary impact as “transitory”, this relies on one very important assumption: that long-term inflation expectations are well anchored. Anchored inflation expectations can no longer be taken for granted, with the current energy-driven inflation shock being the fourth successive price shock in the past five years (the post-pandemic reopening, Russia’s invasion of Ukraine and 2025’s Liberation Day tariffs being the others). With that, there are risks of inflation expectations destabilizing, with second-round effects from higher energy prices potentially driving an upward consumer-price spiral. Fig. 1: Fed very worried about risks to inflation Sources: Federal Reserve, BNP Paribas Last week’s FOMC meeting was as hawkish as possible without major tweaks to the SEP or the policy bias (the former of which Chair Powell quickly dismissed once the press conference began). First, the Fed already had an inflation problem before the Middle East conflict. Core PCE has surprised to the upside in recent months, which alone would have driven policymakers to mark up inflation projections. Second, the Fed is increasingly worried that its policy stance may not even be restrictive. Indeed, when asked why there has been no progress in non-shelter services inflation over the past year (which should have been expected if policy were truly restrictive), Powell simply responded that this is “frustrating”. And third, with inflation having missed the official target for a fifth straight year, there are worries that inflation expectations could destabilize. In an ideal world, Powell would likely prefer to have more time to a ssess the impact of the oil price shock. But ultimately, he does not. April is most likely his last meeting in charge, and he will need to make a choice. To maintain Powell’s own credibility and that of the Fed, we expect officials to adopt a symmetric policy bias (indicating that a rate hike or a cut is equally likely to come) at next month’s meeting,. 22/03/2026 2 provided energy prices remain elevated and the US unemployment rate is broadly stable. This aligns well with our out-of-consensus call from earlier this year that the Fed would keep rates on hold for all of 2026. While rate hikes are not yet part of our base case, we would not stand in the way of the market pricing them. This is for two reasons. First, although positioning has been reduced, the market remains in steepeners, as shown by our COSI index (Figure 2). Second, the recent experience in the eurozone and UK rates markets will likely promote caution, with investors reluctant to try catching a “falling knife”. Fig. 2: Market is still in steepeners Sources: Bloomberg, BNP Paribas But it’s not just the front end of the curve that is vulnerable. We also see risks to the back end as term premiums have recently fallen despite increasing deficit risks. Indeed, the war is reported to be costing $1bn/day, chances are rising of the government pushing through a fiscal package in coming months to support consumers as gasoline prices rise, and there are question marks over the replacement of IEEPA tariffs. Moreover, weakness in swap spreads further increases the risk of a self-reinforcing feedback loop, where higher Treasury yields can lead to more swap spread unwinds and in turn more selling of Treasuries. Our then out-of-consensus forecast from last year for the 30y yield to hit 5.0% may now look too conservative. Although not entirely immune from risk-off from geopolitics, US equities remain resilient on a relative basi s. The equity options market continues to reflect a massive risk premium, however. The VIX is trading at a 13pt premium to SPX 1m realized volatility, the 98th percentile in the past two years. Last week’s 1.3% SPX spot move also compares poorly to the almost 3% implied move at the beginning of the week. As such, we continue to like hedges with a short downside vol bias, such as put VKOs. In Europe, rhetoric from central banks last week made clear that they want to avoid repeating mistakes from 2022. The BoE removed its bias for further easing entirely, with the decision being unanimous – something not seen in a long time. Meanwhile, the ECB is no longer “in a good place” and did not push back against market pricing of rate hikes. The likelihood that the ECB re-engages with tightening (starting in June) has sharply increased. The shift in rhetoric from BoE policymakers has also opened the door to rate hikes this year, perhaps as early as April. This would send the rates market into a very different paradigm compared to just a few weeks ago, one where the bear flattening momentum has room to extend. As such, we are not fading these moves yet. Meanwhile, Italians are heading to the polls today and tomorrow to vote on a constitutional referendum approv ing or reject ing a government-sponsored reform on the justice system. P rime Minister Meloni has not tied the continuation of her government to the outcome of this vote. But in the (unlikely) event of a government collapse, BTPs would likely feel meaningful widening pressure. In FX, we have added a short EURUSD spot position to our strategy portfolio. In recent weeks, higher front-end European rates h ave insulated the common currency from much of the negative terms-of-trade shock (Figure 3). However, we view EURUSD stability in the context of higher energy prices as unsustainable: any related ECB tightening is unlikely to prove bullish for EURUSD as it would likely weigh on growth, discouraging capital inflows. EURUSD has also exhibited extremely strong spot-vol correlation in recent days. A good start to 2026 lessens the urgency for policymakers to deliver incremental stimulus, and we now expect the PBoC’s first rate cut of 2026 to occur in H2. To monetize the commitment to technology developments as outlined in the latest Five Year Plan, we have introduced a new BNPP China ‘Emerging Pillar’ and ‘Future Industries’ basket (BNPBCEPF). Following the hold decision at March’s policy meeting, our base case remains that the BoJ will hike rates in April, given more board members were worried about upside risks to underlying inflation than downside risks to growth. We maintain our steepener on the 10s20s swap curve. In EM Asia, we are running a paid bias in rates, driven by the region’s significant exposure to energy and food prices in CPI baskets. This vulnerability is further amplified by the region’s heavy reliance on imported Middle Eastern oil, which heightens the risk of supply chain disruptions and price fluctuations. We are paid in THB 5y swap and running an INR 1s5s steepener. On the FX side, we are short THB versus long KRW. Thailand has a substantial energy deficit, which makes it particularly susceptible to increases in oil prices, and tourism inflows are likely to decline due to the ongoing Middle East conflict. In CEEMEA, inflation outlooks seem increasingly vulnerable. As the CNB alluded last week, central banks are not yet panicking and rushing to hike rates, preferring rather to wait for data to guide reaction functions. Nonetheless, energy prices remaining higher for longer can meaningfully lower the bar to a policy pivot for some countries in the region. This week’s central bank decisions in Hungary and South Africa are unlikely to deviate materially from what peers indicated last week, but we do think that in the latter case there is the risk of a more clearly defined scenario that could eventually force a rate hike. Finally, in Latam, the rise in oil prices is already affecting central banks’ decisions. Last week’s 25bp rate cut in Brazil was a cautious reaction to increased uncertainty. Although the statement noted that officials intend to accelerate the pace to 50bp in April if the Middle East conflict eases and oil prices subside substantially, we now see higher odds of another 25bp move in April given the low likelihood of oil prices returning to pre-conflict levels. This week brings decisions in Chile and Mexico and we expect holds for both, with Banxico revising inflation forecasts upward again and likely being more flexible on forward guidance. Last week we examined the Sharpe ratio of various trading strategies across Latam FX, swaps, local bonds and cross currency basis in past oil price shocks, identifying steepeners in swaps as the best strategy. Curves tend to steepen during these shocks and remain steep for two months after oil reaches a peak. Consequently, we entered into a 28s31s DI steepener in Brazil.
