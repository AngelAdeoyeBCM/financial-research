# Fixed Income Blog - ECB - this is not 2011

Rates Fixed Income Blog Date 12 June 2026

Hiking in response to a negative supply shock is, by definition, uncomfortable. This is leading some to conclude that the ECB is repeating the policy mistake of 2011. However, the comparison with 2011 is misplaced for three fundamental reasons. Firstly, the private sector is more resilient than in the aftermath of the GFC. Secondly, intra-EU divergences are smaller, as peripheral countries have significantly improved their external positions. Thirdly, on a forward-looking basis, fiscal policy is likely to ease rather than tighten, as it did post-2011. Back in 2011, the ECB tightened monetary policy in response to the negative supply shock triggered by the Arab Spring, which led to higher oil prices. Within six months, the ECB had to reverse course. Ultimately, the easing cycle ended with negative interest rates and large-scale QE. While the parallel with 2011 is appealing, it is unlikely to hold, as both initial conditions and forward-looking dynamics are materially different. The private sector is in better shape, as evidenced by the fact that excess savings accumulated during Covid have yet to be depleted. By contrast, the savings rate was below historical averages going into 2011. Figure 3: Unlike in 2011, households are likely to have excess savings Figure 4: The eurozone external position has improved markedly since 2011 Secondly, we have long argued that the external position is more relevant for sovereign risk than the fiscal position. On that metric, the external position of the EU as a whole has improved materially. Moreover, weaker peripheral countries have made significant macroeconomic adjustments and, as a result, their external positions have converged towards those of core and semi-core economies. In fact, France is arguably the weakest link from an external perspective. However, this is already priced to some extent, and France’s unique position as an independent nuclear power (both military and civilian) should carry some weight in the current geopolitical context. Not only is this unlikely to be repeated, but fiscal policy is more likely to be structurally easier going forward. Figure 7: In 2011, the ECB hike was followed by a substantial fiscal tightening. This time, fiscal policy is expected to ease Source: Deutsche Bank Research, European Commission, Haver Bottom line: Hiking in response to a negative supply shock is inherently uncomfortable. However, both initial conditions and forward-looking expectations for fiscal policy suggest that this is unlikely to be a repeat of the 2011 policy mistake.
