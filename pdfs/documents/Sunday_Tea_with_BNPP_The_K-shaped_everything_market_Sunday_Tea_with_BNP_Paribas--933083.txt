# Sunday Tea with BNPP: The K-shaped everything market

## OUR KEY VIEWS

- Last week’s data supports our view that the FOMC will opt for a ‘skip’ in October, but underlying economic strength means we still expect at least two more hikes.
- The extent and speed of recent OAT-Bund widening has been more than fundamentals alone would suggest. However, several circuit breakers would be required for a sustained tightening.
- We favour long GBPUSD to position for reduced UK political uncertainty and wide rate differentials, while we prefer to hedge against further EUR weakness via short EURAUD and EURMXN. While ‘K-shaped’ has long been a staple of economic discourse to describe divergent prospects between different parts of the population (most notably between high- and low-income individuals), in recent weeks such bifurcation has also appeared across markets. This is evident in the decoupling of US stocks from the rest of the world and the Nasdaq from the Russell, as well as the UST curve where the front-end has outperformed the back end. It is also visible in the energy complex, with crude prices falling yet pressures on product prices remaining, in part driven by ongoing speculation about a potential US ban on diesel exports. And most notably in Europe, the past week has been defined by a sharp widening in EGB spreads rather than a broad-based sell-off. Bunds have rallied amid a flight to quality, while OATs have faced significant selling pressure, driven by intensifying investor concerns around debt sustainability. While the spread has been widening since early September, last week presented the first signs of broader stress, with spillovers into other EGBs, bank equities and credit. Of course, the key question is where to from here? An October FOMC ‘skip’: The relatively soft 29k rise in nonfarm payrolls in September, when combined with PCE revisions that revealed a slightly softer underlying pace of core inflation, likely takes some pressure off the FOMC and supports our view that the Committee will opt to wait until December before removing some further accommodation. However, we don’t think the data materially changes the narrative of a hot economy with sticky price pressures that warrants further tightening. Indeed, the totality of data released in recent weeks points to robust economic activity in the US. The headline NFP number belied a stronger underlying picture, when considering the whopping 406k rise in employment as measured by the household survey, increasing hours worked which points to increased labour utilization, and the fact that the rise in the unemployment rate from 4.1% to 4.2% was primarily driven by a rise in the participation rate. Indeed, if participation had remained steady, the unemployment rate would have printed 3.9% (see US September jobs: Not great, not terrible, dated 2 October). But not a peak: We therefore continue to expect another two hikes – at a minimum – from the FOMC, in order to remove last year’s ‘insurance’ cuts and take policy to a more neutral level in real terms. A key question we have been receiving in recent days and weeks is whether a decline in energy prices would allow the Fed to do less. This has become an even more pertinent question with crude flows from the Middle East close to pre-war levels and Friday’s media reports that the G7 would release up to 100 million barrels of oil and diesel stocks over the next four months – something that we flagged more than a week ago as more likely to occur before any US diesel ban (see Running out of options – EU SPR release likely before US diesel export cap, dated 24 September). This suggests some scope for downside pressure on crude – and product prices – in the near term. However, crude’s remaining strength suggests risks remain and markets don’t assume an imminent and/or complete resolution. We agree with that premise. In our view, every workaround is vulnerable to disruption and Iran losing leverage on the Strait of Hormuz could mean it seeks to rebuild it through other means. But we don’t view energy prices as the primary driver of pressure in the US. Instead, the issue is about the entrenchment of pandemic-era inflation into services, combined with persistently elevated aggregate demand in the economy. As such, lower energy prices would take some headline pressure off but would likely lead to a positive response in sentiment, a rise in investment and consumption that could necessitate an even tighter monetary policy stance (see US: The Fed is likely hiking rates whether oil goes up, down or sideways, dated 29 September). While these developments have provided some relief to the back end of the US yield curve, we don’t think this is a reason to turn bullish on the long end of the yield curve. We think the market will need to see delivery of further rate hikes for the Fed to re-establish credibility in the eyes of investors while fiscal fundamentals have not changed, and we see scope for wider term premiums. Europe is different: A decline in energy prices would be a more welcome source of relief for the ECB, where price pressures are more externally driven than domestically entrenched. Combined with the tightening of financial conditions, reflected – among other things – in wider peripheral bond spreads, suggests a near-zero chance that the Governing Council will vote to accelerate the tightening cycle by hiking again in October, in line with our view. We continue to expect one more hike, in December, before an extended pause. Recent volatility adds weight to our view that the ECB will deliver fewer rate increases than the market has priced, with the Governing Council likely to prefer a ‘high for longer’ strategy instead. However, we have received fewer questions on the likely path for ECB policy rates over the last week than on its reaction function to the widening in EGB spreads. The 10y OAT-Bund spread briefly touched 150bp on Friday, its highest since 2012 during the height of the eurozone sovereign debt crisis. The latest leg of the move looks to be less a reflection of fundamentals – which have not suddenly deteriorated even though they remain weak – and more stop-out-driven selling. Our fundamental analysis suggests that 150bp on 10y OAT-Bund spreads would be a level consistent with the market pricing a country going towards BBB+, levels reached briefly on Friday. However, for a sustained rally in OAT spreads where fundamentals start to reassert themselves, we would need:
1. positioning to be cleared out, especially front-end longs;
2. the budget in France to pass this year, with the social security vote in November an important test;
3. strong OAT auctions as an important source of price discovery and demand;
4. greater details on the RN’s fiscal consolidation plan, expected in the coming week;
5. a shift in the French opinion polls. Meanwhile, we think that there is a high bar for the ECB to step in and use its ‘Transmission Protection Instrument’, which was unveiled in 2022 but remains untested. The tool is designed to counter both “unwarranted” and “disorderly” market moves that “seriously” impair the transmission mechanism of monetary policy. Intervening could also carry moral hazard issues, to the extent that it blunts the price signal for individual governments to tackle underlying fiscal issues. We will be watching closely the cacophony of ECB Governing Council members due to speak this week who will no doubt be asked about their level of openness to using the tool and/or other options – such as an earlier end to rate hikes or putting the brakes on QT. Last week, we added EUR downside protection against OAT-spread widening to our strategy portfolio via short EURAUD, in addition to our EURMXN ratio put spread. Our existing forecasts captured risk premium being embedded into the EUR ahead of the 2027 French election in Q1 as was the case in prior presidential election years. However, this premium is being embedded sooner and more quickly than we thought, implying more front-loaded weakness and representing downside risk to our 1.14 year-end target for EURUSD. While ultimately our base case is that French election risks are evenly balanced, with the election still a few months away and room for risk premium to continue to rise, we prefer to hedge the risk of further EUR weakness. Fiscal concern not extending to the gilt market: Broader market concerns around fiscal policy failed to extend to the UK. Gilts outperformed relative to most European peers and the GBP held its ground through the week. The solid performance of UK assets materialised, despite the timing of the Labour Party conference and the accompanying policy announcements. The most consequential announcement from Prime Minister Burnham’s inaugural speech was a “fully funded” national social care service (about GBP18bn/year). Funding will be partially secured through a reform of the UK’s pension triple lock from 2030, though additional tax hikes or spending cuts will likely be necessary to fully fund the service. Nevertheless, the announcements reinforced that th e government is pursuing a phased approach to major reforms, with many measures likely to be implemented in the next parliament, therefore having limited implications for the forthcoming budget (28 October). We think the GBP is well placed to benefit in the coming weeks given reduced UK political uncertainty, ongoing M&A inflows, and wide rate differentials. We entered a long GBPUSD position to fade extended short positioning into a seasonally bullish period (FX: Long GBPUSD, dated 30 September). BoJ remains on a quarterly cadence: Events over the past week have also increased the probability that the BoJ will pursue a quarterly hiking cycle in the near term, in our view. The July Tankan shows corporate resilience underpinned by the global AI boom, alongside sustained inflationary pressure. September’s Tokyo CPI also signaled a resurgence in consumer inflation; although partly influenced by a policy factor, the data confirmed spillovers from higher energy and semiconductor prices, as well as wage-driven service inflation. However, the Tankan also showed a slight softening in corporates’ inflation expectations, albeit at still high levels; this could provide some relief for the BoJ as it monitors upside risks to underlying inflation. This, coupled with a relatively neutral ‘Summary of Opinions’ from the BoJ’s September meeting, suggests that the likelihood of a consecutive hike in October is limited. We anticipate that Governor Ueda’s upcoming speech on Tuesday will remain non-committal regarding the policy outlook. Brazil election in focus: Brazil’s first round election will take place today, with results to be finalized and announced by 9.30pm local time. The first round will set the results for both houses of Congress and define the second rounds for the gubernatorial and presidential races. A difference of 3 -4pp between Lula and Flavio Bolsonaro in this first round seems to be the neutral scenario, suggesting a tie in the second round between the two leading candidates – our poll aggregator currently shows Lula’s first round advantage at 3pp. Join us to discuss the results of the first round and political implications with political analyst Thomas Traumann, tomorrow at 1pm London / 8am EST (register here). Colombia’s Banrep surprised again with a 25bp hike in its policy rate, above the market’s and our expectation for a pause at 12.0%. The probability of a 25bp hike at the next policy decision on 30 October now rises following Friday’s hike, we believe (please see BanRep: Surprise hike marks hawkish turn dated September 30). A hawkish BanRep reinforced our conviction in our short MXNCOP position. We also took advantage of the hawkish surprise to initiate a Long Coltes 2031 UVR vs Pay 5y SOFR trade and a z36-3y COP basis payer. Energy prices trigger outlook revision in CEE: We have revised our outlook for CEE central bank policy given the lack of a clear path for a swift and sustainable decline in energy and related product prices. With inflation set to temporarily exceed the CNB’s tolerance band by early 2027, we also now see a 25bp rate hike in the Czech Republic in November, before a sustained hold into late 2027. In Hungary, the NBH is likely to remain on hold for now, until external risk sentiment improves. We have removed one of two 25bp cuts we pencilled in for Q4. Fundamentally we maintain that Hungary has bigger mitigating factors to help to stem rising near-term price pressures thanks, in part, to positive sentiment around its euro adoption plans – see Hungary: Euro vision, dated 4 June. For Poland, the latest announcement by the President regarding a re-introduction of large fuel subsidies (fiscal neutral thanks to energy windfall taxes) into the end of the year creates some challenges to our recently updated Polish rates forecast. This should materially improve the short - term inflation outlook which we had previously expected to breach the NBP’s tolerance band. This reduces the risk of the NBP hiking rates in the short term and could shift tightening out to early next year versus our revised expectation for a mini-rate hike cycle to kick off as early as November. The NBP’s post-MPC press conference this coming week is likely to provide more colour on how the central bank is thinking about the medium-term risks to its estimates: see CEE rates: Sustained energy crisis set to trigger policy adjustments, dated 1 October.
