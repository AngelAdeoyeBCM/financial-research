# Fed Chatterbox: March Edition

13 March 2026 | 11:53AM EDT The Fed Chatterbox highlights notable comments on key topics from Fed officials since the last FOMC meeting. A few themes stand out: Since the FOMC’s January meeting, most participants have shared New York Fed President Williams’ view that “monetary policy is currently well positioned to support the stabilization of the labor market and return inflation to our 2% goal.” A few participants have echoed Governor Barr, who said that “it will likely be appropriate to hold rates steady for some time.” Governor Miran, who dissented in favor of a rate cut at the January meeting, said instead that four cuts in 2026 would be “appropriate,” adding that he would “rather get them sooner than later.” Most participants have shared Vice Chair Jefferson’s view on inflation that “some upside risks remain, but I expect the disinflationary process to resume this year once increased tariffs pass through more fully to prices.” However, a few participants agreed with Governor Barr, who said that “there are many reasons to be concerned that inflation will remain elevated,” and characterized “the risk of persistent inflation above our 2% target” as “significant.” A few participants commented on the impact of the war with Iran on inflation before the start of the blackout period. While Governor Waller argued that it “is unlikely to cause sustained inflation,” President Goolsbee warned that “if you’re now going to pile on a quite large energy and commodity shock, this might be big enough to be comparable to some of the big supply shocks in the past.” Prior to the release of the February employment report, President Williams noted that “in recent months, there have been promising signs of stabilization” in the labor market. Several other participants have shared this view but acknowledged that, as Governor Cook said, “the lack of dynamism in the labor market likely makes it susceptible to downside shocks.” After the release of a weaker-than-expected February employment report, a few participants have shared Governor Bowman’s concern that “the labor market is deteriorating.” President Daly also noted that “the hopes that the labor market was steadying— maybe that was too much—and we really have to keep our eye on the labor market.” Governor Bowman added that the labor market “could use some support from our policy rate,” a view shared by Governor Miran. Some participants have agreed with President Collins’ argument “for maintaining policy rates at their current, mildly restrictive levels for some time.” But other participants disagreed with this. A few have shared President Goolsbee’s view Economics Research that “it is not obvious that our interest rate policy is even restrictive.” Others including Governor Waller have instead argued that “monetary policy is still restricting economic activity” and, as Governor Bowman said, that “absent a clear and sustained improvement in labor market conditions, we should be ready to adjust policy to bring it closer to neutral.” The Policy Outlook Bowman There is a trend—that the labor market is deteriorating. This allows me to continue with the path that I believe is appropriate for 75bp of cuts by the end of this year. Remarks Mar 6 Collins

1. Though considerable economic uncertainty remains, exacerbated by recent geopolitical developments like the hostilities in the Middle East, my baseline outlook is fairly benign – featuring continued solid economic growth, relatively balanced labor market conditions, and disinflation resuming later this year as tariff effects fade.
2. I do not see an urgency for additional policy adjustments, and I will be looking for clear evidence that inflation is moving durably toward the 2% target – something that might occur only over the second half of the year. Remarks Mar 6 Daly
1. The hopes that the labor market was steadying, maybe that was too much, and we really have to keep our eye on the labor market. But we also have inflation printing above our target and oil prices rising. How long it will last we don’t know. But both our goals are at risk now and we have to keep our eye on both.
2. I think there is a real issue about whether we should immediately, urgently act on the labor market or whether we should wait to think through the data. Remarks Mar 6 Goolsbee [A sustained rise in the unemployment rate on top of an oil-price shock would create] exactly the kind of stagflationary environment that’s as uncomfortable as any that faces a central bank. Remarks Mar 6 Hammack
1. We need to make sure we are maintaining a balance of policy that is going to help bring inflation back to target while still supporting the labor market. When I look at things broadly, to me it seems that there are two-sided risks to rates.
2. I think we could be on hold for quite some time. Remarks Mar 3 Williams
1. Overall, the U.S. economy appears to be on a good footing. Growth is solid, the labor market is showing signs of stabilizing, and while inflation remains above our 2% target, recent data have been reassuring.
2. Monetary policy is currently well positioned to support the stabilization of the labor market and return inflation to our 2% goal. Looking further ahead, if inflation follows the path I expect, further reductions in the federal funds rate will eventually be warranted to prevent monetary policy from inadvertently becoming more restrictive. Remarks Mar 3 Kashkari
1. I felt like policy was in a pretty good place and we had the luxury to let it glide back to neutral. Now we need to see this new shock hitting the global economy—how long and how big is the effect.
2. I had one expected cut later on this year. Now with the geopolitical events, I just need to see and we need to get a lot more data. Remarks Feb 26 Miran Four cuts [in 2026] I think are appropriate. I’d rather get them sooner than later. Remarks Feb 23 Waller Assuming underlying inflation continues to signal we are close to our 2% goal, the key to setting appropriate policy will be my view of the labor market. Remarks Feb 20 Musalem For me, policy right now is very well positioned to respond to either side of our mandate. Remarks Feb 17 Barr Based on current conditions and the data in hand, it will likely be appropriate to hold rates steady for some time. Remarks Feb 11 Schmid With the cumulative rate cuts carried out since 2024, the federal funds rate is now well off its post-pandemic high and arguably no longer restraining activity all that much, if at all. Remarks Feb 10 Logan Right now, I am more worried about inflation remaining stubbornly high. Fortunately, our policy is well-positioned to respond to risks to either of the FOMC’s dual mandate objectives. Remarks Feb 6 Jefferson
1. I am cautiously optimistic about the economic outlook. I see signs suggesting that the labor market is stabilizing, that inflation can return to a path toward our 2% objective, and that sustainable economic growth will continue.
2. I view the current policy stance as well positioned to respond to economic developments, putting the economy in a good position as we move forward. Remarks Feb 4 Cook At this time, I see risks as tilted toward higher inflation. As a result, I supported the FOMC’s decision to hold the policy rate steady at our January meeting. Remarks Jan 30 Bowman Absent a clear and sustained improvement in labor market conditions, we should be ready to adjust policy to bring it closer to neutral. Remarks Inflation Waller For us thinking about policy going forward, [the Iran War] is unlikely to cause sustained inflation. That’s one reason why we don’t look at energy prices. When we look at core, core is a better predictor of future inflation. Remarks Mar 6 Goolsbee If you’re now going to pile actually a quite large energy and commodity shock, this might be big enough to be comparable to some of the big supply shocks in the past—and a lot of the big ones ended up more persistent. Remarks Mar 5 Barkin
1. [Recent data reflect] a couple months of relatively high inflation, which certainly puts pause to any conclusion that we’re done fighting this.
2. Gas prices, if they’re up, that is inflationary... Textbook monetary policy would be you look through a short-term shock, but you don’t look through a long-term shock, and I think that’s a lot of the assessment people are going to have to make. Remarks Mar 3 Williams I expect we will see some additional pass-through of tariffs into consumer prices during the first half of this year. Given the lack of second-round effects and well-anchored inflation expectations, I expect the tariffs largely to have one-off effects on prices. Therefore, I anticipate inflation to start coming back down later this year when the peak effect of tariffs on the inflation rate is behind us. Remarks Mar 3 Schmid Inflation has been above the Fed’s objective for nearly five years now. I don’t think we have room to be complacent. History has shown us that persistent inflation can shift the psychology around price-setting, and inflation can become ingrained. Were that to occur, re-anchoring inflation at 2% would be difficult and costly. It is unlikely that we would still be talking about soft landings. Remarks Feb 24 Goolsbee Stalling out at 3% is not a safe place to be for a myriad of reasons we know all too well. We need to make more progress. Remarks Feb 17 Barr Looking ahead, it is reasonable to forecast that tariff effects on inflation will begin to abate later this year, but there are many reasons to be concerned that inflation will remain elevated. I see the risk of persistent inflation above our 2% target as significant, which means we need to remain vigilant. Remarks Feb 10 Logan
1. The upward pressure that tariff rates have been placing on goods inflation should begin to fade. Housing services inflation should continue to decelerate as market rent increases remain low amid reduced demand for rental housing relative to supply. And greater balance in the labor market should ease pressures on core non-housing services inflation.
2. But I am not yet fully confident inflation is heading all the way back to 2%. Remarks Feb 6 Jefferson Certainly, some upside risks remain, but I expect the disinflationary process to resume this year once increased tariffs pass through more fully to prices. In addition, projected strong productivity growth may be a source of further help in bringing inflation down to our 2% target. Remarks Feb 4 Cook Those readings indicate that progress on inflation essentially stalled in 2025... As I have said before but cannot repeat too many times, the FOMC’s firm commitment to its inflation mandate is imperative. After nearly five years of above-target inflation, it is essential that we maintain our credibility by returning to a disinflationary path and achieving our target in the relatively near future. Remarks Jan 30 Bowman On inflation, we have seen considerable progress in lowering the underlying trend, considering that still-elevated inflation mostly reflects tariff effects on goods prices that I expect will fade this year. Remarks Jan 30 Waller Though inflation is elevated from tariff effects, appropriate monetary policy is to “look through” these effects as long as inflation expectations are anchored, which they are. Inflation excluding tariff effects is running close to the FOMC’s 2% target and on a path to sustainably reach that goal. Remarks Jan 30 Musalem As the effects of tariffs on inflation ebb, I expect inflation to resume a path toward our 2% target. However, I also perceive a meaningful risk that inflation could remain above 2% for longer than we all would like. Remarks The Labor Market Bowman [The February jobs report] confirms to me that the labor market continues to be weak, and it could use some support from our policy rate. Remarks Mar 6 Miran Labor demand is not strong enough because monetary policy is too tight... I think the labor market could use some more support from monetary policy. Remarks Mar 6 Daly The job market is in this low-hiring, low-firing state that makes it vulnerable to any changes. Remarks Mar 6 Goolsbee If you get several months like [February], that’d be a concerning spot for the labor market. Remarks Mar 3 Williams Despite solid economic growth in 2025, we saw a gradual softening of the labor market through much of the year. In recent months, however, there have been promising signs of stabilization. Remarks Feb 17 Barr With very low levels of job creation and also a low firing rate, there seems to be a tentative balance in labor supply and demand. But it is a delicate balance, and that means that the labor market could be especially vulnerable to negative shocks. Remarks Feb 10 Logan With data flows resuming following the government shutdown in the fall, the labor market now appears to be stabilizing, and the downside risks appear to have meaningfully dissipated. Remarks Feb 6 Jefferson In this less dynamic labor market, the downside risks to employment remain, but my baseline is for the unemployment rate to hold approximately steady throughout this year. Remarks Feb 4 Cook
1. The labor market is roughly in balance, but I am highly attentive to developments, knowing it can shift quickly.
2. While the unemployment rate has shown signs of stabilization, the lack of dynamism in the labor market likely makes it susceptible to downside shocks. Remarks Feb 3 Barkin Low hiring hasn’t been translating into rising unemployment because the growth in labor supply has shrunk at about the same pace as labor demand. But slow job growth is not a comfortable place to be. Remarks Jan 30 Waller This indicates to me that there is considerable doubt about future employment growth and suggests that a substantial deterioration in the labor market is a significant risk. Remarks Jan 30 Musalem Recent data indicate the labor market is regaining some of its footing... While further cooling is possible, I believe the risk of a substantial deterioration in labor market conditions has been lessening. Remarks Restrictiveness of Policy Miran I don’t see having a modestly restrictive stance of monetary policy as opposed to a neutral stance as being appropriate. I think being close to neutral is appropriate. Remarks Mar 6 Collins My baseline features a still-uncertain inflation picture, with continued upside risks. This, combined with recent evidence suggesting a relatively stable labor market, in my view argues for maintaining policy rates at their current, mildly restrictive levels for some time. Remarks Mar 6 Hammack We are around a neutral level and we should stay at least around neutral to make sure we are putting the right amount of pressure in bringing inflation back to target. Remarks Mar 5 Barkin I expect we are still modestly restrictive... I don’t think we are highly restrictive. Remarks Feb 24 Goolsbee With inflation at 3%, it is not obvious that our interest rate policy is even restrictive. The real federal funds rate measured that way is pretty close or even a bit below the consensus view of long-run r-star. Remarks Feb 11 Schmid With demand outpacing supply and inflation running closer to 3% than 2%, I see it as appropriate to maintain a somewhat restrictive policy stance. Restrictive monetary policy can help slow demand growth, giving supply time to catch up and alleviate inflationary pressures. Remarks Feb 10 Hammack I believe we are in a good position to keep the funds rate at this level and see how things play out.
