# Fed Notes - March FOMC preview - A longer wait to see through the fog

Economics Fed Notes Date 13 March 2026

- We expect the Fed will keep rates steady, as their communications emphasize elevated uncertainty from the ongoing conflict in the Middle East. With policy “well positioned”, Chair Powell is likely to avoid any strong signals about near-term policy, most likely setting the Fed up for a third consecutive hold at the April meeting – his last leading the Fed.
- The Summary of Economic Projections (SEP) should be little changed, except for upward revisions to headline and core PCE inflation for this year. Although it will not take much to move the median dot for 2026, we expect it will most likely stay at one rate cut. In contrast, the long-run dot could edge higher.
- Recent developments have reinforced our view that Powell likely delivered the final rate cut of his tenure last December. We see balanced risks to our expectations for one rate cut this year (in September). If the labor market stabilizes, sticky inflation could push the Fed to stay on hold for longer, while a re-emergence of labor market weakness could bring about earlier reductions. A rate hike is unlikely this year (see “Could the Fed hike rates in 2026?”). Statement We expect a couple of changes to the meeting statement. First, the Committee will need to update the labor market language in the opening stanza to reflect intermeeting data developments. It is likely that the Committee will average the January and February reports and therefore state that “On average, job gains have remained low, and the unemployment rate has been stable.” Second, we expect the Committee to acknowledge the latest geopolitical developments with similar language used at the onset of the Ukraine/Russia crisis. In turn, the Committee could add a line to the second paragraph stating “The implications of recent geopolitical developments for the US economy are highly uncertain. In the near term, these events are likely to create additional upward pressure on inflation and weigh on economic activity.” As Chair Powell is likely to expound upon in his post-meeting press conference, it is too early to assess how the Iran war may impact the overall outlook beyond a mechanical mark-to-market forecast adjustment to near-term headline inflation due to the recent rise in energy prices. Matthew Luzzetti, Ph.D. Summary of Economic Projections (SEP) The Summary of Economic Projections (SEP) should be little changed, except for upward revisions to headline and core PCE inflation for this year. Although it will not take much to move the median dot for 2026, we expect it will most likely stay at one rate cut. In contrast, the long-run dot could edge higher. Given very few data points, and the aforementioned elevated uncertainty, we see little reason for Fed officials to make any adjustments to their growth and employment forecasts. However, t he recent spike in energy prices should lift headline inflation projections, though the degree of the revision is uncertain. Regarding core PCE inflation, we expect the median to edge up by a tenth due to recent trends, which point to a higher starting point for core inflatio at the beginning of the year. In addition, some officials may incorporate modest pass through of energy prices into their core inflation estimates. Regarding the policy rate projections, while we expect the 2026 range of forecasts to compress given what officials have already indicated in recent speeches, the median estimate should remain unchanged. That said, we continue to see upside risks to the long-run dot given that it remains near the very low end of the suite of r-star estimates we track. Chair Powell will most likely emphasize that significant uncertainty remains about how recent events could impact the economy and monetary policy. We suspect Powell will note that they are watching events closely and that the primary transmissi on channel is through financial markets / conditions and especially oil prices. In terms of the implications for interest rates, Powell will note that policy remains “well positioned” to respond to the consequences of these shocks. Fundamentally, the latest oil price spike represents another adverse supply shock that would, at the mar gin, create further tensions between the Fed’s dual mandates, all else equal. While markets have interpreted these developments as leaning hawkish for the Fed – an interpretation we agree with directionally – Powell is unlikely to give a strong signal abou t how near-term policy has been affected, if at all (see “Impact of oil price shocks a ‘frack’ -tion of what they once were”). As we argued recently, the Fed’s response to geopolitically-driven oil price shocks has been context-dependent historically (see “What does history tell us about the Fed’s response to oil price shocks?”). At the moment, with inflation entering its sixth year above target, we think that reinforces the Fed’s “wait-and-see” approach. In the fog of current geopolitical tensions, it would be surprising for Powell to send a signal that deviated meaningfully fr om near-term market pricing. Given the potential for a few dissents, Powell will be asked about the level of consensus around the decision to keep rates steady. He could indicate that “a significant majority” of officials supported the decision, as he emphasizes the importance of a ro bust debate about policy, particularly around an uncertain outlook buffeted by several supply shocks. Powell could also be asked about indications from the January FOMC minutes that “several” officials “would have supported a two-sided description of the C ommittee’s future interest rate decisions, reflecting the possibility that upward adjustments to the target range for the federal funds rate could be appropriate if inflation remains at above-target levels.” It would be informative to get a sense of how th is count might have changed since the January meeting, but Powell will likely continue to avoid comments about rate hikes – here he could repeat that this outcome is not part of any official’s base case. How this disagreement across the Committee gets resolved will depend on how economic fundamentals evolve. Extreme volatility in the two jobs reports since the January meeting leave questions about risks to the labor market. We think the truth is somewhere in the middle – smoothing through the last two jobs reports private and headline payrolls have averaged in the 30-50k range and ADP private sector employment has picked up to 60k, all near breakeven estimates. These figures are consistent with labor market slack broadly steady in recent months and could be viewed as consistent with some evidence of stability. However, officials will interpret these data points differently. Indeed, dovish leaning officials are likely to point to the latest data point as supporting a need to take out greater insurance against downside risks to the labor market. The (mild) economic drag from the oil price shock could add to worries about downside risks (see “US Economic Perspectives: How big of an oil shock can the Big Beautiful Bill handle? “). Powell and his colleagues are likely to feel less sanguine about inflation developments since the last meeting, even as they maintain an expectation for disinflationary pressures to re-emerge. Core PCE inflation has printed back-to-back 0.4% monthly prints, lifting the year-over-year rate to 3.1% -- its highest since early 2024 – and we are tracking a third consecutive 0.4% print for core PCE in February. The ongoing oil price shock will significantly lift headline inflation. The key will be how officials interpret any passthrough to core prices. No doubt Powell will aim to treat this as another adverse supply shock that the Fed should look through if they are able. Here he will emphasize the need to keep inflation expectations stable. But it is also worth noting that Fed staff modeling leads to different conclusions about the extent of this passthrough, with FRBUS suggesting an oil price shock can lower core inflation through the aggregate demand channel (see here), while other work focused on the 2022 energy price spike concludes it could lift core inflation for a sustained period (see here). With inflation still well above target, it makes sense for another supply shock to argue for some caution in easing policy imminently. Amongst other topics, we expect Powell to be quizzed on the potential effects of AI on the economy – a topic that dominated market attention prior to the events in the Middle East. While we expect him to emphasize the uncertainty about the impact at this point, he could also echo some of the views from his colleagues in recent speeches, including: AI-driven investment should boost real GDP growth this year, the potential for AI to lift the neutral rate, possible downside risks to the labor market from AI ov er time, and the potential for AI to impact inflationary pressures further out. He could also be asked about financial stability topics, including risks posed by sectors like private credit. We expect Powell will indicate that officials are closely watching developments in that area but provide limited additional information beyond what was discussed in the Fed’s financial stability report last November. Finally, Powell is likely to be asked about issues related to Fed personnel, including Kevin Warsh, his potential replacement. Like his colleagues, Powell is likely to welcome Warsh’s nomination. However, we expect Powell to add little on other related topics, including the ongoing DoJ investigation into his handling of the Fed building renovations, Lisa Cook’s outstanding case at the Supreme Court, and the potential that he could decide to remain on the Board as a governor after his chair term ends in mid-May.
