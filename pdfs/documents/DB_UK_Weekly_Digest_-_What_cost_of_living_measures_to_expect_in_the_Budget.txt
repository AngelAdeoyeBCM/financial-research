# UK Weekly Digest - What cost of living measures to expect in the Budget

Economics UK Weekly Digest Date 9 October 2026

As we inch closer to the Autumn Budget (28 October), one key question for markets will be around what sort of cost-of-living measures we get as energy prices soar. Indeed, a central plank of the Budget will be how the government responds to the twin challenge of rising household bills and higher costs for businesses. With CPI due to cross 4% y-o-y later this year, we expect the Autumn Budget to dampen some of the rising cost of living pressures emerging as a result of the Middle East energy shock. But make no mistake: any relief measures will likely be temporary – given the Chancellor’s dwindling fiscal buffers. In this weekly, we look at what sort of measures we could see as part of the Autumn Budget. We assess the impact on CPI/RPI and estimate the cost of potential measures likely to be on the table for the Chancellor. In short, we expect a package of around GBP ~5-6bn to support households and businesses – with more top-ups likely the longer the price shock persists. From an inflation perspectiv e, we think total measures announced since the Spring Statement will subtract nearly 0.4pp from inflation (CPI/RPI). What sort of cost-of-living measures could we see in the Autumn Budget? Measures to support households and businesses have already featured in the last several months. The previous Government announced an extension to the fuel duty freeze (at least until 31 December). Chancellor Healey didn’t waste much time either in pushing through some inflation-busting measures, including introducing a temporary VAT cut to electricity prices (from Oct -26 to Mar-27), a GBP 2 bus fare cap for 2027, and a business rates cut for pubs and venues from next April. What else can we get? Here, we set out potential measures that we think the Chancellor may be considering as he finalises his cost-of-living support package. 1/ Extending the VAT cut in electricity and gas. At present, Government policy has cut VAT to 0% on electricity bills. There are two things the Government can do come 28 October. First, extend the VAT cut to gas. And second, extend the duration of the VAT cut to include Q2-27. Why tackle VAT further? For starters, on our calculations, we estimate that the Ofgem Energy Price Cap will rise to just under GBP 2,100 in Jan-27, likely staying at those levels (or indeed push ing higher based on current market pricing) through Q2 -27. At present, a meaningful drop in energy bills won’t be coming until the summer. Cutting VAT on gas bills and extending the broad VAT cut to energy bills through to Q2-27, in our minds, seems sensible. How much will this cost? Based on our Ofgem Price Cap assumptions, and seasonal usage of gas/electricity, we estimate that both an introduction of a temporary two quarter cut to gas VAT across Q1 -27 and Q2 -27, alongside an extension of the VAT cut to electricity bills through to Q2 -27, would cost GBP 1.1bn (straddling the current and next fiscal year). The impact on CPI? The cut in VAT to gas alone would amount to 0.06%. For RPI, given the higher weight, we estimate that the impact would be slightly larger at closer to 0.11%. Beware the cliff-edge effect, however. Indeed, a reversal in October would mean that inflation effectively rises on the back of the VAT rises to both electricity and gas bills. This is one reason we wouldn’t discount a VAT holiday until the end of the 2027/28 fiscal year altogether. 2/ Extending the fuel duty freeze. The 5p cut was extended in May and is now expected to end on 31 December 2026, pushing fuel duty from GBP 52.95p to 55.95p on 1 January 2027 and to 5 7.95p on 1 March 2027, with RPI indexation resuming in April 2027. What could we see? A one-year extension to both the 5p reversal and indexation uplift. How much would this cost? For the 2026/27 year, our calculations point to a cost of GBP 0.4bn. For 2027/28, we estimate the cost of a one-year delay to amount to GBP 2.7bn. Moreover, given the lost indexation, there would be a residual cost that would also impact the fiscal headroom across the remainder of the OBR’s forecast horizon. On inflation, the inflation impact would vary by month. Using the above, Jan -27 CPI/RPI would be around 5bps lower, with Mar -27 3bps lower and Apr -27 2bps lower. 3/ Freeze in rail fares. Former Chancellor, Rachel Reeves, announced a freeze to rail fares across the country until Mar -27 (with the default rise in regulated rail fares amounting to July RPI + 1%). One low hanging fruit for Chancellor Healey would be to extend that for another year (until Mar-28). Any cost would be funded through general taxation (and existing departmental/capital budgets). So, from a net perspective, the cost to the Exchequer would be minimal. What’s the impact on inflation? The impact on infl ation would be marginal (and largely symbolic) – but would add to estimated cost savings for households. National rail fares account for about 0.4% of the CPI/RPI basket. As such, the net impact on CPI and RPI would amount to a marginal -0.02pp. 4/ Freezing prescription charges. Prescription charges in England have sat at GBP 9.90 since Apr -24 and ha ve been frozen twice. The latest Treasury documents put the value of last year’s freeze at GBP 12m – a negligible impact on the public finances. The impact on inflation would be broadly negligible too (and again more symbolic) at roughly -0.002pp. 5/ Targeted increase in Warm Homes Discount. We expect the Chancellor to opt for a GBP 1 50 increase in the Warm Homes Discount as part of a one-off winter support package (aimed to partially offset the January Ofgem Price Cap increase). This would cost the Chancellor GBP 0.9bn (spread across nearly 6m households). 6/ Removing the remainder of the Renewables Obligation. While likely to be lower down the Chancelor’s list, and while news reports have suggested there may be some doubts on implementing such a measure, we think removing the remainder of the renewables obligation (worth roughly GBP 25-30) will certainly be on the table for the Chancellor. Such a policy measure would cost roughly GBP 0.7bn in 2027/28 (and a similar amount in 2028/29) and funded via general taxation (i.e. within the existing spending envelopes). What’s the impact on inflation? For both CPI and RPI, we estimate the impact of removing the remainder of the Renewables Obligation to amount to r oughly - 5bps. Putting it together Altogether, if all six measures noted above were introduced, how much would this cost the Chancellor? For 2026/27, we estimate the total of the above (assuming a freeze in prescription charges and rail fares would come from existing budgets), would amount to additional spending of GBP 1.8bn. For 2027/28, that total we estimate would rise to GBP 4bn. In terms of inflation, if all measures listed above were implemented, this would mean a cumulative aggregate reduction in CPI of 0.35pp (and for RPI, -0.45pp). Note that the VAT cut to electricity bills has already been incorporated into the October Ofgem Price Cap. Excluding, the VAT cut to electricity bills, the impact on CPI would be closer to -0.25pp (and -0.30pp for RPI). We will make a final judgement in our Budget preview as to what policy measures are implemented. We don’t expect any broad-based package for businesses - but we do see some policies targeted at supporting key industries. Indeed, the Chancellor has already announced some measures to help businesses with a 20% business rate cut extension for pubs, clubs and live music events in Apr-27. The Chancellor has also pledged to offer some small business rates relief reform (paid for by e-commerce tax compliance and higher rates on large warehouses). What else can the Chancellor do? First, expand the business rates discount to include other industries such as restaurants – and possibly, supermarkets. The Chancellor is reported to be meeting with retail/supermarket bosses in the coming weeks (potentially costing GBP 1bn). Second, topping-up/widening the Government’s industrial electricity discount – including potentially doubling energy-intensive compensation for 2027/28 (as a bridge into the industrial competitiveness scheme). This could cost the Chancellor close to GBP 0.4bn. Third, it’s possible that the Chancellor could also consider cancelling the Apr-27 uprating of the climate change levy (which would cost a very marginal sum). And we wouldn’t rule out a temporary (Jan-Jun) capped small-firm energy discount – modelled on the prior Energy Bills Discount Scheme which knocked GBP 2p/kWh off electricity and GBP 1p/kWh off gas (implemented with a consumption cap). The latter, we estimate, would cost GBP 0.7bn. What kind of cost? Implementing all of the above could cost the Chancellor GBP 2-2.5bn across 2026/27 and 202 7/28. Relative to the above, we think, risks are skewed to a narrower and more targeted set of support measures, with the Chancellor looking at a GBP 1-2bn range. A packed MPC speaking calendar, coinciding with the IMF - World Bank Annual Meetings will also provide some further clues on MPC’s assessment of the inflation and policy outlook. GDP: We expect August GDP to have stalled after July’s stronger-than-expected 0.4% m-o-m expansion, with some course correction likely as strength in more volatile industries unwinds. This doesn’t mean the UK’s story of resilience is over, however. We still expect Q3 GDP growth of 0.5% q-o-q before momentum slows more meaningfully towards year-end as the energy shock and tighter financial conditions weigh on households and businesses. What to expect for August in particular? We see GDP coming in flat on the month (0% m-o-m). Services activity, we think, will have edged higher by 0.1% m-o-m, with both production and construction output contracting by 0.3% m-o-m. Risks are skewed marginally lower, with some payback likely in the industries that drove July’s upside surprise. Beware of revisions too: the release will incorporate the latest Blue Book changes, which will potentially reshape the monthly growth profile. Surveys: The key survey t o w atch in the week ahead will be the BRC Sales monitor. After a strong rise in H 1-26, it’s likely that retail spending continues to moderate, especially given the unfolding energy shock. BoE Speak: It’s a busy week of MPC engagements, alongside the IMF-World Bank annual meetings taking place in Bangkok. Governor Bailey and Deputy Governor Breeden’s appearances will be particularly important to watch for signals ahead of the next meeting. We will keep an eye out on their views, and whether they have changed, on second-round effects, growth and geopolitical developments.
