# United States: Inflation Markets Check your premises

We continue to recommend longs in 5y real yields and 5y5y breakevens, evolving the latter to a 0.5 beta weight. We consider 5y5y BE and ZCIS performance, takeaways from the inflation path indicated by the latest SEP, and the potential for the increase in fertilizer prices to pass through into CPI. This week, we address three topics:

1. Why the underperformance of 5y5y BEs and CPI swaps on a beta-adjusted basis may reflect a secular AI-related driver, but be a tactical opportunity. We evolve our long 5y BE recommendation into a long 0.5 beta-weighted BE.
2. Takeaways from the inflation path implied by the latest Summary of Economic Projections, emphasizing the importance of what one assumes for the CPI/PCE basis.
3. A look into the pass-through from the rise in fertilizer prices for food CPI. What if... Thinking through the beta-adjusted performance of 5y5y BEs and CPI swaps We have been monitoring the performance of the 5y5y sector with interest, given that the near-term risks from the war in Iran are clearly headline inflationary, whereas the medium-term balance with the potentially disinflationary impulse from AI is more nuanced. Moreover, the sector failed to rally meaningfully in January, or drop in February, before taking a leg lower in recent days. Indeed, outright, 5y5y inflation swaps have recently sold off to their lowest level since early May 2025, when the market was still recovering from a recession scare post- Liberation Day, but on a beta-adjusted basis, the performance is arguably more stark (Figure 1). From a tactical perspective, entering longs on a beta-adjusted basis seems compelling at first pass. FIGURE 1. 5y5y ZCIS has underperformed sharply on a beta-adjusted basis... Source: Barclays Research However, it is worth emphasizing that 5y5y has been underperforming on a beta-adjusted basis for several years now, so such an expression is arguably more tactical than fundamental. Specifically, there seems to have been a regime shift in late 2022/early 2023 (Figure 2). What exactly shifted is difficult to identify (CPI y/y had already peaked in June 2022, the SVB scare was in early 2023, the 2024 US election had not yet occurred, etc.), but we would offer another potential catalyst: AI. At a high level, it seems reasonable to think that the productivity benefits from AI could put upward pressure on forward real rates (higher returns to capital, higher productivity, higher growth, massive AI capex investment), yet simultaneously be disinflationary (a technology-induced positive supply shock). If true, this should manifest in the medium term in higher real yields and lower inflation risk (check and check). Mechanically, this results in 5y5y CPI underperforming on a beta-adjusted basis, at least using an assumed historical beta. FIGURE 2.... but the underperformance has been thematic for years Source: Barclays Research As such, if the market pricing in AI is driving the multi-year underperformance of 5y5y CPI on a beta-adjusted basis, from a fundamental perspective, it is less obvious that we should anticipate significant, sustained outperformance. By contrast, a tactical rebound is one for which we have more sympathy; as such, we evolve our recommendation for longs in 5y5y breakevens to be on a 0.5 beta weight coming out of this week's 10y TIPS auction. Unsurprisingly, performance in the 0.5 beta 5y5y BE is largely similar to the ZCIS version, with the slight complication of the January roll. We enter the position at a spread of 39bp, target a tactical rebound to 20bp, and set a largely symmetric 60bp stop. Of course, Chair Powell walked back the signaling value in the press conference, though, all else equal, this could read as dovish and support our view of long 5y real yields and long 5y5y breakevens. That said, at first pass, if one assumes a traditional 30bp basis between headline CPI and PCE, this implies pricing for something largely in line with CPI swaps for 2026 and 2027, with a very modest positive inflation risk premium in 2028 (Figure 4). Although since 2000 the basis has average 36bp, it is currently -45bp (Figure 5). However, the question is what to use on a forward basis. Using Barclays' economists projections for the basis to be +10bp in Q4 26 and 0bp in Q5 26 (US Outlook: One battle after another, March 13, 2026), rather than closer to the longer-run average United States: Inflation Markets of 30bp for 2028, we mechanically get a less bullish CPI outlook from the FOMC and one that is below market pricing for 2026 and 2027, but near market pricing for 2028. This is more intellectually consistent with our prior view that the market should be pricing in a positive inflation risk premium for the next couple of years. FIGURE 5. Urea, a nitrogen-based fertilizer, is the most vulnerable, with about 40% of all US imports coming from the Gulf. Potash, by contrast, is relatively insulated, given Canada supplies 75% of US imports, largely via non-Gulf logistics. Ammonia exposure is mostly indirect: while the US does not heavily import Gulf ammonia, the loss of Gulf volumes tightens the global nitrogen complex and lifts prices broadly. US farmers are preparing for the spring sowing season now, and fertilizer inventories matter more than ever. Articles and dealer commentary1 estimate that farmers have likely locked in about 75% of their fertilizer needs for the season at pre-war prices, leaving a 20-25% inventory gap. That raises the risk of last-minute substitution from corn to soy, as the latter has much lower fertilizer needs, and cutbacks in phosphate application, which would matter for yields if elevated prices persist into the application window. That said, it does not appear that anyone's base case is a massive shortage of crops, which would be the most obvious inflationary channel. The macro implication is farm cost inflation first, food inflation later. Changes in food prices are the outcome of shocks to farm production and costs, processing costs, end-user demand, and market structure (Tegene, 2009).2 It is well understood that agricultural commodities make up a small fraction of US food costs (USDA, 2024a) and that demand has played a larger role in explaining food inflation (Adjemian et al., 2023).3 However, shocks that constrain the availability of agricultural production and processing can have a large effect on the price that the end consumer pays for food. To try to scale the potential risk in CPI terms, a simple chart of food CPI y/y alongside North American fertilizer prices y/y (with an 8m lag) shows a pretty strong positive correlation. Running a parsimonious OLS regression, we find an intuitive and statistically significant relationship (Figure 8). A few key nuances to highlight:
- The R2 of the relationship with CPI food is quite low, at 0.16, implying that other factors influence food prices, not just fertilizer. As expected, fertilizer has better explanatory power for CPI food at home (R2 0.21) than CPI food away from home (R2 0.02). ° Yet past instances of fertilizer price shocks (2007-08; 2010-11; 2021-22) have all been followed by an acceleration in food-at-home CPI. This is because fertilizer prices tend to be pro-cyclical, ie, higher when demand is robust, and tend to respond to the same global shocks (energy/supply disruptions) that affect costs through the food value chain, including transportation and processing, which are more directly inflationary. Therefore, fertilizer prices, have, historically co-moved with other associated upstream and downstream food costs.
- The coefficient for CPI food y/y is 0.021, implying that for every 10% persistent rise in fertilizer prices, food CPI will rise 21bp. ° Consistent with the idea that the relationship is stronger for CPI food at home than CPI food away from home, the estimated pass-through is approximately six times larger (coefficients: 0.032 vs. 0.005).
- Fertilizer prices are up 16% since the end of February (and 38% year-to-date), so to scale the potential pass-through, the month-to-date rise implies a 5bp boost to headline, while the year-to-date inflation move implies 11bp.
- Barclays' baseline food CPI forecast is for a 20bp/month run-rate beginning in April. The model results, taken at face value, indicate a clear upside risk to this path, and given the c.6m lag, the impulse could be felt well into H2 26, in contrast to the airline fares pass-through, which we expect to be more concentrated/faster. All told, much like oil and energy prices, we think the persistence of the shock matters more than the peak prices themselves. The longer the Strait remains closed, the more risk of spillover effects into food transportation and processing costs, a potential diversion of corn towards biofuel production (as in 2007-08) and higher fertilizer costs, which could all compound and push grocery store prices higher.
