# India: RBI measures to push NDS rates lower

FOCUS FOCUS 03/07/2026 1

Chandresh Jain, EM Asia FX and Rates Strategist | MAR | Joey Li, FX and Rates Strategist, APAC | Parisha Saimbi, EM Asia FXLM Strategist | BNP Paribas Singapore Branch. Source: Bloomberg, Macrobond, BNP Paribas Fig. 1: Spread between 3y NDS and NDOIS has widened The Indian rupee non-deliverable forward (NDF) and swaps (NDS) market is one of the largest NDF markets in the world. It exists because the INR remains only partially convertible, in that onshore forward access requires documented underlying exposure and approval from the Reserve Bank of India (RBI). Therefore, international investors often use the NDF/NDS market to express their views on INR movements. What flows drive the NDS market? The NDS market is highly flow-driven, especially in the 3–10y part of the curve, with the main flows as follows:

- Indian corporates and banks that raise money abroad typically pay Mumbai Interbank Forward Offer Rate (MIFOR) swaps to hedge their forward USDINR risk and meet their USD needs when the bonds or loans mature. The MIFOR and NDS markets in India are highly correlated because they reflect the same underlying economic variables – the interest rate differential between India and US rates, and the perceived future value of the INR. Therefore, when the MIFOR goes higher due to these corporate hedging flows, so do NDS rates.
- Supranational agencies issuing high-quality, INR-denominated bonds offshore (INR-Supras) also impact the NDS market. These bonds allow foreign investors to hold Indian local currency interest rate risk without navigating onshore restrictions, and banks handling these issuances receive NDS to hedge currency and rates risk, pushing NDS lower.
- Interest rate expectations also drive the NDS curve. As the NDS curve deviates further from the onshore Overnight Indexed Swaps (OIS) rates rates, arbitrageurs engage in relative-value trades to exploit the valuation differences. Views on rates, FX matter too: Flows aside, the NDS curve reflects a combination of interest rate expectations and investors’ views on USDINR, including its volatility. A bearish or bullish view on INR directly dictates NDS pricing by altering offshore demand, risk premiums and hedging flows. Because the RBI tightly regulates domestic derivatives, the offshore NDS market acts as a primary barometer and driver of currency sentiment.

## KEY MESSAGES

We think the INR NDS market is driven by corporate hedging flows, INR-Supra issuance and arbitrageurs, as well as fundamental views on INR rates and sentiment around USDINR. Recent RBI measures should push the NDS rates lower, in our view, thanks to reduced corporate hedging flows, lower USDINR volatility, and potential receiving NDS flows from local banks. We believe that macro fundamentals support a received position in INR rates, and we expect the NDS curve to have the largest potential for a rally. The NDS curve has recently underperformed the NDOIS curve, which may offer an attractive opportunity to enter. We see the 3y point as the most suitable entry for this trade, as it aligns with FCNR and external commercial borrowing tenors, has a better carry profile, and is less volatile than shorter tenors. The Indian Rupee Non-Deliverable Forward (NDF) and Swap (NDS) market is one of the largest NDF markets in the world. It exists because the Indian rupee (INR) remains only partially convertible; onshore forward access requires documented underlying exposure and approval from the Reserve Bank of India (RBI). Therefore, offshore corporates, global banks, foreign hedge funds, macro traders, and others use the NDF market to hedge or speculate on INR movements. What drives the NDS market? The NDS market is highly flow-driven, especially in the 3-year to 10-year part of the curve, with the main flows as follows:

1. Indian corporates and banks that raise money abroad typically pay Mumbai Interbank Forward Offer Rate (MIFOR) swaps to hedge their forward USDINR risk and meet their USD needs when the bonds or loans mature. The MIFOR and NDS markets in India are highly correlated because they both reflect the same underlying economic variables – the interest rate differential between India and US rates, and the perceived future value of the INR. Therefore, when MIFOR go higher due to these corporate hedging flows, NDS also go higher.
2. When supranational agencies issue high-quality, INR-denominated bonds offshore (INR-Supras), it allows foreign investors to hold Indian local currency interest rate risk without navigating direct onshore restrictions. The banks handling the supra issuance on behalf of the agencies receive NDS to hedge the currency and rates risk, which pushes NDS lower.
3. Interest rate expectations also drive the NDS curve. Investors monitor the gap between NDS rates and onshore Overnight Indexed Swap (OIS) rates. When rate hike or cut expectations heavily skew one market, arbitrageurs engage in relative value trades to exploit the valuation differences.
4. The view on USDINR also impacts the NDS curve. A bearish or bullish view on the USDINR directly dictates NDS pricing by altering offshore demand, risk premiums, and speculative flows for INR. Because RBI tightly regulates domestic derivatives, the offshore NDS market acts as a primary barometer and driver of currency sentiment. When volatility in USDINR is high, asset holders (such as investors in IGBs and equities) typically increase their hedges by buying USDINR forwards, which pushes the NDS curve higher. During 2023–2024, when USDINR was trading in a tight range, the spread between the NDS and NDOIS curves (FX implied interest rate minus overnight interest rate swap) was trading close to 40bp. However, when the new RBI governor introduced policies that allowed for higher USDINR volatility, it widened to 65bp (Figure 2). RBI measures to push NDS lower: We believe the RBI’s recent measures, particularly those related to Foreign Currency Non-Resident (FCNR) rules and external commercial borrowing (ECB), will likely push the NDS curve lower (see FX: RBI, BI try different paths to fight depreciation, dated 12 June). We are currently long INR against EUR (see FX: Sell EURINR, dated 1 July). First, Indian corporates and banks will no longer need to pay NDS to hedge their FX risk when borrowing from the offshore market using ECB. Instead, they will be able to hedge their FX risk with the RBI at a discounted cost, which will not impact the market. We expect USD15– 20bn worth of ECB flows to be routed through this mechanism by the end of the year, significantly reducing paying flow on the NDS curve. Second, we expect the RBI measures to generate USD70–80bn worth of total inflows in the second half of this year. With higher headline reserves over the next 3– 5 years, the RBI will have more ammunition to intervene in the FX market if needed. This would reduce depreciation pressure on the currency, and many short INR positions will need to be unwound. As a result, we think this should bring relief to the forwards market and push the NDS curve lower, relative to the NDOIS curve. We also expect the realized volatility in USDINR to decrease, as the RBI can become active on both sides. If the USDINR exchange rate rises due to depreciation pressure, the RBI can sell dollars using the new flows to stop the move, and then buy dollars if the USDINR exchange rate falls to recoup the FX reserves lost in the last few quarters. With lower volatility, the premium in the NDS curve over the NDOIS curve should decrease, which we have observed historically during low-volatility environments. Finally, as local banks will be flush with INR liquidity once FCNR flows arrive, we think some banks may try to engage in USDINR B/S FX swaps to invest in US Treasuries, which may also push NDS lower. Macro fundamentals support a received bias in INR rates: We think the 60bp of rate hikes priced into the NDOIS curve over the next 12 months looks slightly high. The RBI did not hike rates in June, despite greater upside risks to inflation presented by higher oil prices and a depreciating INR. Now, with energy prices lower and USDINR having stabilized, we don’t expect a big hiking cycle from the RBI. Governor Sanjay Malhotra has recently pushed back against market expectations of tightening, saying that it is “premature” to discuss monetary policy tightening. We think current market pricing in the front end of the NDOIS curve can be attributed to a global shift towards rate hikes across central banks (including the Fed), and a premium for potential re-escalations in the Middle East. RBI measures to impact NDS: We believe that the recent measures announced by RBI (see FX: RBI, BI try different paths to fight depreciation, dated 12 June 2026) – particularly on the Foreign Currency Non-Resident (FCNR) and External Commercial Borrowing (ECB) fronts – will impact the NDS curve and compress the NDS to NDOIS spread in the following ways:
1. Before the RBI’s recent FX announcements, Indian corporates and banks used to borrow money from the offshore market using ECBs and paid NDS to hedge their FX risk. However, with the recent RBI measures, these borrowers will now hedge their FX risk with the RBI at a discounted cost, which will not impact the market. We expect USD15-20bn worth of ECB flows to be routed through this mechanism by the end of this year. This means that the paying flow from NDS will decrease significantly, resulting in a compression of the NDS to NDOIS spread.
2. We expect the measures announced by the RBI to generate USD70-80bn worth of inflows in total in the second half of this year. This will reduce the depreciation pressure on the currency, and many short INR speculative positions will need to be unwound. As a result, this should bring relief to the forwards market and push the NDS curve lower versus NDOIS.

Levels are attractive for NDS/NDOIS spread: Over the last couple of weeks, the NDS underperformed NDOIS, with the 3-year NDS rate increasing while the 3-year NDOIS rate has decreased (Figure 1). In our view, this has occurred due to three reasons: 1. On June 5, 2026, India announced the removal of taxes on interest income and capital gains for foreign investors on Indian government bonds (IGB). This reduced the relative attractiveness of INR- Supras, as Supras had zero tax and offered the same INR rates exposure for investors. As a result, some investors who held Supras and received NDS cut some of their positions over the last couple of weeks, pushing NDS higher. However, we believe that this flow is not sustainable, as Supras will likely rally with NDS coming lower over time and the need to sell Supras won’t be there. Additionally, Supra are still good for foreign investors who worry about onshore setup to get INR rates exposure. Additionally, the Bloomberg index committee is expected to meet mid-July to review the inclusion of IGBs in its Global Aggregate Index, which can bring up to USD15-25bn of inflows if included. NDS curve has most scope to rally: Combining the aforementioned factors with our view of lower volatility in USDINR, we think the NDS curve should have more potential to rally than NDOIS or IGBs. We prefer to initiate an outright received position on the NDS curve, rather than receiving it against a paid position in NDOIS, due to our received bias in INR rates and the negative carry associated with a paid position in NDOIS. Current levels provide an attractive entry: Over the past two weeks, INR NDS have underperformed despite the NDOIS rate falling (Figure 1). We attribute this to three main factors:
- On 5 June, India announced the removal of taxes on interest income and capital gains for foreign investors in government bonds. This reduced the relative attractiveness of INR-Supras, as Supras had zero tax and offered the same INR rates exposure for investors. Some investors who held Supras and received NDS have therefore reduced their positions over the last couple of weeks, pushing NDS higher. However, we do not see this flow as sustainable, with Supras likely to rally as NDS come lower over time and reduce the need to sell Supras. Also, Supras are still good for foreign investors who worry about an onshore setup to get INR rates exposure.
- We think some onshore corporate hedging flows have pushed NDS higher over the last two weeks, which we view as a one-off event.
- We think the front end of the NDS curve has also risen due to paying interest from foreign investors to hedge their INR FX exposure, after buying IGBs in anticipation of a rally following the FCNR announcements (Figure 4). Although sustained paying momentum in the short end of the FX implied yield curve can keep the rest of the NDS curve supported, we think there are limitations to this dynamic. The negative carry associated with fully FX-hedging a long bond position has become more pronounced following the increase in short-end implied yields. When combined with the recent rally in IGBs, we believe fully-FX-hedged long bond positions are no longer attractive due to the negative carry, and expect incoming bond flows to arrive either without FX hedging, or with reduced FX hedging. We think the 3y point is the most suitable entry point for measures. Additionally, it corresponds to the tenor for IGB inflows, has a smaller negative carry profile, and is less volatile compared to short tenors. The main risk to this trade is a re-escalation in the Middle East conflict, which could put upward pressure on INR rates. However, as our overall portfolio has a paid bias, we see this trade as a hedge to our paid positions elsewhere, which should perform well in a re-escalation scenario.
