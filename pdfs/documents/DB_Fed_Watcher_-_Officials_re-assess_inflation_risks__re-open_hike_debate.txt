# Fed Watcher - Officials re-assess inflation risks, re-open hike debate

Fed Watcher Fed Watcher Date 8 June 2026

Fed communications continued to lean modestly more hawkish on balance, with more officials prioritizing inflation concerns and/or expressing a greater willingness to contemplate further tightening if inflation fails to improve. Hawkish-leaning officials, i ncluding Cleveland Fed’s Hammack, Dallas Fed’s Logan, and KC Fed’s Schmid, have voiced growing concern that inflation may remain above target without additional policy restraint. Hammack, while supporting a near-term hold on rates, underscored that policy may not be sufficiently restrictive and cautioned that a continuation of current data trends could warrant rate actions “soon” to address inflation risks. Logan suggested that policy may be closer to neutral or even “loose” and higher rates could be needed later this year. Schmid similarly framed inflation as the primary risk and explicitly questioned whether additional tightening may be required to “tamp this thing down.” Notably, SF Fed’s Daly, typically considered a centrist on the Committee, while still viewing policy in a good place, now ranked inflation as her top priority, a view shared by a growing portion of the Committee. On the more dovish side, NY Fed’s Williams reiterated that policy is modestly restrictive, with no clear need to adjust rates in either direction in the near term. He emphasized solid growth, a stable labor market, and some continued downward pressure on underlying inflation. Governor Barr echoed this wait-and-see stance, noting that current policy is likely to remain appropriate “for some time.” Despite some officials, like Williams and Daly, seeing limited value in forward guidance at this juncture, inflation concerns have intensified in the risk assessments of most officials. Logan, in particular, highlighted short-term inflation expectations and cited staff research cautioning against misinterpreting low prints of their trimmed mean measure, as this could underestimate underlying inflation. Instead, she relies on a suite of underlying inflation metrics and argued there has been limited inflation progress, which aligns with our trend inflation dashboard (see Sideways trend for inflation, uptrend in rates?). As inflation risks rise and labor market risks recede, Fed policy may have become overinsured against downside risks to the labor market, and prospects for future rate hikes might best be interpreted as a prudent reversal of this insurance as the balanc e of risks tilts towards inflation (see Fed Notes: Overinsured?).
