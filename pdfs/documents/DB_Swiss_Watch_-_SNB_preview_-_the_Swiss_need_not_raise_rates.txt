# Swiss Watch - SNB preview - the Swiss need not raise rates

Economics Swiss Watch Date 21 September 2026

As major central banks around the world are moving through hiking cycles, OIS markets are pricing in some rate increases from the SNB next year as well. We are sceptical and find it more likely that the SNB will keep its policy rate unchanged at 0.0% through 2027. Inflation is increasing in Switzerland, too. However, while monthly readings may peak at around 11⁄4% yoy this winter, we expect annual averages of 0.7%, 0.8%, and 0.9%, for 2026, 2027, and 2028, respectively – all well within the SNB’s effective target range of 0 to 2%. This week’s policy meeting We expect the SNB to keep its policy rate unchanged at 0.0% at its upcoming quarterly meeting on 24 September. This call is in line with Bloomberg consensus and market pricing. However, the SNB may adjust the wording around the exchange rate. In June, the SNB had still noted “an increased willingness to intervene” if that were to become necessary to counter a potential “rapid and excessive appreciation of the Swiss franc, which would jeopardise price stability in Switzerland”. Over the past three months, though, the franc depreciated further, declining about 2.5% on a broad basis. Thus, the exchange rate is now less of a downside risk for inflation in Switzerland. Rather, it is now adding upward pressure on consumer prices – although probably neither the size nor the duration of this effect will be enough to raise serious concerns about inflation overshooting. We would not read a potential change in the SNB’s language on currency interventions as a signal for incoming rate hikes. The outlook beyond this week OIS markets price in roughly two 25bp rate hikes by the SNB meeting in June 2027. While uncertainty is elevated, we struggle to reconcile this market pricing with Switzerland’s current inflation outlook. We believe it is more likely that the SNB will keep rates steady at 0.0% throughout 2027. Although inflation rates should come in higher in the years 2026 -2028 than the SNB projected three months ago, we expect them to settle at a bit below 1% in the medium term, well within the central bank’s target range. In our base case, the SNB therefore does not need to raise its policy rate to keep inflation in line. We expect the Swiss consumer price index (CPI) to increase by 0.7% this year, followed by 0.8% in 2027 and 0.9% in 2028. Thus, our inflation forecasts exceed the SNB’s projections from June of 0.6%, 0.6%, and 0.7%, respectively. In the near term, monthly inflation rates should rise further from 0.8% yoy in August and may peak at around 11⁄4% this winter. Beside the weaker franc, higher energy costs are contributing decisively to this. The futures curves for wholesale oil and gas prices on world markets have shifted up substantially further compared to three months ago (see Figures 1 and 2). But helped by base effects, yoy inflation rates should then subside from March 2027 onwards. We do not expect substantial second-round effects. Figure 1: Brent oil prices and futures curves (in USD/barrel) Figure 2: TTF gas prices and futures curves (in EUR/MWh) Oil prices pass through relatively quickly to consumers and are set to add to the next few incoming monthly data points for inflation. In contrast, Swiss consumer prices react much more slowly to higher international benchmark prices for natural gas. Besides petroleum products, household electricity is the most important subcomponent of energy prices in the Swiss CPI.1 However, these electricity tariffs generally only adjust once a year in January and suppliers need to report their prices for the year ahead by the end of August. Due to this, we know already that the median electricity tariff for a household with a standard consumption pattern will actually decline a little in 2027 compared to 2026. Thus, it will take until January 2028 before household electricity prices will push up CPI inflation – and even then, the headline rate will likely remain consistent with the SNB’s price stability target. Individual price changes, including prominently for energy products, can drive movements in the overall CPI in the short term. However, in the longer term, inflation is a monetary phenomenon – and here we think the SNB is well - positioned to achieve its price-stability mandate. Despite short-term fluctuations in exchange rates, Switzerland remains one of the safest harbours. Compared to other central banks, the SNB does not need to counteract loose fiscal policies: Gross government debt is low relative to GDP and the country is running fiscal surpluses. Even the share of unfunded implicit pension liabilities is lower than in many other European countries. Separately, the SNB’s very large foreign asset holdings can to some extent act as an automatic stabiliser: When the CHF depreciates against other currencies, these holdings tend to become more valuable, thereby strengthening the backing behind the outstanding francs and making it easier for the SNB to drain excess francs from circulation if needed. Thus, inflation expectations should remain anchored within the target range, in our view, restraining potential second-round effects for inflation. The main reason why, contrary to our base case, the SNB may want to lift its policy rate next year, would be to limit the spread between its rate a nd those elsewhere. Note, while the SNB has stayed put at 0.0% so far, the European Central Bank (ECB) already raised its deposit rate from 2.0% to 2.5% this year. 1 The Swiss economy overall is also comparatively less exposed to higher natural gas import costs because hydro and nuclear make up large shares of its power generation mix. Further, our colleagues find it more likely than not that the ECB will add a final 25bp move to this hiking cycle in December. OIS markets price in further increases to around 3.25% by mid-2027. However, some gap between SNB and ECB rates seems reasonable. Inflation is significantly higher in the Eurozone (our colleagues expect consumer price increases as measured by the HICP of 3.1% for 2026 and 2.9% for 2027) than in Switzerland (recall our CPI forecasts of 0.7% and 0.8%, respectively). In addition, with its very solid public finances, Switzerland can enjoy some safe-harbour premium.
