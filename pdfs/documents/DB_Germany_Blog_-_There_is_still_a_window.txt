# Germany Blog - There is still a window

21 September 2026 Deutsche Bank Research Europe Germany Cross-Discipline Germany Blog Date Two weeks ago, the state election in Saxony-Anhalt put the spotlight on the prospect of the AfD forming the first far-right state government in Germany’s post-war history. This morning, the focus has shifted. Rather than centring on another AfD victory in Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania (MV), the debate has moved on to the ramifications of the CDU’s poor performance in MV, which Chancellor Merz described as a “disaster” last night. For the first time in Germany’s postwar history, the CDU failed to clear the 5% threshold required for representation in the state parliament. Combined with poor popularity ratings, the CDU’s recent election performances have fuelled speculation about Chancellor Merz’s political future. We continue to regard such speculation as overdone: neither is there a well-trodden path for replacing a Chancellor, nor is there an obvious or willing successor. This is not the UK. Nevertheless, a number of clients have asked what a potential change at the helm of the German government would mean for policy. In our view, the implications are often overstated. To be sure, even without a snap election, replacing the Chancellor would almost certainly delay parts of the government’s reform agenda. However, leading figures across the Conservative camp remain aligned on the need for structural reforms, and the party will keep pushing for them under any leadership. Moreover, any plausible successor from the CDU/CSU would likely remain committed to the new fiscal policy paradigm established by Merz last spring. There has been little substantive criticism within the CDU/CSU of the government’s fiscal stance. While the reform of the debt brake might have damaged the Chancellor’s standing among parts of the electorate, criticism from within the party has focused primarily on communication rather than policy. As a result, policy continuity would probably remain intact even in the event of a change in leadership. The more tangible risk to reform momentum may come from the SPD. This may seem counterintuitive given yesterday’s relatively strong performance of the SPD’s incumbent Minister-President in MV, Manuela Schwesig, who came close to defeating the AfD. Yet Schwesig campaigned as an outspoken critic of the pension reforms proposed by the federal government. Her electoral success may therefore reinforce calls within the SPD to shift further to the left. Such a move could complicate the passage of the pension reform package planned for this autumn. Indeed, SPD co-leaders Bas and Klingbeil have already signalled a demand to revisit some of the reform’s most contentious elements, without yet going into details. Our base case, however, is that the federal government will soon return to a modicum of stability and pass a meaningful reform package in the autumn, notwithstanding some tweaking around the edges. As we wrote last week, there are good reasons to think that the good economic momentum picked up this year will start to pay some political dividends by the time of the crucial state election in North Rhine-Westphalia next April.
