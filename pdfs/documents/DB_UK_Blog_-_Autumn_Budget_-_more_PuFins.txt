# UK Blog - Autumn Budget - more PuFins

Economics UK Blog Date 26 October 2026

In our Autumn Budget preview, we noted that one of the key pillars underpinning Chancellor Healey’s inaugural Budget would be investment. News reports over the weekend seem to have confirmed this and more, with Chancellor Healey likely to surprise markets with a larger investment cash injection than we assumed. What’s being reported? For context, our baseline assumption was that the Chancellor would tap into the fiscal rule flexibilities, using financial transactions via public financial institutions (the so-called PuFins), to boost investment spending via a two-stage approach: a GBP 8bn expansion in the coming Autumn Budget, followed by an equal increase in the Spending Review due next year. Headlines from the FT over the weekend, however, suggest that Chancellor Healey will do more in the Autumn Budget, adding around GBP 20bn in investment over the next five years. This would mark a substantial increase in cash borrowing relative to our baseline expectations. What are the details? The FT reported a sizeable GBP 20.4bn expansion in public financial corporation investment via the National Wealth Fund and the British Business Bank. This would be administered over the next five years, creating more than 100k jobs in key sectors such as AI, defence and green energy in ten years ’ time. Specifically, the FT noted that the National Wealth Fund would receive an extra GBP 15.4bn while the British Business Bank would get another GBP 5.4bn in investment capacity. Investments cited by the FT included GBP 2bn for a renewables-focussed Energy Wealth Fund (which would sit under the NWF). Separately, The Times reported that as part of the increase in NWF spending, defence would also benefit, with Chancellor Healey expected to “unveil billions of pounds of new defence spending” with drone manufacturers and military technology companies due to benefit. This would be a “down payment” towards the Chancellor’s goal of getting UK defence spending to 3% of GDP by 2030. When will this be spent? Investment spending will be largely backloaded. FT reporting noted that any increase in PuFins spending will come only from the 2028/29 financial year and is expected to be “raised gradually by about 2031/32”. Savings via HMRC expected to be slightly larger than we thought. Part of the investment drive is also expected to be financed by a greater effort from HMRC to close the tax gap. FT reporting suggests GBP 4bn in additional revenues from granting HMRC greater powers to recover tax debts directly from taxpayers (our baseline assumption was for GBP 2bn in additional revenues from HMRC tax avoidance programmes). Taking into account the slightly higher savings from HMRC, we project a near GBP 10bn increase per annum over the back half of the OBR’s forecast horizon, leaving the government’s gross financing requirement at just over GBP 250bn at the end of the forecast horizon (2030/31). Figure 2: Gross Financing Requirement Projections, revised post-FT headlines Source: Deutsche Bank Negative tail risk removed? The increased size of public financial institution spending may have been the big fiscal rabbit out of the Chancellor’s hat for Wednesday. Indeed, the GBP 20.4bn increase will have likely exceeded all expectations. And despite the backloaded spending profile, we think markets will still be sensitive to the higher borrowing profile over the entirety of the OBR’s forecast horizon. Put simply, m ore borrowing – despite the benefits of higher investment – will command more compensation in the current global environment. Why pump up PuFins more than expected? We see a few reasons. First, PuFins spending is ‘costless’ from a fiscal rules perspective. Indeed, investment via financial transactions won’t impact either of the Chancellor’s fiscal rules. On the Chancellor’s debt rule, financial transactions will be net-neutral, with equity injections or loans generating both an asset and a liability on the government’s balance sheet. Higher interest or equity valuation could even reduce public sector net financial liabilities – the government’s preferred debt measure. And with limited room to manoeuvre, spending via PuFins may be the only genuine channel to boost the UK’s comparatively low investment spending. Second, a ‘steady Budget’ or a ‘do no harm’ Budget was never really going to sell with the Labour Party or the electorate. The investment surprise allows the Chancellor to drive the narrative that this is ultimately a growth Budget. Higher investment will attract higher multipliers. Using the OBR’s multipliers, we estimate that projected financial transactions could result in a 0.5% boost to GDP by 2031/32. By raising defence spending too, the Chancellor can show that he’s making further strides compared to his predecessor towards meeting NATO’s 3% of GDP defence target. Third, the pre-measures headroom may be better than we think. Perhaps one offset to higher investment spending is that the public finances outlook isn’t as bad as we, or others, expect. On our projections, we see the Chancellor’s pre - measures headroom having shrunk to just over GBP 10bn. More favourable assumptions around earnings growth and nominal GDP could boost the government’s pre-measures headroom by a few billion pounds, giving the Chancellor more space to borrow to invest. That is something we will be watching closely on Budget Day. Negative tail risk now smaller. The increase in PuFins spending may have been the Chancellor’s biggest rabbit out of his hat. The Chancellor’s inaugural Budget will now be badged as a serious growth Budget, with a serious effort to address some of the Government’s key priorities, including cost of living, cost of doing business, housing, devolution, defence, and industrial investment. With investment spending now primed to be bigger than expected, one large tail risk for Budget Day has been removed with markets likely to adjust to the news ahead of the 28th of October. Where else can the Chancellor surprise? We’ll be looking closely at whether there may be a smaller rabbit left in the Chancellor’s hat on cost-of-living support. We’ve toned down our expectations as part of our baseline. But we think the next surprise could come from a more generous package come Wednesday.
