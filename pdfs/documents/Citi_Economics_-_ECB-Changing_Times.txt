# Economics ECB: Changing Times

## CITI’S TAKE

An apparent change of emphasis in ECB communication from the first pillar of its decision framework (inflation outlook) to the other two (underlying inflation dynamics and policy transmission) justifies market expectations of a more moderate path for policy rates. This change also highlights what we see as a relative instability of the ECB reaction function recently. This may change soon. We believe the President of the ECB may announce her early departure soon (Bloomberg, 30-Sep), given that it would facilitate government decisions on the quasi-simultaneous replacement of half of the Executive Board in early 2027. It does matter then to understand how candidates to her succession believe price formation to take place, as this is the key to the reaction function. We take this opportunity to explain what “hawk” and “dove” really mean in the context of monetary policy. n | ECB: Changing Times? It is customary for sell-side institutions to publish a preview note ahead of each central bank policy meeting. We do not deviate from tradition, but we do it this month in an unusual way. Rather than focusing on immediate decisions, we discuss below whether the reaction function of the European Central Bank (ECB) may change - and why - when the President leaves her position. The reason we do this now is threefold: first, we believe that the past few months illustrate that the ECB reaction function has not been stable. Second, it seems to us rational to expect that the President of the ECB might announce very soon her early departure from the ECB, which will immediately raise questions as to what that implies for the direction of policy. Third, it gives us again an opportunity to explain what a “hawk” or a “dove” really means in the context of monetary policy. 1. Three pillars, but not equally weighed We start this short discussion with the “framework” that the leadership of the ECB regularly quotes as the guide to their decisions. This is based on three pillars: nThe outlook for inflation; nThe underlying dynamics of inflation; nThe transmission of monetary policy. In practice, the outlook for inflation refers to a relatively short horizon. It includes near-term inflation developments as captured by the ECB staff’s projections, and risk thereto, described in the staff scenarios. In our reading of past policy decisions, this pillar also includes a backward-looking element, because we believe that realised inflation does affect the interpretation of this first pillar, and we explain below how we rationalise this observation. The second dimension of the framework is the underlying dynamics of inflation. We see this as encompassing all the information that allows to distinguish between endogenous and exogenous price shocks. In the current circumstances, for instance, it relates to the emergence, or lack thereof, of second round effects. That means in particular the question of whether higher energy prices will feed into higher wages. This should be the critical point of interest, as second round effects must include wages. We do not believe there can take place a price-price loop (where higher prices feed into higher prices) without there being a wage-price loop (where higher prices feed into higher wages). The third pillar – the transmission of monetary policy – has to be an assessment of the degree of accommodation or restriction generated by policy, in relation to what is required at a given moment in time. It therefore has to include a comparison of policy rates and the central bank estimate of the neutral range, but also go further. It has to include a judgment on how overall financing conditions affect demand and thereby eventually prices. At the moment, this assessment has to take account of the global uptrend in real rates, and the consequences of the idiosyncratic increase in sovereign spreads caused by a loss of confidence in French fiscal dynamics. The emphasis on each of those three pillars has not been stable over time. At the time of the September meeting of the Governing Council, for instance, we have been surprised that in her press conference, the President focused almost exclusively on the first pillar, and seemed place less weight on the other two, for instance by saying that the Governing Council is not “attaching great importance in the current circumstances to the neutral rate”. If one does not know whether policy rates are neutral or restrictive, it is not clear to us how policy transmission is assessed. More recently, the balance of weight put on the three pillars seems to have changed. In her hearing at the European Parliament on 28 September, the President emphasised the second and third pillar, underlying the lack of evidence that higher energy prices feed into wages, and the rise in long-term yields, which slow growth and reduce pass-through from an exogenous price shock. This seems to place the ECB now broadly in the position we mistakenly thought it would be in September: a situation where, with policy rates at the upper end of the neutral range, the bar for further tightening becomes higher. Our expectations then were that a minority of “dovish” Council members would be able to reduce the appetite for additional rate hikes, even in the context of a deterioration of near term inflation projections, because they would demand evidence of the emergence of second round effects to take policy into properly restrictive territory. The policy statement and press conference that followed the September meeting contradicted our expectation. Communication since seems to bring it back into the field of probable. We have accordingly not changed our baseline expectation that the Governing Council will leave policy rates unchanged this week, probably hike them by 25 bps in December, and possibly hike them again in March of next year, conditional on there being no further deterioration in the French sovereign bond market, and no global financial tensions, related for instance to confidence in AI investment. We recognise of course that, given the possible exogenous influences that we just mentioned and the two-way risk to energy prices rooted in a still unstable geopolitical situation globally, our expectations for policy rates are better described in the form of a distribution rather than a modal forecast. 2. Hawks and doves: the definitive definition The point, which we wanted to make in this note, however, is not that the outlook for rates is contingent upon the “data”. It is that this outlook is also dependent on which data one looks at, and why. We highlighted what seems to us to be a shift in emphasis between the three pillars that guide ECB decisions because in our mind it illustrates that the ECB reaction function has not been stable. This, however, may change. The ECB President has several times conveyed publicly that she would not leave her position before 2027, but may do so several months before the end of her term. It seems to us plausible to expect that the announcement of this departure might be imminent. There are several reasons why such an announcement could be in her own and governments’ interest. An early announcement may allow her to join the World Economic Forum in time for the Davos meetings in January 2027 (if it is indeed her intention to do so, as reported in the press (see Reuters 15 October). It could also allow governments to approach the replacement of the three key positions on the Executive Board of the ECB (President, chief economist, board member in charge of markets) in one single discussion. Isabel Schnabel’s decision to leave the ECB in early January adds urgency to this discussion. Other considerations may point in the same direction. If the President announces her resignation, the attention of the market will evidently turn very quickly to who will replace her. We have long thought this was an important question, for two reasons: first, because the next President likely will exert considerable influence over the course of policy; And second, because we expect that the next President may have more clearly articulated views on the reaction function of the central bank. Our first assertion is substantiated by the already mentioned fact that in the next few months, the three most important positions on the six-member Executive Board of the ECB will change hands (and even possibly four positions). Our current expectation is that the new President will be one of the two candidates most often cited in the media, i.e. Pablo Hernandez de Cos and Klaas Knot (Bloomberg 23October). Both have considerable expertise, experience and reputation. Both have been closely involved in monetary policy and other ECB policies for decades. Both know the ECB itself and the Eurosystem closely. By contrast the German and French Executive Board members, who will replace Isabel Schnabel (in January) and Philip Lane (in June) are likely to be relative newcomers. The same observation applies to some extent to the Governing Council. Few Governors (with the possible exception of Fabio Panetta) appear to combine the depth and breadth of experience that both Pablo Hernandez de Cos and Klaas Knot have accumulated over the years. Our expectation therefore is that if either candidate is appointed President, their leadership will be perhaps even more pronounced from the outset than that of any previous President of the ECB. How they understand the reaction function of the central bank then becomes relevant for markets. This takes us again to the discussion of what constitutes a “dove” or a “hawk”. Those labels are often used but rarely explained. In a recent interview (La Croix, 30 September), Christine Lagarde described hawks as individuals “who favour tightening monetary policy to fight inflation”, while doves would be those “who are keen to support economic activity with accommodative monetary policy”. Those definitions seem to imply that the doves are more willing than the hawks to compromise on the central bank’s primary objective. Our own definition is entirely different. It recognises that both hawks and doves are equally committed to the central bank mandate, but that they have different understanding of how prices are formed, and that this translates into noticeably different reaction functions. “Doves”, in our interpretation, are central bankers who believe that inflation is primarily a matter of the confrontation of supply and demand. Economists of this obedience will be very sensitive to the origin of a shock to prices. They will be very keen to respond if the shock is endogenous (a shock to demand, or a persistent shock to supply), but will show substantial patience if the shock is exogenous (such as is the case now). In their mind, because monetary policy works by steering demand, and because transmission lags can be long, it can only affect inflation at a relatively long horizon. In the three-pillar framework used by the ECB currently, they would place relatively more emphasis on the last two. “Hawks”, by contrast, are central bankers who put much more emphasis on inflation expectations in the formation of prices. If one believes that inflation expectations impact on future prices, and if one recognises (reasonably) that current inflation has a bearing on inflation expectations, then one will be much less patient in the face of a shock. Hawks are more backward-looking, more willing to consider that any deviation of realised inflation requires a policy response, because of the risk of de-anchoring of expectations that they assume. In the three-pillar framework of the ECB, they would pay more attention to the first. At this stage, we underline that neither constituency is “hawkish” or “dovish” in the traditional sense. Both are equally committed to price stability, but their horizon is different because of how much they believe the expectations channel matters in relation to other channels. Where this difference translates into the commonly accepted understanding of “hawks” and “doves” is via the aversion for error that each constituency will rationally exhibit. That is important, because as we have argued from time to time, human behaviour is not motivated solely by what we want to achieve, but also by what we most want to avoid. This is true of all of us, irrespective of our walk of life. It is certainly true of central bankers. They cannot avoid policy mistakes, and so will naturally have a bias – this is to be put to their credit – to prefer a policy mistake whose consequences they can control to one whose consequences they cannot. Since doves generally believe inflation to be a matter of supply and demand, they know they can always bring inflation down from levels above target. This can be achieved by destruction of demand, which monetary policy can always deliver. If inflation were to fall towards zero, however, the experience of the 2010s in Europe and the US, that of the US in the 1930s and that of Japan over much of the past three decades is less encouraging. It shows that the zero lower bound is binding, and that reflationary policies then become difficult, and possibly unavailable to the central bank without assistance from governments. So central bankers of this constituency may be marginally less adverse to inflation being a bit too high than a bit too low. The weight put by hawks on expectations leads to a diametrically opposed conclusion. If expectations matter, then the consequences of inflation undershooting are less worrying than those of inflation overshooting. Downward nominal rigidity means that deviation of inflation from target on the downside can be persistent but are unlikely to be very large. Indeed, during the 2010s, inflation hovered fairly close to zero in Europe but outright deflation never materialised. There is no similar stickiness of prices on the upside and so, if as in 2022 there occurs a very large exogenous shock in that direction, inflation can quickly rise far above target. If the size of deviation of inflation from target is relevant to expectations, and if expectations drive prices, then the conclusion has to be that it is less dangerous for the central bank to undershoot the target than to overshoot it. In our reading, whether any individual central banker is closer to the definition of a hawk or a dove cannot be identified solely from their inclination to hike or cut rates at a given moment in time. It is better assessed by looking at the frequency of references to the output gap or to inflation expectations in their communication and in their description of the economy. The reason why we gave this (stylised) explanation of what it really means to be a dove or a hawk is that when a new President takes up the position at the ECB, we expect the central bank reaction function to be relatively more stable than it has been recently, if the new President has a more clearly articulated view on what matters most to price formation.
