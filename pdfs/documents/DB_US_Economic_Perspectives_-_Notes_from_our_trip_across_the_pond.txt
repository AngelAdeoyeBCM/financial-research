# US Economic Perspectives - Notes from our trip across the pond

Economics US Economic Perspectives Date 18 May 2026

Last week we spent several days seeing clients in London. Over the course of the trip, we spoke with roughly three dozen clients across hedge funds, asset managers, and banks. Several key themes from these discussions were notable. Strong consensus for bearish bond bias: It was likely in part a function of the price action during the week of our trip, but there was a strong bearish bond bias across clients. As an example, at a breakfast with ~12 clients, all but one leaned bearish bonds. Most of the questions on the US front-end focused on what could get the Fed to hike, and conditional on hiking, how high rates could go. While some saw the potential for the Fed to deliver "insurance hikes" this year – i.e., earlier hikes of a smaller magnitude – most saw it lagging the data, supporting higher term premia out the curve. The bearish bias was much clearer in the US than elsewhere, with several clients noting that the US is the paid leg in cross-market trades. But low conviction / risk allocation: This point is best summarized by a survey at one multi-client meeting where everyone noted a conviction or risk allocation at 2 to 4 out of 10. Reasons for the low conviction despite the bearish consensus included: rates have already moved substantially and some sense that 5% could prove to be an important barrier at the long end; at least in the short term, any resolution in Iran would likely lead to a knee-jerk reaction of lower rates across the curve; a view that the Fed remains sensitive to downside risks in the labor market; and a still-lingering expectation that new Fed chair Warsh could prove to be more dovish. AI as a source of near-term inflation pressure: Related to the bearish bond bias and questions around Fed hikes, there was a clear focus on near-term inflationary effects of AI rather than any medium-term disinflationary or downside labor market impacts. We had extensive discussions around inflation categories where AI effects are especially visible – e.g., computer software and accessories – and the possibility that the Fed may be misattributing some AI-related core goods inflation to tariff effects that they would be inclined to look through. Elevated capex and hyperscaler issuance were also important themes. Fed sequencing in focus: With the market now pricing nontrivial risk of a rate hike by the September meeting, and the SOFR curve showing a curious hump shape over the next 12 months (see "The curious curves in SOFR"), clients were laser focused on the potential timing of any Fed hikes and the sequencing leading up to them. Consistent with our prior analysis (see "Could the Fed hike rates in 2026?"), we continue to believe that a rate hike is unlikely in the near term, as the Fed will first drop its easing bias, signal no further cuts as a baseline, and register hiking dissents before a hike is delivered. More fundamentally, the prevailing Fed narrative on inflation, i.e., that elevated inflation is mostly about supply-side one-offs and the labor market is not a source of inflationary pressures, will take time to be disproven, even if we have become more cautious about this story (see "Five Matthew Luzzetti, Ph.D. doubts about the US disinflation story"). Moreover, Warsh is likely to argue that, at least over time, AI and the ongoing productivity boom will prove disinflationary. Taken together, while we view a debate around hikes as appropriate and believe that hikes may ultimately be needed, it will tak e some time for the Fed to reach that conclusion. Fed balance sheet, QRA, and funding: There was significant interest in the outlook for the Fed's balance sheet. Unsurprisingly, Warsh's plans, particularly by what means and how quickly he could achieve a smaller balance sheet, were the main focus. There appeared to be broad agreement that by far the most likely path is first through regulatory changes that reduce bank demand for reserves. As such, the strong consensus is that Fed balance sheet reduction will occur gradually over time – i.e., over the course of years rather than quarters or months. Part of the interest around the Fed's balance sheet related to their desire to maintain benign funding market conditions. We reiterated our view that the Fed appears committed to this outcome, regardless of the pursuit of a smaller balance sheet. Relatedly, the TBAC's consideration in the latest Quarterly Refunding of investing excess TGA balances into private repo provided further confirmation that policymakers appear committed to this outcome. By and large, clients saw these developments as supportive of wider swap spreads. There was some indication that front-end swap spreads have become more crowded / consensus in recent weeks.
