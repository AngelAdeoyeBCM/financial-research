Economics Research: No EA and UK economic forecast changes on new geopolitical tensions President Trump has threatened to:
- Impose 10% additional tariffs on all exports from Denmark, Finland, France, Germany, Norway, Sweden, the Netherlands, and the UK, starting on 1 February 2026.
- Raise these tariffs to 25% on 1 June 2026.
- Keep them in place until a deal is reached for the “Complete and Total purchase of Greenland.” This move came after these countries sent troops for military training exercises in Greenland, a move defended by President Costa and President Von der Leyen in the joint statement stating: “the pre-coordinated Danish exercise, conducted with allies, responds to the need to strengthen Arctic security and poses no threat to anyone.” European leaders condemned the move by President Trump, reiterated their support for Greenland, and debated the potential response during the weekend.
- An emergency meeting of European ambassadors is scheduled to start at 5pm Brussels’ time on Sunday to coordinate a collective response to Trump’s tariffs. It is worth reminding that trade is a EU matter and that EU countries face and impose the same tariffs and non-tariffs barriers vis-a-vis third countries. Hence, imposing tariffs only on a subset of EU nations might lead to more intra-European trade to circumvent these tariffs.
- During the weekend, media1 reported that: ° President Macron called for the activation of the Anti-Coercion Instrument, which, for example, would allow the European Union to restrict access to its market to US tech giants. ° European Parliament members, including Mr. Manfred Weber, the president of the European People’s Party, suggested that the vote on the trade deal reached last summer between the US and the European Union, which sets US tariffs on imports from the EU at 15% in exchange for the bloc not applying levies on American exports, would be delayed or voted down. ° European leaders could also consider activating the counter-measures package prepared by the European Commission in 2025. This package covered a list of products amounting to approximately €93bn of US exports to the EU on which tariffs would be imposed. ° Some non-identified EU diplomats went as far as proposing the restriction of access to the US to military bases in the EU. These events add to the recent challenges to Transatlantic relationships and cloud our economic outlook. However, at this stage, we make no changes to our economic forecasts for the euro area, the EA countries we cover, or the UK for the following reasons:
- It is too early to assess whether or not the new tariffs President Trump threatened to impose will ultimately be imposed or not.
- It is too early to know whether and how the European Union and the UK will respond to them. We think that, despite attempts by the US administration to challenge the unity of the European countries, a collective response will occur, even though this might require more time as an agreement among all countries needs to be secured. As we have pointed out in our previous research, tariffs impact the economy through multiple channels: the direct trade channel, the FX channel, and the uncertainty channel. To summarise:
- Tariffs imposed on EU and UK exports to the US result in a negative demand shock for the euro area and the UK based on our analysis, with a negative effect on economic activity and inflation. While the effect via trade tends to be small when measured using standard economic models, in practice the size is hard to pin down as it can be non-linear and increase with the size of tariffs, for example.
- If the EU and the UK were to retaliate imposing tariffs on imports from the US, their external balance might improve, but the increase in import prices might weaken domestic consumption in the euro area and UK. Hence, the final impact on inflation in the euro area and the UK would be less clear.
- As we learned since the April 2025 tariff announcement, the exchange rate response might not be the standard textbook reaction of the US dollar appreciating.
- These new developments create new uncertainties and geopolitical concerns, which could lead households to increase precautionary saving, and firms to delay investment projects.
- These geopolitical tensions could be additional positive forces leading to higher defence spending and, ultimately, more European integration and a closer relationship between the EU and the UK. However, as we discussed in our report Geopolitics: EU reaction to geopolitical challenges, the EU will likely continue relying on a “step-by-step” approach, as the governance of the EU and the different political agendas at the national level remain a constraint to more ambitious pan-European solutions. In conclusion, we think recent developments pose more downside risks to the euro area and UK growth outlook, and will likely have a disinflationary impulse when all potential channels through which they would operate are considered. That said, we pause before making changes to our economic projections and central bank calls until we have more clarity on how the events of the weekend unfold. Equity Strategy - Hold your nerve Global equity markets started the year in full risk-on mode, shrugging off the tense geopolitical and policy noise coming from the US administration. But Trump threatening to apply an additional 10% tariffs rate on eight countries (Germany, France, UK, Netherlands, Norway, Sweden, Denmark, and Finland) marks a decisive escalation in tensions between the US and its NATO allies regarding the fate of Greenland. With index prices at highs and volatility at lows, the technical set-up isn’t great and EU equity markets look vulnerable to some re-pricing of risk premia. In the very near term, EU exporter (BCEUEUEX basket) and trade-related names (BCEUTRAD basket) in Autos, Luxury, Chemicals, Mining, Semis, Staples, Healthcare may be hit the most, along with high beta outperformers like Banks, which could see some profit-taking. In contrast, Defence sector (BCEUDEFN basket) and bond proxies like Utilities, Telcos and Real Estate may be seen as relative safe havens. Countries with a defensive and domestic tilt and/or not directly threatened by Trump’s tariffs like UK, Switzerland may fare better, although Spain and Italy are heavily tilted to Banks (see Banks – Higher hurdle, but upside left, 14 Jan). However, while the renewed tensions add to market uncertainty and downside risks to growth, we believe this does not automatically imply a repeat of the post-Liberation Day drawdown, which saw SXXP falling c15% and prompted a broad-based de-risking. First, a lot of EU trade losers remain depressed and under-owned (Autos and Chemicals in particular), with our tariffs basket still down c5% vs pre-Liberation day highs. Second, markets may resist over-reacting this time around, as investors have been increasingly leaning into the so-called ‘TACO trade’ when policy noise rises but concrete action remains uncertain. Third, the US political cycle also matters. With consumer affordability becoming the central theme ahead of the midterms, the scope for a major tariff escalation looks constrained, in our view. Any tariff pass-through into inflation would also complicate the Fed’s cutting path, raising the probability of incremental exemptions rather than blanket measures. Finally, the Supreme Court’s forthcoming IEEPA ruling adds another layer that may limit immediate policy implementation. So while not dismissing the tail risk of more escalation, we believe any pull-back could end up offering a good medium-term buying opportunity, and stick to our constructive central scenario (see Starting on a high – January Chart Pack, 7 Jan). This week will likely prove eventful again, with Trump expected to travel to the World Economic Forum in Davos. Big picture, we believe that an increasingly aggressive US administration raises the risk of reinforcing the slow-building ‘sell America’ bias among global allocators. The process is well under way already, as c70% of MSCI AC World AC countries outperformed the US in 2025, and so far 93% in 2026, while dollar positioning remains depressed.
