Economics Research January FOMC Recap: Some Signs of Stabilization The main message from the January FOMC meeting was that better growth news and early signs of labor market stabilization left the Committee feeling well-positioned—a term Chair Powell used five times—to remain on hold today while they assess the incoming data. The FOMC noted in its statement that the unemployment rate has shown “some signs of stabilization” and removed an earlier reference to downside risks to employment having risen. During the press conference, Powell similarly noted that data since the last meeting showed a “clear improvement in the outlook for growth” and “evidence of stabilization” in the labor market. Powell qualified this by making it clear that he does not see the evidence of stabilization as definitive just yet, which is our view as well. He echoed the language in the statement that there have been “some signs of stabilization,” but added that he “wouldn’t go too far with that” and that there are also “some signs of continued cooling,” such as the latest decline in the Conference Board’s measure of job availability. Both we and the FOMC expect the labor market to remain stable this year, with the unemployment rate moving roughly sideways in the mid-4s. This could prove wrong, in which case, Powell said, “clearly a weakening labor market calls for cutting.” But if it proves right, then the next cut will probably not come for a while. Powell described the policy stance as “loosely neutral” or “somewhat restrictive” and noted that the December dot plot showed that 15 of 19 FOMC participants anticipate additional normalization. He also reiterated his own view that inflation is running “just a bit above 2%” excluding the effects of tariffs, and he said that he expects tariff effects on inflation to eventually fade, at which point “we can loosen policy.” But it will likely take some time for the inflation data to improve enough to create a strong consensus on the FOMC to normalize further because some participants prefer to just look at the official core PCE inflation data as they are and, largely for that reason, are still quite reluctant to cut. Even in our inflation forecast, which has core PCE inflation falling to 2.1% by year-end and is more optimistic than the FOMC’s or the forecaster consensus, the year-over-year rate would remain closer to 3% than 2% until mid-year. Mainly for this reason, we have penciled in the next 25bp rate cut in June, followed by a final cut in September. This would leave the funds rate at 3-3.25%, roughly the middle of the range of FOMC participants’ estimates of the appropriate terminal rate. Powell was also asked several questions today about the court case involving Governor Lisa Cook, the Department of Justice investigation into him, and whether he intends to stay on the FOMC as a governor after his term as Chair ends, but he declined to answer them. & Co.
