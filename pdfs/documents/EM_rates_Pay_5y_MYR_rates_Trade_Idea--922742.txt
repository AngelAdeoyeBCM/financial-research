# EM rates: Pay 5y MYR rates

Chandresh Jain, EM Asia FX and Rates Strategist | MAR | Joey Li, FX and Rates Strategist, APAC | BNP Paribas Singapore Branch. We are adding a paid position in MYR rates, as we view that Malaysia's strong economic growth will continue and that markets underestimate the fiscal implications of energy prices staying high. Robust growth: We expect economic growth to remain robust despite a slight disappointment in Q1 GDP (advanced estimate: 5.3% y/y, consensus: 5.5% y/y). Barring energy prices rising on a severe re-escalation in the Middle East conflict, investment and exports will support full-year growth, in our view. We anticipate electronics exports remaining strong due to the resilience of the AI cycle, as highlighted by the region's strong export performance (Figure 2). This should keep overall growth supported, given supply-chain links across the economy. On the investment front, data center investment looks set to continue, while ongoing public infrastructure projects will also boost growth. Framework of rates during oil shock: In a higher oil price shock, one of the two things tend to happen. First, if governments allow higher prices to be passed on to the economy, markets price in more rate hikes in the front-end. We have seen this in most EM Asian markets, where front-end rates have sold off on rising oil prices and inflation risks (Figure 3).

## KEY MESSAGES

We add a paid position in 5y MYR rates, as we think Malaysia's robust economic growth will persist, fueled by resilient investment and export activity, which are poised to benefit from the ongoing AI cycle. Markets are underestimating the fiscal implications of prolonged high energy prices though, specifically the cost of fuel subsidies, we argue. MYR rates are trading below pre-Middle East conflict levels, which appears too low to us. Additionally, the yield curve is too flat relative to historical levels when growth and policy rates were similar to the current environment. With the front-end pricing in no rate hikes, we see little value in receiving it and therefore prefer to express our view of a steeper curve through an outright paid position in 5y swaps. 3.485% in 20k/01. ## KEY MESSAGES

We are adding a paid position in 5y MYR, as we expect economic growth to remain robust in 2026 due to investment and exports, which is expected to supported on the back of the AI cycle. However, we believe markets are underestimating the fiscal implications of prolonged elevated energy prices, specifically the cost of fuel subsidies. MYR rates are trading below pre-conflict levels, which screens as too low to us. Additionally, the yield curve is too flat relative to its historical levels. Alternatively, if governments opt to shield the economy from higher energy prices through subsidies, it weighs on their fiscal position, which has led to steepening pressure in countries like Thailand and India. Neither has happened in MYR rates though, with front-end swaps pricing in almost no chance of rate hikes, while the curve has flattened recently. We think the moves in MYR rates thus far may be explained from two angles. First, markets have been more inclined to pay front-end rates in countries that are large net oil importers and highly exposed to the Middle East, and Malaysia ranks relatively low in this regard. Second, Malaysia has so far maintained fixed petrol prices at RM1.99/liter for Malaysian residents during this conflict, whereas most other countries in the region have started to allow some pass-through to the consumer. However, we note a few caveats to both arguments. First, despite being an energy exporter, Malaysia remains a net oil importer and exposed to the ME (Figure 5), with a quarter of its imports coming from the region (see FX: Asia's sensitivity to supply-driven energy shocks, dated 11 March). Second, since Malaysia's BUDI95 fuel reform last December, petrol subsidies are only available for eligible Malaysians, not the entire population. Third, with energy prices expected to remain structurally elevated in the medium term, second-round effects will start to filter through to Malaysia via neighboring economies, putting upward pressure on inflation over time. In fact, we are already seeing inflation momentum picking up before the oil shock (Figure 4). Fiscal costs appears underappreciated: Although RON95 petrol subsidies may be kept in place, this comes at significant fiscal cost – fuel subsidies are estimated to cost the government about RM7bn in April, a tenfold increase from the pre-conflict monthly average of RM700mn. Most recently, Malaysia has also announced RM5bn of measures to aid MSMEs affected by the crisis. If oil prices remain close to $100 per barrel for another three months, we could see subsidy bill increasing by another RM20bn. Although a potentially larger dividend payment from Petronas due to higher energy prices may provide some offset later this year, it may not compensate fully for the increased subsidy costs.
