# UK Economics and Rates BoE: Sending a Signal

## CITI’S TAKE

The Bank of England held rates at 3.7%% in a distinctly hawkish meeting that signalled a break away from its previously dovish bias. The shift in tone is justified, but the underlying message is more nuanced that contrasts a domestically weak economy against a stag-flationary shock that leaves the future direction of policy unclear and path-dependent. We change our call for the BoE and remove our remaining two cuts for the year.

- **A break in tone** — The MPC delivered an unexpectedly hawkish message today. While the decision to hold was expected, the 9-0 vote was note and we think was a deliberate effort to move away from the idea that rates are on a downwards trajectory.
- **On standby** — The clear message was that the bank is ready to act if necessary. However, the route to doing so is path-dependent and tied to unpredictable geopolitical developments. For now, we think the committee will still favour optionality and caution while the risks unfold.
- **A change of call** — We remove our remaining two cuts for 2026 for the BoE and now see the policy rate closing the year at 3.75%. We retain our view on 3.25% at the terminal rate with the next cuts now coming in 2027. Strategy View: hiking without hiking — The BoE’s unexpectedly hawkish communication triggered a sharp market reaction, amounting to an effective 30– 40bp tightening and a dramatic repricing of end-2026 rates, even though the Committee’s underlying message suggested a preference to hold and wait for more information on the Middle East shock. While the Bank likely felt compelled to sound firm given recent inflation history, the scale of the market move looks excessive— especially in light of Governor Bailey’s later warning against drawing “strong conclusions” about imminent hikes. n | The Bank of England voted today to hold the policy rate at 3.75%, as widely expected. The tone, however, took a surprisingly hawkish turn. The vote split was 9-0, with the doves all responding to the ongoing energy supply disruption. We think this meeting marks a change in attitude for the BoE from a dovish bias towards a stance that places both higher probability (and risk from) inflation overshooting through the remainder of the year. As such, we adjust our call for the bank rate path to remove all remaining cuts 2026. The MPC’s have used the policy rate decision to send a firm message to markets. T o some extent this was a surprise: The vote split was more hawkish than we expected; some members have pivoted from pushing for cuts to discussing hikes. The intent appears to be a deliberate effort to not push back against the recent shift in market pricing. The effect has been to shift away from the narrative that the bank rate is on a downwards trajectory, at least for now. We think that the communication (and specifically the absence of any dissenters) was designed to shift the ‘median’ stance of the MPC. This is a meaningful move given that even the most dovish members are now accommodating a discussion around hikes. Importantly, there was an unambiguous message that the committee was prepared to hike if it thought it necessary to do so. The framing has shifted to one that acknowledges the possibility of inflation persistence rather than ‘just’ a transient energy shock. Potential second round effects, via inflation expectations, wages, food prices, etc., are all more concerning that the immediate jump in fuel prices from a monetary policy perspective. T o that end, the shift in rhetoric is per se important as an effort to anchor expectations and there is little incentive to push against market pricing is said pricing also signals a belief that monetary policy will respond effectively. Despite the clear shift in risk assessment, the future path for the policy rate is, in our view, less clear than market pricing would imply. The rhetoric around a hike links it to a path dependent route that sees the energy disruption as a “severe and longer-lasting constraint”. Importantly, this is just one scenario that the committee discusses as an outcome. In practice, we think caution is the most practical route for now. nFirst, the politics is leading the economics. By this, we mean that the route that some on the committee have outlined to higher rates hinges on a political assessment (with the economic impact already a second order effect) which appears impossible to quantify. A “prolonged disruption” to supply is thus less an endstate but rather a process which the committee will continually assess. This has the effect of increasingly the likelihood of policy error whereby any action taken is promptly undermined by an ‘unpredictable’ outcome. nSecond, we already see rates in the UK as restrictive to output. This view is shared by a significant portion of the committee. We consider this an important point because it the UK is left in a fundamentally different position at the outset of this shock compared to its position in 2022. Demand is weakening, unemployment is rising and output faltering. The Governor is thus right to point that the “starting point for this shock is a real economy with limited pricing power”. The character of this shock is one tension between a weak, and disinflating, domestic situation and a stagflation external shock. On the shock, as Pill notes, the concern is less the price of Brent or TTF but rather spillover into wage expectations, inflation expectations, fertiliser costs, etc. As such, we think an immediate reaction from monetary policy risks putting the cart before the horse given transmission lags. All of this is to say that while a hike is possible, it appears to be path dependent on variables that are yet unknown and difficult to predict. The nature of the shock is stagflation and until its clear whether the “stag” or the “flation” will prevail, any action may be pre-emptive. Rather, a decisive message that the committee appreciates the risk is enough for now. As such, we adjust our expectation for the Bank Rate by removing all remaining cuts this year. We think talk of a hike is premature while prices and geopolitics remains volatile, especially given the weak domestic backdrop that we describe above. However, we also agree that it is appropriate that rates should remain restrictive to weigh against the risk of de-anchoring. This call is inherently difficult. We judge the underlying position of the UK to be disinflationary and see a risk that output growth is revised lower from here, which may offset some of the transmission to CPI and force price absorption to occur through the labour market instead. In such a scenario, we think there is a case to be made that easing could continue in 2027, with cuts in Q1 and Q2 to bring policy rate back in line with our view on terminal (see: UK Economics and Rates - BoE: A monetary kite in a geopolitical hurricane). However, the argument for hikes could become equally compelling if we see signs of de-anchoring and a CPI spike feeding wage growth again. In the short term, the MPC will likely remain in an unenviable position. Risks exist in both directions and the cost of policy error appears even higher than usual. We think communication from here will remain hawkish, but beneath this will be a preference for optionality while the nature of the shock is assessed. Rates Strategy View: Hiking Without Hiking The BoE’s hawkish rhetoric delivered a 30bp (at one point 40bp+) effective rate hike today alone (to 14:15 GMT) via the market reaction. Moreover, end-2026 pricing has now increased 110bp since the conflict began such that a significant tightening in financial conditions is underway. The curve reaction was also staggering, with 30yr yields relatively stable (with 2s30s GBP swaps 26bp flatter as of 14:15GMT). The BoE had little choice but to sound hawkish into a fresh inflation shock given recent history, and jaw-boning the market is an effective strategy in times of great uncertainty. However, it might not have intended a re-pricing of quite this magnitude. That may be one price of the new individual paragraphs (which have never been tested in a shock having only been used since November). While offering greater transparency, it arguably means less control of the overall messaging as the market reacts to individual comments (in this case referencing hike possibilities). Our read of the overall tone was that it was consistent with a base case to hold for the next few meetings to await more information on the scale, duration, and second-round effects of the Middle East conflict. Governor Bailey later cautioned against “any strong conclusions” about rate hikes (see Bloomberg, 19Mar26). Against that backdrop, the near 50bp of hikes priced over the next three meetings looks an overshoot. Jamie Searle
