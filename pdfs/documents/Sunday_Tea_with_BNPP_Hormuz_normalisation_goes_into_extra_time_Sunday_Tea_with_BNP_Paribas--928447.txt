# Sunday Tea with BNPP: Hormuz normalisation goes into extra time

. 19/07/2026 1

.

## KEY MESSAGES

- With no obvious immediate catalyst to curtail renewed hostilities in the Middle East, energy prices look set to remain elevated in the near term. This may sustain the inflationary impulse, but should not derail the resilient global growth picture, in our view.
- We expect the ECB to hold rates on Thursday but see this as a pause, rather than the end of the cycle, and forecast another hike in September. Cool core US CPI in June bought the Fed some time but we continue to see a rate hike coming in December, if not earlier.
- With major US data releases done for now and markets entering a typically quieter summer trading window, we think trades based on relative value and backed by seasonality can work. Accordingly, we are long 5y on 2s5s10s butterfly. We also trailed our stop for our December 2026-March 2027 steepener. Resilience amid (renewed) uncertainty: The market appears to have largely shrugged off the re-escalation of tensions in the Middle East. Although Brent futures prices are around USD10–15/bbl higher than in early July, they remain considerably lower than the peaks reached in April. We see a few potential reasons for the relatively contained reaction thus far. First, the market could be assuming there is little incentive for either the US or Iran to return to full-scale hostilities that decisively bring to a close the talks initiated by the memorandum of understanding signed in mid-June. Second, the prevailing narrative of a post-conflict ‘supply glut’, as well as past experience of successful verbal intervention to cap prices, suggests that prices can remain rangebound unless and until physical shortages become more apparent. Third, the global economy has already proven resilient in the face of the higher energy prices and geopolitical uncertainty that greeted the start of the conflict in late-February. We broadly agree with this interpretation. Although risks have increased, w e don’t think recent events will significantly challenge the underlying resilience of the global economy, which remains supported by a combination of broadly accommodative financial conditions (at least in the US), supportive fiscal policy, and structural tailwinds from AI and higher defence spending. What’s more, although we see the cooling in US June CPI as an outlier rather than the beginning of a trend, it certainly appears to have diminished the perception that the Fed might need to act urgently. So, the Fed can maintain the hawkish rhetoric, but perhaps now has some time before it needs to back up its words with action – a July hike cannot be ruled out, but looks distinctly less likely after this print. This has limited upside yield momentum, and with no major US data releases due as markets enter the typically quieter summer trading window, we think tactically that trades focussed on relative value and backed by seasonality can perform. We entered a long 5s on the 2s5s10s UST fly on Thursday, which has worked in 13 of the last 15 years and in four of the last five (see US rates: The bellyweight Champion, dated 16 July). Central banks not out of the woods. We would caution against taking too sanguine a view on the likely broad impact of the re-escalation, however. For a start, at face value, the potential fundamental effect may be far greater than in April because
1. oil stocks have since degraded,
2. oil product prices are now indicating severe shortages, and
3. postponing LNG purchases is no longer an option with the Asian summer approaching and EU gas storage looking light. (see Gas storage woes: Molecules, spreads and arbitrage, dated 16 July). This only hardens our view that the Fed will need to hike rates at some point in the face of resilient growth, a gradually tightening labour market and persistent above-target inflation – our base case remains for a move in December. And while we expect the ECB to hold rates on Thursday, we think the fresh increase in energy prices means the argument for further tightening will now be less reliant on decisive evidence of second-round effects (see ECB preview: A pause, not a peak, dated 16 July). We continue to expect one more hike from the ECB, in September. It is unlikely to ‘pre-commit’ to this second hike at its meeting this week, but we will be looking look for any walk-back from ECB President Christine Lagarde of her recent comment (prior to the escalation) that risks had become “more balanced”. Although the market has been quick to price it, we see a higher bar for a third hike, given that this would take the policy rate above the upper bound of the ECB’s neutral rate estimate (of 1.75–2.50%) and economic growth remains muted, certainly compared to the US.. 19/07/2026 2 For EM, our refreshed reflation risk framework and supply-chain vulnerability metrics flag south and east Asia as most vulnerable to a resumption of energy supply bottlenecks. Energy-intensive economies also face the prospect of higher prices (and demand destruction) should a slower resumption of energy supply prompt fiercer competition for future energy flows (see EM: Reflation risks beyond the war, dated 3 July). The risk of high-for-longer energy prices can also add pressure to domestic inflation for those economies where output gaps are positive and underlying inflation already runs hotter than central banks are comfortable with. We see South Korea as a good example of this dynamic, and have recently raised our terminal rate view (see Bank of Korea: Higher terminal rate likely, dated 16 July). EU-China trade tensions: Media reports last week suggested the EU has set up an emergency crisis task force, partly to deal with a potential trade conflict with China (see Financial Times, 15 July). This comes against a backdrop of a continuing deterioration in the EU trade balance with China, and the notable feature that Germany is now a net importer of capital goods from China. We are hosting an expert call with Dr Valbona Zeneli of the Atlantic Council on Tuesday 22 July to discuss European policymakers’ response to t he so-called ‘China Shock 2.0’, including potential EU trade policies – you can register for that call here. Great expectations: Meanwhile, in US equities, attention remains on Q2 earnings. Bottom-up earnings expectations are lofty, with the pace of upward earnings revisions for S&P 500 names at its highest since 2021 (+10% 3m change in 1y BF EPS revisions) at a moment when broad positioning looks neutral – our US Equity Positioning indicator (BNPEPUS) is at 45/100. As such, we think an earnings-led rally will be a grind higher showing spot-up/vol-down behaviour. This was reflected last week as the large-cap banks kicked off the reporting cycle with robust results marked by headline EPS beats. Initial price action was mixed, however, with the majority underperforming their implied moves and then closing higher the day after results. The next two weeks are the most crucial of earnings season, with the majority of the S&P 500 by market cap, including six of the ‘Magnificent-7’, reporting results. For bullish trades, we prefer 1x1.5 Call Ratios, which we have been suggesting as a buy-the-dip strategy on the MAG-7 stocks. For hedges, investors could consider collaring long positions or scanning for cheap outright puts (see US Equity Flows Insights: Earnings and positioning, dated 12 July). Japanese reflation: On 21 July, the Japanese government will release the first ‘Basic Policy’ under Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi, outlining the economic framework for the next fiscal year’s budget. The draft released in late June sparked a negative reaction in the JGB market, primarily due to the omission of the perennial reference to “fiscal consolidation” and the addition of language emphasising that appropriate monetary policy management is crucial to achieving a “strong economy.” In response to rising long-term yields, press reports suggest the government will now revise the wording to acknowledge the Bank of Japan’s independence. However, the draft’s commitment to working with the BoJ to ensure “price stability without returning to deflation” still suggests a government bias towards reflation. As such, we believe the Takaichi administration’s inclination toward expansionary fiscal policy and low interest rates remains fundamentally unchanged. Beyond large-scale public-private investment plans in strategic sectors, Takaichi reaffirmed in the Diet last week her intent to implement consumption tax cuts starting next fiscal year. While we expect the government to tolerate continued rate hikes under persistent inflationary and market pressure, we maintain that the political environmen t remains unsupportive of a proactive tightening cycle by the BoJ. In turn, the BoJ is likely to stay behind the curve. Our Taylor Rule analysis suggests that the BoJ is roughly 50bp short of the appropriate level, even accepting the BoJ’s view that underlying inflation is still slightly under 2% (see BoJ: Taylor rule suggests two more rate hikes in 2026, dated 17 July). This reinforces our view that the BoJ will ultimately have to hike by more – our terminal rate expectation is 2.50%, in 2028. New PM, new cabinet: The UK is set to welcome its seventh prime minister in ten years as former Manchester Mayor Andy Burnham prepares to take the helm on Monday. With Burnham’s premiership all but rubber-stamped, market attention has turned to potential cabinet appointments, in particular speculation around who will become the next Chancellor of the Exchequer. An official appointment is not expected until Monday, but media sources have suggested it will come from the centre-right of the Labour Party, with current Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood flagged as the most likely contender. This and other recent news flow tends to support our long-held view that the new leadership is likely to deliver a “fiscally responsible shift to the left” marked by adjustments to the composition of policy rather than a renewed borrowing impulse. That said, we would expect a moderate risk premium to remain embedded in UK assets and the GBP while markets await clarity on policy, which we expect around the Labour Party conference in September and into the next fiscal event, when headline risk may also be at its greatest. For rates, this period of uncertainty may limit the scope for a meaningful rally in gilts as we head into a summer of lower net supply net of QE/QT. Fresh tensions in the Middle East drove a stark repricing in the UK front end as higher energy prices made the case for rate hikes more compelling. However, we think domestic data, rather than commodity prices, will ultimately determine whether the Bank of England hikes or opts for a prolonged hold. The coming week brings a host of releases including May labour market data (BNPP unemployment forecast: 4.9%, unchanged from April) and June inflation data (BNPP headline CPI forecast: 2.6% versus 2.8% in May), as well as the BoE’s DMP survey. We note these releases may well be noisier than usual due to an “operational issue” with the Labour Force Survey (ONS, 19 June) and the start of the Great British Summer Savings scheme in late June. We forecast a rate hold in July, but see scope for a single ‘insurance’ hike in September on the expectation second-round effects will build and become visible in domestic data later this year. Eurovision: Hungary’s parliament last week approved a constitutional amendment to remove President Tamás Sulyok, a process that Prime Minister Péter Magyar pledged to pursue after April’s election. While not yet a done deal, we think it is likely that impeachment proceedings are passed to remove Sulyok, who was heavily associated with the prior administration under Viktor Orbán. We doubt this would meaningfully impact Magyar’s reform agenda, but it would signal a robust approach to the rule-of-law issues cited as the rationale for the blocking of EU funds as policymakers seek to clear a path towards euro adoption (see Hungary: Euro vision, dated 4 June).
