# European Daily: Euro Area—Checking in on Inflation Expectations

21 May 2026 | 4:05PM BST Inflation expectations across all horizons have shifted upwards in the Euro area following the recent surge in energy prices. Median short-term inflation expectations have increased notably, while long-term expectations have moved little. Looking at the distribution of expectations, the latest Survey of Professional Forecasters (SPF) also shows that the right tail of short-term expectations has fattened notably. The distribution around long-term expectations, however, has remained anchored around 2% with a broadly unchanged distribution. Market expectations have shifted more materially towards pricing higher inflation at all horizons. Across countries, we find that short-term expectations in the ECB’s Consumer Expectations Survey (CES) have moved up throughout the Euro area, with consumers in smaller Southern countries reporting the biggest increases. Across income groups, we see that most of the move higher in expectations has been driven by lower earners who also report lower growth expectations, pointing to some stagflationary concerns. Taken together, the recent inflation expectations data point to a material rise in short-term expectations—with concerns around further upside risks—consistent with significant direct and indirect inflation effects from the energy shock. But longer-term expectations remain anchored, pointing to limited second-round inflation effects at this point. Economics Research Euro Area—Checking in on Inflation Expectations Inflation expectations across all horizons have shifted upwards in the Euro area following the escalation of the conflict in the Middle East and the subsequent surge in energy prices. This trend raises the question of additional upside pressure on inflation via the expectations de-anchoring channel. In this Daily, we update our summary measure for short- and long-term inflation expectations, document the recent shifts in expectations distributions and provide granular insights into the ECB’s Consumer Expectations Survey (CES)—a pivotal indicator of consumer inflation and broader economy perception in the region. Our “short-term” expectations series aggregates data from firms (European Commission Price Trends Survey and the ECB’s Survey on the Access to Finance of Enterprises, SAFE), professional forecasters (the ECB’s Survey of Professional Forecasters (SPF) and Consensus Economics), consumers (the ECB’s CES), and market-based indicators (1-year zero-coupon inflation swaps). Each of these series has seen a notable increase in the readings published since the beginning of March, with our summary measure rising to its highest value since late 2023 (Exhibit 1, left). Notably, median consumer expectations for inflation over the next 12 months in CES jumped to 4% in March from 2.5% in February. Conversely, long-term expectations—a key metric for assessing the persistence of inflation shocks and the risk of “second-round” effects—have remained largely anchored. While most series have ticked upwards, they continue to hover near 2.0% in recent releases. In line with that, our “long-term” summary measure has moved up only marginally and remains close to the values seen over the past two years, consistent with long-term inflation broadly at target (Exhibit 1, right). Nevertheless, median consumer expectations for inflation five years ahead in CES edged up to 2.4% in March from 2.3% in February, above most other metrics. The latest SPF results indicate that the short-term expectations distribution has become less symmetric, with the probability mass (along with the mean) shifting notably to the right: the probability of inflation being higher than 2.8% in three quarters’ time rose from 10% in 2025 to 46% in 2026Q2 (Exhibit 2, left). The distribution around long-term expectations, however, has remained anchored around 2% with broadly unchanged density, suggesting little upside risk for the long-term outlook for now. Market expectations have shifted more notably towards pricing higher inflation. The distribution of implied probability of inflation outcomes, derived from options pricing, has shifted to the right and shown a more notable widening of the right tail across the horizons, suggesting increased hedging and higher inflation risk premium across the curve (Exhibit 2, right). The ECB’s CES also provides granular country-, age- and income-level data on inflation expectations, along with consumers’ perception on income, spending, broader economic growth and their economic behaviour ahead. The geographical decomposition reveals that short-term inflation expectations rose across the board in March. Greek consumers reported the biggest incremental increase on record, with the series jumping to 10%, Italy and Portugal saw an increase to 6%, and Spain to 4.9%, while the increase in German and French expectations remained comparatively muted (Exhibit 3, left). Probabilistic bins around 1-year-ahead inflation expectations allow us to calculate the (so-called) Bowley skewness coefficient of the distribution to gauge the relative position of the median within the interquartile range. The March round reveals the highest coefficient of skewness of the CES data on record, indicating significant dispersion above the median. This reflects heightened upside inflation fears and growing fragmentation among respondents with hawkish inflation outlooks (Exhibit 3, right). Bloomberg While the rise in short-term inflation expectations is notable, it is not surprising and is driven by high sensitivity to “salient” prices (such as petrol, food or utility bills). To test for this, we estimate a simple panel model regressing inflation expectation on different horizons on the contemporaneous rate of energy inflation, controlling for country fixed effects. As expected, we find a positive and statistically significant relationship between the two for short-term expectations, but a coefficient close to zero for 3- and 5-year-ahead expectations (Exhibit 4). The income quintile split of the CES data reveals several interesting insights. Lower-income households typically exhibit a higher and more volatile perception of inflation as they are more exposed to more volatile “non-core” items prices, for example, food and energy. At the same time, lower earners also expect worse growth prospects, but higher nominal spending, together suggesting stagflationary concerns in the economy (Exhibit 5, right). While an increase in spending most likely relates to “forced” spending on more expensive necessities, liquidity constraints are likely to cause a contraction in discretionary spending, leading to a disproportionate effect on aggregate demand compared with a similar shift in the expectations of high-income quintiles. As for the ECB, the heterogeneity of inflation and growth expectations across geographies and income levels has important implications for monetary policy transmission, making its effect less predictable and potentially weaker at the aggregate level. Taken together, the recent inflation expectations data point to a material rise in short-term expectations—with concerns around further upside risks—consistent with significant direct and indirect inflation effects from the energy shock. But longer-term expectations remain anchored, pointing to limited second-round inflation effects at this point.
