# UK economic notes - BoE Recap - Crude Awakening

19 March 2026 Deutsche Bank Research Europe United Kingdom Economics UK economic notes Date As expected, the MPC left Bank Rate unchanged at 3.75%. But this was never really in doubt given the unfolding uncertainty in the Middle East. With energy prices expected to rise in the coming months, the inflation outlook has rarely been so uncertain. We take three messages from today's decision. The unfolding energy shock has unified the MPC. Indeed, today's decision came with a resounding 9-0 vote tally - the first unanimous vote since September 2021. Put differently, the size and scale of the shock was enough to coalesce the entire MPC to pause and reassess the policy outlook – underscoring the magnitude of the potential inflation shock. The Bank is officially in 'wait-and-see' mode. The MPC's message was simple: "All members stood ready to act as necessary to ensure that CPI inflation remained on track to meet the 2% target in the medium term." The Bank's easing bias was removed. Any talk of rate cuts was pushed to the back-burner. And despite increasing trade-offs between growth and inflation, the MPC has given itself enough optionality and flexibility to move policy in either direction – a big shift from February. For us, the urgency in reacting to unfolding events today was a major shift in the MPC's reaction function. Policy risks are now one-sided. We are changing our BoE call. We no longer expect any rate cuts this year. We expect Bank Rate to stay put at 3.75% for the remainder of the year. And, the prospect for rate hikes can no longer be discounted. The bar for rate hikes, we think, has fallen meaningfully – contrary to our previous view. What will it take for the MPC to hike rates? One, limited fiscal support to curb inflation. And two, duration of the Iran conflict lasting into April (and beyond), extending the length of the inflation shock. Should fears of second-round effects ratchet higher, the case for a policy pivot in Q2-26 will likely strengthen. We will be monitoring this closely. BoE Recap: Crude Awakening 19 March 2026 Page 2 Deutsche Bank AG Key takeaways on the economic outlook from the March minutes We look at the key messages on the economic outlook from the March minutes below:

- Growth: The MPC referenced the weaker growth outturns of late, with Q4- 25 GDP missing expectations at 0.1% q-o-q (below the 0.2% q-o-q projection marked in the February MPR). Bank staff continued to estimate that underlying quarterly GDP growth for Q1-26 would be around 0.1-0.2% q-o-q. What did the Agents report back for March? Growth in consumer goods and services sales volumes remained subdued so far into 2026, weighed down by weaker consumer confidence. Investment intentions were also expected to remain weak. Contacts were more optimistic around trade, citing likely improvements in both manufactured goods exports volumes and services export values.
- Labour Market: There were very few bits of new information on the labour market. The MPC acknowledged that employment growth remained subdued, while the vacancies-to-unemployment ratio had remained below the Bank staff estimate of its equilibrium rate. Bank Agents reported that employment intentions remained slightly negative this round, reflecting weak demand, higher labour costs and increasing automation (though further headcount reductions were expected to be modest). On wage growth, Bank Agents revised higher their estimate of pay deals from 3.4% to 3.6% for 2026 - highlighting another upward revision to early estimates.
- Inflation: While price momentum had outpaced the Bank's expectations to start the year, the minutes acknowledged that "higher-frequency measures of underlying services inflation had picked up a little" with February CPI expected to be a little over 3% in February. Further ahead, Bank staff noted that based on energy prices (as of 16 March), CPI inflation "was now expected to be close to 3.5% in March, almost 0.5pp higher than expected in the February Report". And in Q2-26 as a whole, the Bank estimates CPI sticking at 3%, with the direct contribution of energy prices to CPI inflation adding 0.75pp in Q3-26, taking CPI in this period to 3.5%. In perhaps some good news, Bank Agents continued to report that underlying consumer price inflation was expected to ease. While food inflation was expected to be stubbornly resilient (2-4%), Bank Agents noted that non-food price inflation would be "low in the face of subdued demand and easing input cost pressures". A more hawkish reaction function The March decision underscored the primacy of the Bank's price stability target. Reading between the lines, we see two emerging themes from today's decision. The MPC is prioritising its inflation mandate. There were no 'ifs' or 'buts' – the MPC staked its view plainly: the committee was ready to act if needed. This was a line echoed by Governor Bailey, Deputy Governor Clare Lombardelli, Deputy Governor Sarah Breeden, Deputy Governor Dave Ramsden, and Chief Economist Huw Pill. Ultimately, the key question for the MPC was whether the energy supply shock would impact medium-term inflation, with the committee noting that upside risks "appeared to have increased most notably since February". Downside risks to growth were moved to the back-burner, though the committee acknowledged the very different starting point to the energy shock, relative to 2022, including the prospect of a "more rapid or larger rise in unemployment". The focus on inflation in the minutes was notable, however. And, the MPC gave itself more optionality to pivot policy in either direction over the coming months. The next six weeks will be important in shaping the policy outlook. The MPC was at odds to stress that more will be known by the next meeting, noting that it "would have more information and analysis ahead of its next meeting". A resolution of the conflict would allow the MPC to bide their time – or even resume its path for gradual/ cautious easing. More fiscal support could also buy the MPC more space to keep rates on hold. But should the conflict intensify, and energy prices pick up further, the case for a policy pivot would undoubtedly strengthen, with the MPC explicitly stating that "the next six weeks could shed light on the likely scale and duration of the conflict, as well as providing some early evidence on the likely propagation of the shock". Policy Path: changing our BoE Call Even before the March decision, events overnight have raised the bar for rate cuts. With oil prices trading 45% higher, and gas prices up over 90% since the Iran conflict began, the risks to inflation remain firmly skewed to the upside. We think the case for near-term rate cuts has diminished significantly. Higher energy prices will push inflation expectations higher. As Bank staff have evidenced, higher energy prices tend to be more salient and persistent in shaping household expectations. With prices rising, inflation expectations will almost certainly follow. This will add to tension within the MPC, especially given the successive waves of supply shocks faced by the economy. The risk that inflation expectations de-anchor is now a rising threat to the Bank's price stability mandate. Trade-offs for the MPC will become acutely uncomfortable. Markets are already pricing in multiple rate hikes on the back of the energy crisis. We do not see rate hikes as the modal outcome – at least not yet. Governor Bailey's comments in the press pool were indicative of this, cautioning against strong conclusions about rate hikes. Two other factors also make us think rate hikes are not the modal outcome. One, we think market expectations fail to account for the downside risks to growth and the labour market. Indeed, higher energy prices will squeeze real disposable incomes, dampening spending. And increased uncertainty will almost surely cap investment plans. Crucially, we see very little wage bargaining power in the economy. In fact, weaker growth and higher unemployment will only lead to lower wage settlements (notwithstanding any productivity enhancements from AI). The MPC even noted that "policy would need to be less restrictive...if there were to be a larger opening up of slack in the economy that was expected to reduce medium-term inflationary pressures". Two, we think markets may be prematurely discounting the prospect of fiscal policy support. Indeed, the longer the crisis rages on, and the higher energy prices soar, the stronger the likelihood for government intervention. An extension of the fuel duty freeze seems likely. Further (temporary) cuts to energy bills (on the policy side) also now seem more likely. And we suspect, Treasury may even consider a reintroduction of the Energy Price Guarantee (albeit at a lower threshold). This could, in our view, reset the inflation trajectory closer to where it was pre-conflict. That said, the timeline for Chancellor Reeves to respond has been shortened, with the MPC due to take stock on next steps at its next meeting (30 April). Changing our call. We now see no rate cuts this year. Should a cease-fire 19 March 2026 Page 4 Deutsche Bank AG materialise and fiscal policy curb inflation prior to the Bank's next decision date (30 April), we expect Bank Rate to remain unchanged at 3.75% for the remainder of 2026. In this scenario, rate cuts could resume as early as Q2-27, with Bank Rate eventually landing at our long held estimate of 3.25%. What would make us change our minds on a rate hike? Simply, a larger and more protracted shock. As the MPC itself noted, such a scenario "which risked greater second-round effects in wage and price setting would require a more restrictive policy stance". As we set out last week, we see three factors that could shift our view in this direction. First, no fiscal support. Should the Chancellor opt for targeted measures to deal with the unfolding energy shock instead of broad-based support, the impact on inflation would be large, and would consequently lift inflation expectations alongside it. This, we think, would push the MPC to hike Bank Rate. Second, growth remaining broadly stable, with unemployment continuing to unwind. The January labour market came with many silver linings with employment back up and the jobless rate staying put at 5.2%. Survey data have also erred on the more positive side, highlighting some cause for optimism. And while GDP disappointed to start the year (i.e. January), we still expect some positive momentum in Q1-26. Should we see positive growth, including unfazed PMI data in the coming months, this could strengthen the case for rate hikes. Moreover, if soft data continue to point to a resurgence in hiring demand, the case for rate hikes would strengthen further. Third, a drift higher in wage settlements. We are already seeing this take place – even before the Iran conflict began. Bank Agents revised higher their estimate of pay deals to 3.6% in March. IDR data has also edged higher in recent months. A further ratcheting higher in pay deals over the coming months could bolster the MPC's confidence to increase the level of policy restrictiveness. Indeed, the Bank may not want to wait until they see 2027 pay settlements from Bank Agents (i.e. September) before dialling up the scale of restrictive policy. For us, the urgency in reacting to unfolding events today was a major shift in the MPC's reaction function. How early could the MPC lift Bank Rate? Given the rapid shift in reaction function, without fiscal support or a cease-fire in motion, if we were to see the above conditions met, the first rate hike could come as early as June/July, with rate moves taking place on a quarterly basis at first. Here, we could see a peak in Bank Rate of 4.5-4.75%. The next few weeks will be pivotal in shaping the policy outlook.
