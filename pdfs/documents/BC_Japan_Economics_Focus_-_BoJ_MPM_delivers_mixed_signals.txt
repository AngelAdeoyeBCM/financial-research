# Japan Economics Focus BoJ MPM delivers mixed signals

BoJ accelerated its hiking pace, raising rates to 1.25%. Two dissents suggest the administration remains wary of further tightening, while Gov. Ueda’s mixed signals preserved flexibility. We retain January as our baseline for the next hike; key checkpoints are the summary of opinions and Sep Tankan.

- The BoJ raised policy rates from 1.00% to 1.25% at its monetary policy meeting on 17-18 September, as widely expected. This marked a substantial acceleration from its previous pace of roughly one hike every six months to about one every three months. It was the first time in 36 and a half years, since March 1990, that the BoJ had delivered hikes at such an interval.
- As we expected, Board Members Asada and Sato voted against the decision (7-2). Both were nominated by PM Takaichi, who has been resistant to rate hikes. We note that both dissented despite calls from US Treasury Secretary Bessent for an early hike and market pricing of such a move at nearly 100%. After the terms of two hawkish board members expire in July 2027, we believe the Takaichi administration is likely to nominate new doves, strengthening the Policy Board’s bias toward maintaining the status quo.
- Forward guidance was left entirely unchanged. We had considered the possibility that the BoJ might add minimal wording, such as stating that it would adjust rates “nimbly,” to justify continuing to raise rates at the unusually short interval of around three months if conditions warranted. That said, the fact that the BoJ sharply accelerated the hiking pace under the same guidance could be interpreted as leaving scope for further hikes at the same pace depending on conditions.
- At his post-MPM press conference, Governor Ueda appeared to deliberately mix hawkish and dovish remarks to maximize future policy optionality. First, he characterized underlying inflation’s approach toward 2% as a “change in the policy phase.” Although the substance of his remarks was nothing new, they left markets with a hawkish impression and may arm the BoJ with the logic for another rate hike after a short interval. He then said that, although financial conditions remain accommodative, the degree of accommodation is gradually diminishing as rates rise, making it necessary to carefully assess the cumulative effects going forward. He also said that rapid rate hikes that could trigger major adjustments in lending or asset values should be avoided. Overall, our impression is that, even if the next hike comes sooner than the previous six-month interval, the BoJ itself does not yet have a firm view on its timing.
- As the meeting offered no concrete hints about future rate hikes, we stick to our base case of additional hikes in January and July next year to a terminal rate to 1.75%. The policy rate has already reached 1.25%, clearly exceeding the lower bound of the BoJ’s neutral-rate range for the first time. The BoJ raised policy rates from 1.00% to 1.25% at its monetary policy meeting on 17-18 September, as widely expected. Since it had also raised rates in June, this represented a marked acceleration from roughly one hike every six months to about one every three months. It was the first time in 36 and a half years, since March 1990, that the BoJ had delivered hikes at such an interval. As we expected, Board Members Asada and Sato voted against the decision (7–2). Asada had been the sole dissenter against the June rate hike. In a Reuters interview on 7 July, he said that he would need to see demand-driven inflation accompanied by wage growth before supporting another hike. Sato, by contrast, had made few public remarks, leaving the criteria for her support unclear. Both were nominated by PM Takaichi, who has been resistant rate hikes. We note that both dissented despite calls from US Treasury Secretary Bessent for an early hike and market pricing of such a move at nearly 100%. Moreover, despite Treasury Secretary Bessent’s calls for fiscal discipline, PM Takaichi has once again declared the continuation of proactive fiscal policy (Nikkei, 18 September). Accordingly, after the terms of two hawkish Policy Board members, Takata and Tamura, expire in July 2027, we believe the Takaichi administration is likely to appoint new dovish members – bringing the number of clearly dovish members to four out of nine – and strengthening the BoJ’s overall bias toward maintaining the status quo. Forward guidance was also left entirely unchanged, with the following three-part structure intact.
1. Given that underlying CPI inflation has been approaching 2 percent and financial conditions have been accommodative, the Bank will continue to raise the policy interest rate [...] in response to developments in economic activity and prices as well as financial conditions.
2. It will consider the timing and pace of adjustment, while examining the likelihood of realizing the baseline scenario of the outlook for economic activity and prices and the risks to the outlook, including the impact of the situation in the Middle East, the expansion in AI-related demand, and developments in foreign exchange rates.
3. In particular, the perspective of stabilizing underlying CPI inflation at a level around 2 percent becomes important in order to keep the risk of [...] deviating above [...] 2 percent from materializing and thereby exerting an adverse impact on the economy afterward. We had considered the possibility that the BoJ might add minimal wording, such as stating that it would adjust rates “nimbly,” to justify continuing to raise rates at the unusually short interval of around three months if conditions warranted, while also stepping up efforts to curb JPY depreciation. The statement offered neither an explanation of why the BoJ chose to hike at this meeting nor any indication of the future pace of hikes. On the other hand, the sharp acceleration in the hiking pace under the same guidance could be interpreted as leaving scope for further hikes at the same pace depending on conditions. The assessment of business fixed investment as being “on a moderate increasing trend” was unchanged, but the phrase “despite being affected by a rise in construction costs” was added. At his post-MPM press conference, Governor Ueda appeared to deliberately mix hawkish and dovish remarks to maximize future policy optionality. First, he characterized underlying inflation’s approach toward 2% as a “change in the policy phase.” Although the substance of his remarks was nothing new, they left markets with a hawkish impression and may arm the BoJ with the logic for another rate hike after a short interval. He then said that, although financial conditions remain accommodative, the degree of accommodation is gradually diminishing as rates rise, making it necessary to carefully assess the cumulative effects going forward. He also said that abrupt rate hikes that could trigger major adjustments in lending or asset values should be avoided. Overall, our impression is that, even if the next hike comes sooner than the previous six-month interval, the BoJ itself does not yet have a firm view on its timing. As this meeting provided no concrete hints about future rate hikes, we stick to our base case that, following September, the BoJ will deliver additional hikes in January and July next year to a terminal rate of 1.75%. The policy rate has reached 1.25%, clearly exceeding for the first time the lower bound of the BoJ’s estimated neutral nominal interest-rate range of 1.07%-2.53%. The median of 1.64% comes next. As noted above, Governor Ueda remarked on the need to carefully assess financial conditions. Today’s August CPI came in below expectations. With sticky services inflation also stagnating at just over 1%, the likelihood of second-round effects remains low for now, suggesting little need to rush rate hikes to curb inflation. However, if renewed JPY depreciation pushes USDJPY well above 160 again, pressure from the US side could compel the BoJ to deliver further hikes. The next checkpoints are the September meeting’s summary of opinions and the September Tankan, both due on 1 October. Governor Ueda’s tone has sometimes differed from that of the Policy Board as a whole, so this will need to be assessed in the summary of opinions. In addition, as the BoJ emphasizes “underlying inflation,” centered on inflation expectations, firms’ inflation expectations in the Tankan will also be important.
