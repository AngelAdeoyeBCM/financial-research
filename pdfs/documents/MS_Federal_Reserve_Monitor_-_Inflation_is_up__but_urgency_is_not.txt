# Federal Reserve Monitor - Inflation is up, but urgency is not

Federal Reserve Monitor | North America

September CPI surprised to the upside, reinforcing our view that inflation will remain sticky. Still, with limited signs of overheating and Fed officials signaling patience, we expect the Fed to remain on hold in October and hike again in December. Key Takeaways Supply shocks remain an important driver of inflation, with higher energy prices potentially generating second-round effects as tariff pressures fade. Signs of domestic overheating remain limited, although resilient consumption and the AI investment boom could generate pressures ahead. Many Fed officials continue to signal patience, favoring a measured recalibration rather than an aggressive tightening cycle. We expect the Fed to hold in October and hike in December and March, with risks skewed toward faster and greater tightening if inflation remains strong. M Idea 2 Inflation is up, but urgency is not September CPI inflation came in above expectations, with headline and core rising 0.6% m/m and 0.3%, respectively. On a years-on-year basis, this left headline CPI up 3.7% and core up 2.6%. In this edition of our Federal Reserve Monitor, we assess the report in the context of our broader thinking on inflation and our outlook for monetary policy. Despite the upside surprise, we believe the Fed will remain on the sidelines in October and raise its policy rate by 25bp in December and again in March for 75bp in total rate hikes. That said, risks skew in the direction of a Fed that needs to show more urgency to address above-target inflation. A multipolar world and a higher resting inflation rate At a high level, the upside surprise to September inflation is another datapoint that validates our long-held view that the US and global economy is being rewired for a multipolar world, a theme we highlighted in our 2026 Year-Ahead Outlook and 2026 Mid- Year Update. We continue to see a shift away from an era of benign globalization toward one defined by rising economic nationalism, intensified geopolitical competition, and strategic resources used as policy tools. The conflict in the Middle East has intensified competition for energy sources and delivery channels in the same way that trade and pandemic shocks reshaped the landscape in prior years. The direction of travel is toward a higher resting inflation rate, with supply shocks playing an increasing role in driving elevated inflation outcomes. Sticky inflation from multiple sources We have accounted for this in two ways in our inflation and monetary policy outlook. First, following the failure of the MOU to hold, we shifted to a permanent oil risk-premium baseline where we penciled in stronger second-round effects of higher energy prices into core inflation (see here). Our analysis indicates that second-round effects from higher energy prices could show through in airline fares, apparel, new and used vehicles, and motor vehicle equipment and supplies, among other items (see here and here). Airline fares are up substantially, rising 23% y/y, and the September CPI report included strong readings in the remaining categories. Is this a sign of newly emergent second-round effects on good prices that will offset diminishing price pressures from tariffs? It is hard to say for sure, but an important driver in our outlook for disinflation is the end of tariff pass-through. At a minimum, the evidence favors a sticky disinflation path and a slow return to the Fed’s price stability target. Second, we have assumed that monetary policy would accept a longer timeline for inflation to converge to the 2% target. We reflected this initially through our view that the Fed would remain patient during the recent oil-induced rise in inflation and, more recently after the Fed’s decision to raise rates in September, through our expectation that the Fed will seek a modest recalibration of its policy stance over a more aggressive tightening cycle. In addition, our assessment of inflation readings is complicated by elevated uncertainty about the primary drivers of inflation. The pandemic, Russia-Ukraine conflict, tariffs, and M Idea Morgan Stanley Research 3 the Middle East conflict have created clear supply-side drivers. However, the US is also in the midst of the AI spending boom that could be fueling demand-side price pressures. In our view, the US economy shows few signs of overt overheating, particularly in the labor market where employment growth remains subdued and wage growth is decelerating despite a low unemployment rate and solid productivity gains. We believe the high import content of hyperscaler capex spending is more likely to fuel positive output gaps and price pressures abroad, particularly in Asia, than at home. Nonetheless, real consumer spending is growing at a reasonably solid pace, and the consumer has held up despite a rising cost of living. The economy may not be showing signs of overheating now, but they could emerge with a lag. A measured recalibration of policy still seems likely The sources of inflation matter for the outlook for monetary policy. Traditional economic analysis says the central bank is most effective at leaning against the second-round effects of supply shocks, as opposed to reacting to changes in headline inflation. Yet if inflation is increasingly being driven by demand-side forces, then monetary policy has a clearer role. Most FOMC participants that gave their view during the inter-meeting period have said they do not see an aggressive rate hike cycle as appropriate. We point to comments by New York Fed President Williams, Governor Bowman, and San Francisco Fed President Daly as recent examples, with Daly’s comments coming after the release of the September CPI report. Each of these speakers say they expect the policy rate will be raised in the coming months, but they said there is little urgency to do that now. The risk in moving more slowly is the market increasingly views the Fed as behind the curve. Chairman Warsh refrained from offering his views at the IMF meetings in Thailand. Alternatively, the Fed could raise rates at sequential meetings, putting in a second 25bp rate hike in at its October meeting later this month. While this would signal a determination to promote price stability, the decision is not without risk. By surprising markets — which only see a 15% chance of a rate hike in October — and acting at sequential meetings, investors may price in a more aggressive path for monetary policy and tighten financial conditions by more than policymakers think appropriate, at a time when equity markets are assessing risks to the AI buildout. Neither option is risk free, of course. We believe the Fed will stick with a measured recalibration, remaining on hold in October and raising its policy rate next in December by 25bp. In the meantime, should inflation prints continue to come in hot, the risk is the Fed shifts to sequential rate hikes and raises its policy rate to a higher terminal range than we expect. A skip in October followed by a catch-up 50bp rate hike in December is low risk, in our view, given inflation trends and labor market conditions.
