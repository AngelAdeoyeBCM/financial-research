# China Economic Perspectives China vs Europe - From diagnosis to response

The PBOC issued a strongly worded statement on the RMB exchange rate The statement made the following points:

- The RMB is market-determined, with no preset target or intervention in long-term trends; policy guards against short-term overshooting, especially sharp depreciation.
- The RMB has floated in both directions over the past two decades, with repeated appreciation and depreciation cycles and greater flexibility since 2010.
- China’s trade strength reflects industrial competitiveness; it neither needs nor intends to gain trade advantages through depreciation and never pursues competitive devaluation.
- Exchange rates reflect multiple factors and have no simple linear relationship with the current account.
- Equilibrium exchange-rate assessment methods remain immature; treating individual findings as official evidence of RMB undervaluation misinterprets and misuses those findings.
- Global imbalances reflect structural factors and require collective action; blaming other countries’ exchange rates evades domestic adjustment responsibilities.
- During the 15th Five-Year Plan, China will support global rebalancing by transforming its growth model, expanding domestic demand, improving the business environment and deepening opening-up. We believe China likely sees the appreciation over the past 1.5 years as meaningful and is open to allowing further appreciation, provided it is not overly rapid. The revealed preference indicates the pace of appreciation against the dollar is capped by 7% per year. A significant acceleration in the pace of appreciation is highly unlikely, especially with EU pressures. Media report also indicated China rejected EU’s request to curb Chinese exports of EVs. China is not likely to compromise on this. The two sides also disagree on issues such as overcapacity and unfair subsidies. China’s Import Fair will start shortly. Buying more from Europe can ease tensions but with the exception of airplanes, many EU products are bought by consumers so its unclear how much the government can do on this. Last week leaders of Germany and France made a joint statement which would make it easier for the EU to move on trade policy. This was a significant move because Germany had been the more conciliatory country within the EU. View from Europe – Tipping point reached In her state of the union, European Commission President Von der Leyen highlighted that the EU’s trade deficit with China has now reached a “tipping point” at €1bn/day while warning about “dangerous dependencies.” More than 80% of many critical raw material and 90% of some rare earths come from China. French-German non-paper and industrial act Ahead of the Oct 15-16 European Council, France and Germany have a released an informal French-German non-paper urging faster and more efficient use of existing trade defence instruments with a targeted revision of anti-dumping and anti-subsidy instruments. But there was also a call for new country-agnostic instrument to react to “cases where third countries seek to undermine the restoration of a level playing fields and fair market conditions by political or economics means.” Legislative discussions also continue on the “Industrial Accelerator Act” proposed by the European Commission earlier this year to support clean technologies and European industry. Market implications We have previously argued that it is not straightforward to incorporate broader geopolitics in portfolios. But a more fragmented trading system has a clear macro implication: less efficient supply chains and, potentially, greater price pressures over time. That matters as euro-area inflation is already turning higher in the near term, even if ECB officials reminded us this week that there is little evidence so far of meaningful second-round effects. Taken together, it seems to us that these developments could point to higher level of tensions between the two economies. Trade in both directions will likely be under pressures. In theory China could make the impacts symmetric, especially when the magnitude of the policy changes is limited. In the early years of China-US trade war China’s response had a bias to be less than proportional to avoid further escalation. But this changed last year as China changed its strategy and became firmer. We would like to thank Yutong Yang for her assistance in preparing this research report.
