# Sunday Tea with BNPP: Market volatility despite macro goldilocks

. 15/02/2026 1

RKE.

## KEY MESSAGES

- The robust US January employment data is likely to alleviate Fed concerns regarding the US job market, and we believe the risks to the unemployment rate are bidirectional.
- We expect US inflation to remain sticky throughout the year, with core CPI holding at around 3% and supporting our view that a cautious Fed will keep rates unchanged in 2026.
- Goldilocks macro conditions for risk assets are further supported by the latest US data, and while residual de-risking has continued in equities, we think the risk of a broad-based equity sell-off is contained – albeit with private credit as a potential pressure point. Resilient US macro: The January US employment data exhibited broad-based strength, with a 172k gain in private payrolls, a decline in the unemployment rate to 4.3%, and above-consensus average hourly earnings growth of 0.4% m/m. This robust data is likely to alleviate Fed concerns regarding the US job market and may influence the views of Fed officials for whom such doubts were underpinning potential support for near-term cuts. As discussed in our 10 February piece US: Sharp immigration decline to weigh on population growth, we have revised down our breakeven rate for employment data to 0 -20k/month from 50k previously driven by sharp declines in immigration flows and slow population growth. Consequently, lower hiring rates can maintain low unemployment rates, as limited labour sup ply balances out weak hiring demand. Therefore, we believe the risks to the unemployment rate are bidirectional, given the stimulative fiscal and monetary policy mix, declining trade policy uncertainties, and structurally lower labour supply in the economy. US January CPI on Friday was softer than expected at 2.4% y/y with the headline print helped by softer used car and shelter prices. Core CPI, on the other hand, was in line with expectations with non-shelter services remaining especially hot. Following the data, we reiterate our view that inflation will remain sticky throughout the year with core CPI holding at around 3%, with two sided – but upwardly skewed risks at least in the near term (see US Inflation Outlook 2026: Sticky, not solved, dated 9 February). Overall, the backdrop from the labour market and inflation supports our view for a cautious Fed keeping rates unchanged throughout the year. Pressure points from US data strength: Despite the broad-based data strength, the reaction in rates was limited, with risk-off sentiment weighing on the front-end. The biggest move was the 6m point, and the market is reluctant to price out the 50bp of cuts anticipated for the second half of the year, reflecting expectations of a Fed with a one-sided reaction function to data. The limited move in front-end rates has kept a lid on FX impact. Still, we expect the rates-sensitive complex to be the main pressure point in FX, while high-beta currencies continue to benefit from cyclical tailwinds of strong data and higher terms of trade. Hence, we maintain our short EURBRL position given its attractive carry to vol ratio and the lack of election-related volatility in the short term. The yen remains the most vulnerable part of the rates-sensitive Asia FX complex. Expectations of a budget-neutral consumption tax cut and a faster rate hike cycle from the Bank of Japan have contributed to some reduction in JPY shorts, as highlighted by our proprietary positioning indicators. However, even with our revised path (2% terminal with a pace of hikes every 4 –5 months), we think the BoJ will remain behind the curve, and we prefer to use such positioning-driven retracements as opportunities to buy the dips in USDJPY. We remain long USDJPY via 157 calls to express this view, with an EKO at 161, as the market may become concerned about official FX intervention risks at these levels, which could limit the chance of a topside breakout in USDJPY (see FX: Positioning for a grind higher in USDJPY, dated 9 February). On the rates side, we think the aggressive bear-steepening in the curve is behind us, as the BoJ turns more sensitive to further sell-offs in the back end, so we rather prefer paying 10y. The Chinese New Year holiday period kick s in this week, meaning seasonality is turning bullish for USD pairs in the region (see our 29 January Seasonality roadmap 2026 update). This seasonal bid for USD can be amplified by positive carry from USD longs, as local markets remain mostly closed, and the KRW is the main currency in the region where our bias is to fade any such USD bid. We think the Korean government will actively support local assets via measures such as
1. temporary capital gains tax relief,
2. promoting FX forward products for retail,
3. changing National Pension Service allocation and rebalancing rules, and
4. more active FX hedging. However, residual de-risking continued in equities, and cross-sectoral volatility remained elevated, with the main weakness coming from the software sector. We think the contagion risk to trigger a broad-based equity sell-off is contained, not only due to strong macro data but also due to a continued decline in positioning. Our own positioning metric (BNPEPUS Index <GO>) has dropped to around the 20th percentile – one of the lowest levels in the last 12 months, which does not suggest an extremely fragile technical backdrop. In our recent piece analysing possible tail risks for markets in 2026, we highlighted that private credit is possibly one route for contagion, given that t he asset class is quite exposed to the software sector, which is being undermined by disruption from AI evolution (see Tail risks 2026: BNP Paribas grey-swan analysis, dated 12 February) US equity positioning score down to 20th percentile Growth in private credit recently outpaced public leveraged finance Sources: Bloomberg, Macrobond, BNP Paribas Sources: Preqin (left-hand chart), Bloomberg (right-hand chart), BNP Paribas Political risks back on stage: In the UK, political risk premium remains high after spiking at the start of the week on fears Prime Minister Kier Starmer could resign. While he managed to secure the backing of his Cabinet – even from a number of potential contenders – and received support from his MPs, we see potential catalysts that could reignite pressure, starting with the 26 February by-election in Gorton and Denton. As such, we do not expect major retracements in fiscal risk premium anytime soon and therefore stay long EURGBP as the risk of a change in leadership and fiscal policy may persist until after the May local elections. In UK politics: Succession scenarios, published 13 February, we examine three potential scenarios for fiscal policy alongside their implications for the markets. We conclude that, while a lot of bearishness is already in the price, UK assets are still at ri sk of underperforming further until clarity on new policies is unveiled at the Autumn budget. And with the Bank of England’s hands tied until clarity is provided, we see the odds of another cut delivered by the September MPC meeting as overdone – and therefore now like to pay that meeting. Besides, the UK CPI is out on Wednesday, and we see room for BoE disappointment. Indeed, while we expect headline CPI to drop to 3%, we see services lower but still firm at 4.4%, versus the BoE’s own 4.1% forecast. Elsewhere, there is a growing sense of urgency among European leaders to cut bureaucracy and deliver reforms that boost the bloc’s competitiveness. That was the tone coming out of last week’s EU leaders’ retreat, which should be followed by a roadmap and an action plan ahead of the formal meeting in March.. 15/02/2026 3 Another crucial piece of EU economic integration that grabbed attention last week is common issuance and the creation of an E U safe asset. We have analysed these topics extensively in Safety in Numbers: Common EU Issuance for a Joint Future, dated 15 October 2025. In a nutshell, we think more progress on common issuance is likely by 2027 – defence spending appears the most likely candidate – whereas the creation of an EU safe asset will probably take longer because it will require a larger political and policy effort. History shows that progress on EU integration is rarely linear and often needs a negative shock to generate the political capital necessary for action. Nonetheless, we believe that last year’s policy shift in Germany, together with the renewed impetus to i nvest in defence and to boost EU growth, has created enough political capital to move the agenda forward. While this shift will take time to fully show up in the economic data, we think that this week’s PMI s should provide further evidence of economic momentum in Q1 – we look for a slightly above-consensus pickup in the composite PMI from 51.3 in January. Furthermore, ahead of the Hungarian parliamentary election on 12 April, we have outlined the binary risks, policy outlooks and possible market reactions into and after the elections (see Hungary election: Binary outcomes, minimal tail-risk priced, dated 13 February). Lastly, SCOTUS returns from their winter recess on Friday, 20 February. There is no guarantee of an IEEPA decision but it remains a possibility and thus another risk for markets to consider. We hosted a call earlier in the month to discuss the impact of the ruling, with the replay available HERE in case you missed it. Remain bullish EM, adding some payers for improving growth prospects: Last week, we added some payers in EM where growth prospects are improving. The recent trade agreements between India and Europe, as well as India and the US, have mitigated the downside risk to India’s growth prospects. With high-frequency economic indicators, such as credit growth, automobile sales, and industrial production, already showing signs of improvement, we are optimistic about India’s economic outlook. Meanwhile, in Thailand, we anticipate a stable government will emerge after the recent elections, which should lead to a resurgence in fiscal spending. Given that bond supply far exceeds demand in both countries, we are paid in INR NDOIS 5y and THOR 5y. In Latam, Chile appears to show a similar backdrop with strong growth tailwinds following elections (see Chile: Stronger growth outlook, BCCh at a crossroads, dated 27 January). We are waiting for the BCCh to close the door on further cuts before engaging in payers in the belly of the curve (2y-5y).
