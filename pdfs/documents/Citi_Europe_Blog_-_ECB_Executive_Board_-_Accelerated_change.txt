# Europe Blog - ECB Executive Board - Accelerated change

Economics Europe Blog Date 30 September 2026

ECB succession management is starting to accelerate. Isabel Schnabel has announced her intention to leave the ECB Executive Board on 3 January 2027 and take a senior position at the IMF. By leaving almost a year before her term was due to end, Schnabel’s move triggers a political process to select her successor. It also lowers the hurdle for President Christine Lagarde to depart before her term ends on 31 October 2027. Schnabel’s resignation creates an opportunity for EU leaders to negotiate the ECB’s three upcoming Executive Board vacancies — president, chief economist, and head of markets – as a package. A package deal that solves all three positions at once is arguably politically easier than a sequence of separate decisions over the next year. Lagarde’s increasingly non-committal language and potential interest in playing a role in the French presidential campaign add to the case for an earlier exit. In this note we argue that Lagarde would need to announce her exit soo n. We also argue that exit won’t necessarily disrupt ECB policy making. The institutions and processes imply continuity, not disruption. Why an early departure has become more likely President Lagarde’s language on an early departure has shifted from saying she expects to finish her term (20 February 2026) to more recently saying that she will depart the ECB “in 2027”. When pressed on whether that means at the end of her term in October 2027, she only said “we’ll see”. The forthcoming French presidential election is a potential catalyst for Lagarde’s early departure. In July, Lagarde told the French newspaper Les Échos that it was “certainly possible” she ends her tenure at the ECB early to play a role in the upcoming French presidential elections. She has ruled out standing as a candidate but did not rule out being involved with campaigning and advocating for pro-European values “in whichever capacity” she deemed most efficient. The first round of the French presidential election is on 18 April 2027. The candidates need to be formalised by 12 March 2027, with official candidate debates likely to follow soon after. If Lagarde wants a meaningful role in the campaign – even if not as a candidate – she would probably need to leave the ECB by early 2027. Furthermore, for the European Council (heads of state and government) to have sufficient time to have Lagarde’s successor in place by early 2027, the process would need to begin soon. Schnabel’s exit opens the way for a package deal As with many senior EU appointments, the composition of the ECB’s Executive Board reflects a compromise among countries and political groups. Lagarde’s appointment as ECB president in 2019 formed part of a broader bargain between France and Germany: Ursula von der Leyen, a German national, became European Commission president, while Christine Lagarde, a French national, became ECB president. There are three Executive Board seats to allocate next year: Schnabel’s (now in January, almost a year ear lier than expected), Lane’s (May) and Lagarde’s (October). France and Germany have always held an Executive Board seat (Figure 1), and there is an informal agreement that no country should hold more than one Executive Board seat. A sequential process would therefore be difficult: France and Germany could be reluctant to accept one of the other vacancies before the presidency is settled, because doing so would effectively surrender their option to nominate a candidate for president later in the process. Schnabel’s early departure creates an opportunity to bring the succession decisions together. If Lagarde also left early, France and Germany could negotiate the ECB presidency alongside the other vacant Executive Board seat (Lane’s chief economist position) rather than decide each appointment sequentially. A package agreement would resemble the EU’s previous grand bargains and may be more politically feasible than asking either country to accept another Board seat before the presidency is settled. We have based our timing of those off the historical precedence Timeline for an early departure and successor decision According to the EU Treaty, members of the ECB Executive Board are appointed by the European Council, acting by qualified majority. The candidate must be of “recognised standing” and have “professional experience in monetary or banking matters”. The candidate is appointed after the European Parliament and ECB Governing Council have been “consulted”. In other words, they get to comment but they do not have veto powers. Figure 2 sets out a hypothetical timeline in which Lagarde announces at the ECB’s October meeting (29 Oct) that she intends to step down early. This hypothetical timeline assumes decisions in the replacement process are made at the next available scheduled meeting of various groups. In the past, votes and decisions about ECB executive positions have been made at regular meetings rather than scheduling special meetings. The timeline suggests that at a normal pace, the next ECB president could be in place by the end of March. Figure 2: Timeline for a hypothetical decision by Lagarde to announce at the October meeting her early resignation Source: Deutsche Bank Research The ECB Governing Council meets to set monetary policy on 17 -18 March, meaning the timeline above creates uncertainty about whether the next ECB President would be in place for the March meeting or not. If Lagarde remained in office until the March ECB Governing Council meeting, she would have less than one month before the first round of the presidential vote in April 2027 — a short amount of time to play a role in the campaign. Therefore, to play a more significant role in the French presidential campaign, Lagarde would most likely need to leave before the ECB’s 17–18 March meeting. An earlier departure could be achieved in two ways:

- Lagarde announces her exit before the October ECB meeting – Schnabel’s early departure was announced with a press release from the ECB, not at an ECB policy meeting. Lagarde could follow the same route and make an announcement before the October meeting. That would start the process earlier, allowing for an earlier conclusion. In addition, the timeline for selecting Schnabel’s replacement has not yet been announced. If Lagarde was to announce her departure in the next few days or weeks, it may be possible to combine the process for both positions into one, leading to a new President as early as January (when Schnabel departs). This might be too quick, however. If the candidates filling Lagarde’s and Schnabel’s positions are approved between the January and March ECB meetings, then Schnabel’s board position will be unfilled for a short period. This eventuality was allowed for in the press release announcing Schnabel’s early exit (responsibilities would be temporarily reallocated to other Board members).
- Political decision-making happens on an accelerated timeline — Decisions on ECB Executive Board appointments are usually taken at scheduled meetings, but the relevant institutions could convene special meetings if necessary. If Lagarde announced an intention to leave before the end of March, the European Council and other bodies could compress the process rather than leave the ECB presidency vacant. Media reports have cited EU diplomates saying the aim is to have a grand package agreement for all three ECB seats agreed by the end of this year. That suggests an accelerated timeline for appointing a new ECB president is plausible. The central implication is that an early-departure announcement could come at any time. The standard institutional timetable could install a successor by the end of March, but an earlier announcement or accelerated meetings could shorten the process materially if policymakers wanted a new president in place sooner. Early departure could preserve policy continuity An early departure would be more likely to preserve than disrupt ECB policy continuity. If Lagarde chooses to leave early and triggers a process that chooses successors for the ECB president, chief economist and head of markets as a block in the next few months, some of the most influential positions at the ECB will be set for the next 8 years. This could lock in the type of monetary policymaking the current European Council (heads of state and government) wants to preserve before the various European elections over the next year potentially challenge the status quo. Our baseline assumes the ECB hikes once more to 2.75% at the December meeting. The risk is that the ECB hikes to 3%. At the current pace of tightening once per quarter, a decision to hike to 3% could be faced in March. By preserving the current ECB approach to monetary policy with an early agreement on personnel, EU leaders would make it easier for a new ECB President to hike at their first meeting in March, if that emerges as the appropriate course of action. The ECB has a well-established strategy and reaction function, and ECB Chief Economist Lane – whose role is to propose the appropriate policy stance – will be in office until May. This h elps preserve a sense of continuity, not disruption, should Lagarde leave early. In any case, the two leading candidates for the Presidency, Knot and de Cos, have extensive ECB experience, reducing the risk that a leadership transition would delay a policy decision. Lead candidates and considerations Klaas Knot (Netherlands) and Pablo Hernández de Cos (Spain) have been the two names most frequently mentioned for the ECB presidency for quite a while now (we wrote about them back in February). Joachim Nagel (Germany) is a possibility too, but less likely given a German is running the European Commission (Ursula von der Leyen). Knot and de Cos were until recently the heads of their respective national central banks, and both the Dutch and Spanish governments have publicly announced their intensions to nominate their respective candidates when the time comes. However, there is no guarantee that the next ECB president will be one of these two candidates. During the last round Lagarde’s name came as somewhat of a surprise as part of a deal to choose the ECB presidency and EC presidency together. A similar surprise candidate could once again emerge, with Lagarde’s presidency proving that successful candidates need not be central bankers or economists. Furthermore, as we have previously written, the decision of the next president will not only be based on candidate merit and the country composition of the ECB Executive Board, but it will also take into account many other factors important to the EU such as gender balance (there will be no female representation on the ECB’s GC when Lagarde and Schnabel leave), other EU appointments, etc. The ECB Board appointments need to achieve a balance between countries and responsibilities too. For example, if the new President is neither Knot or De Cos, this will be a position for Netherlands or Spain. In this case, Germany and France will likely both be seeking the powerful chief economist role – with names such as Markus Brunnermeier and Monika Piazzesi as potential German candidates and Agnes Benassy-Query and Laurence Boone as potential French ca ndidate as the new ECB chief economist, according to the FT.
