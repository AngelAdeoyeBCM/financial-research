# AUD rates Receive Nov RBA as back-to-back rate hikes appear unlikely Trade Idea

## OUR KEY VIEWS

- We interpret hawkish rhetoric from the Reserve Bank of Australia as an effort by the central bank to keep financial conditions tight, rather than an explicit signal that another rate hike is imminent.
- At current levels, we prefer to fade the market’s recent repricing of the November RBA meeting, as back-to-back rate hikes remain unlikely, in our view. Following hawkish commentary from Reserve Bank of Australia Deputy Governor Andrew Hauser, the balance of market expectations has shifted once again, from a hold to a hike for the November meeting. This is reflected in the aggressive repricing in November RBA OIS, which jumped from 5– 6bp earlier this week to 16–17bp on Thursday. Strategic move? While the tone was hawkish, the comments do not seem meaningfully different from the RBA’s recent stance, in our view. Although Hauser said the inflation outlook looks “materially worse” than the RBA’s August projections (which were followed by a rate hike in September), we consider the statement in the context of his hawkish messaging throughout the year, and see it as a component of a deliberate RBA communication strategy: utilizing rhetoric to maintain tight financial conditions, regardless of whether a rate hike will be delivered at the subsequent meeting. We have held a received bias in Nov RBA for some time, predicated on our view that back-to-back rate hikes were unlikely (see EM Asia: What you need to know this week (28 Sep - 4 Oct), dated 28 September). While inflation remains elevated, other developments point towards softness in the economy. Consumer sentiment has fallen again following the RBA’s rate hike in September and are approaching decade lows (Figure 1), which is likely to further the ongoing housing market correction (Figure 2). On the labour market, although the latest unemployment rate falls within the RBA’s implicit 4.5–5.0% target range, it has continued to trend upwards (Figure 3). Coupled with the negative employment print in August, it suggests that monetary policy was already restrictive prior to the rate hike in September, supporting the argument for a pause in November. Softer trajectory: In fact, we believe risk–reward is more attractive to receive the November meeting at these levels. While Q3 CPI remains a risk, the inflation trajectory since the latest hike has been softer than market’s expectations, with both headline and trimmed-mean surprising to the downside in the August CPI print. There has also been a divergence between the quarterly and monthly trimmed-mean indices – the former rose by only 0.81% over Q2, compared to the 0.96% implied by its monthly counterpart (Figure 5) – something we think can happen again in Q3. Additionally, given AI-driven demand was a key factor in the RBA’s September hike, the recent sell-off in AI-related equities could prompt a re-evaluation of the sector’s inflationary impulse, in our view. If the current equity weakness worsens, it may also weigh on consumers via negative wealth effects. Collectively, these positions reflect our view that the RBA is forced to respond to negative supply shocks by maintaining a hawkish stance to ensure financial conditions remain tight. However, as frequently noted by the RBA Governor, traditional monetary policy is a blunt instrument, which is ill-suited against supply-side shocks such as productivity slowdowns or rising global energy prices. Given the mixed domestic economic outlook, we believe the likelihood of back-to-back rate hikes remains low and expect the RBA to first observe the impact of its September hike before tightening further.
