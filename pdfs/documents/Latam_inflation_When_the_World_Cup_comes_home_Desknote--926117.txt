# Latam inflation: When the World Cup comes home

DEEP DIVE DEEP DIVE 11/06/2026 1

Laiz Carvalho, Brazil and Chile Economist | Banco BNP Paribas Brasil S.A.| Pamela Diaz, Mexico Economist | Alejandro Fajardo, Mexico Economist | BNP Paribas Mexico SA.. Sources: FIFA, BNP Paribas estimates Fig. 1: World Cup matches distribution and maximum inflation effect – Brazil 2014 vs Mexico 2026 Premier sports events and inflation: The link between flagship global sporting events (such as soccer’s 2026 World Cup) and consumer prices is intuitive but often underestimated, we think. When a host is selected for a World Cup tournament or a round of Olympic Games, a multi-year economic cycle begins. Investment typically flows into venue construction, urban infrastructure, transportation networks and hospitality capacity. Workers are hired, wages tend to rise in construction and services jobs, and domestic demand is generally pulled forward. When the event itself arrives, the economy typically has already absorbed ample above-trend stimulus – and this shows up in prices. Economists call this the anticipation effect. Inflation does not spike on the day of a sporting event’s opening ceremony. Rather, it begins to build two to five years earlier, peaks around the event year and then fades. Crucially, there seems to be no lasting legacy inflation – once an investment cycle ends, price pressures normalize, typically within two years of the final whistle. Brazil, a case study: Our analysis is grounded in the peer-reviewed methodology of Areosa and Areosa (2014), published as a working paper of the Banco Central do Brasil (BCB). The BCB aimed to quantify the inflation impact for Brazil of hosting soccer’s 2014 World Cup and the Summer Olympics in 2016, using a panel of 173 countries from 1948 to 2012. We replicate this framework with three material updates suited to the 2026 event:

- Updated dataset: Bloomberg CPI data for 27 countries from 1990 to 2024, incorporating all events through soccer’s 2022 World Cup and the 2024 Summer Olympics.

## KEY MESSAGES

Hosting flagship global sporting events (such as soccer’s 2026 World Cup) generates a measurable but transitory inflation premium. This averages 0.9–2.1pp at the cumulative peak for full hosts, we find. The effect is front-loaded with most inflationary pressure materializing before the event, driven by infrastructure investment and anticipatory demand. The current soccer World Cup is structurally unique. Mexico and Canada will each host only around 13% of matches, while the US will host 75% of all matches including the final. This implies a meaningful inflation asymmetry across the three co-hosts. Our model estimates a peak cumulative CPI impact of around 0.3pp in Mexico. Short-term (2026) effects are seen at 0.1pp. There is no evidence of a lasting inflationary legacy. The premium dissipates within two years after the event in the historical record. - Extended event coverage: All soccer World Cups and Summer/Winter Olympics between 1990 and 2024, totaling 29 events.
- Partial-host innovation: The 2026 World Cup is the first to split hosting duties across three countries with materially different match allocations. We introduce a proportional scaling factor to differentiate the inflation impact by match share. The model estimates a panel regression with two-way fixed effects (country and year), identifying the inflation impact of event hosting while controlling for country-specific structural differences and global macroeconomic cycles. The key parameters are the leads (anticipation) and lags (legacy) of the host indicator, which together map the full inflationary arc of a top-tier sports event. World Cup’s inflation arc: Across all soccer World Cups and rounds of Olympic Games in our sample, the model identified a consistent pattern for full host countries. Inflation begins to drift upward approximately three to four years before an event, accelerates through the event year, and peaks one to two years afterward. The maximum cumulative effect in our updated model was approximately 2.1pp, above the BCB’s 1.76pp estimate. This reflects the greater infrastructure intensity of more recent events and the more resilient inflation profile from recent years. Critically, the effect is self-correcting. By two years after an event, the cumulative inflationary impact becomes statistically indistinguishable from zero. There is no permanent shift in the price level and no evidence of a structural inflation legacy from being a World Cup or Olympic Games host. With Mexico hosting only 12.5% of the 2026 World Cup matches, the model estimates that the cumulative 0.3pp effect will take place between 2025 and 2027. 2026 World Cup – an asymmetrical inflation story: The 2026 tournament creates unprecedented inflation asymmetry. For Mexico, the impact is more muted and shorter, reflecting the limited economic footprint of hosting only 13 matches fixtures. Mexico: Limited spillover, but watch services The estimated cumulative inflation impact on Mexico is a modest 0.3pp, as per our model. However, the headline number masks sectoral heterogeneity: hospitality, food-service, transport, and tourism-adjacent industries in host cities (Mexico City, Guadalajara, Monterrey) may experience more acute price pressures. We may find more signal in high-frequency services CPI releases in 2026 than in headline inflation alone. Why the footprint is so contained: There’s almost no greenfield investment related to the tournament for Mexico. All three World Cup venues in Mexico existed before the country was selected to be a host – the Estadio Azteca required only refurbishment, while Monterrey’s Estadio BBVA (2015) and Guadalajara’s Estadio Akron (2010) were already fully functional – so the multi-year construction boom that accounts for most of a full host’s inflation premium is largely absent here. Besides group-stage fixtures, Mexico will stage only two more matches: one round-of-32 and a round-of-8. Hence, the anticipation arc the model identifies for full hosts never really forms. Instead, there’s a short, demand-side tourism pulse concentrated in three cities around the time of the matches, which is why the cumulative impact stays at ~0.3pp, with less than 0.1pp in 2026. Part of the demand in host cities around the time of the matches will be substitution rather than addition, with domestic and business travel displaced to other dates. There will also be a degree of crowding-out, as regular leisure visitors avoid the congestion and peak pricing. The inflationary impact on Mexico sits an order of magnitude below that in the Brazil case and is closer to a localized seasonal blip than to a macro inflation event. What to watch. As the pulse is seen in lodging, restaurants, flights and package tourism, we think the clearest signal will come from Mexico’s bi-weekly core CPI prints spanning the tournament. We would interpret any mid-2026 firmness in host-city core services as transitory and look through it: the data would not be indicative of underlying persistence, and any price increase would typically dissipate well inside the two-year window the model identifies. For Mexico, the durable legacy will be reputational and investment-profile visibility, not a measurable mark on the CPI. The Brazil case Brazil is the only country that has hosted a FIFA World Cup (2014) and a Summer Olympics (2016) within two years of each other, making it the closest real-world test of the model underlying our 2026 projections. The BCB predicted a peak cumulative CPI impact of 3.5pp for this two-event overlap. However, headline IPCA peaked at 10.67% in 2015, well above the BCB’s forecast, but the excess was almost entirely explained by three exogenous shocks unrelated to the sports events: a severe El Niño (estimated contribution: 2.5pp), a forced unwind of artificially suppressed electricity and fuel prices after the 2014 election (~1.5pp), and a 50% currency depreciation triggered by the Petrobras scandal and lower commodity prices (~1.0pp). Stripping those out, the residual attributable to the mega-events sits at roughly 1.5–2.5pp, which is consistent with the model’s range. Crucially, by 2018, IPCA had returned to 3.75%, confirming the model’s core prediction: no lasting inflationary legacy. Appendix: Core equation used in our model The core estimating equation follows the equation used by Areosa and Areosa (2014): πit = a0 Host_it + A(L) Host_it + B(F) Host_it + c0 Bid_it + C(L) Bid_it + D(F) Bid_it + G(L)πit + αi + βt + εit, where πit denotes the natural log of gross consumer price inflation in country i in year t. Host_it and Bid_it are binary indicators for event-hosting and failed-bid countries. A(L) and C(L) are lag polynomials capturing legacy effects; B(F) and D(F) are lead polynomials capturing anticipation effects. G(L)πit controls for inflation inertia. αi and βt are country and time fixed effects, and εit is the error term. The model is estimated via Ordinary Least Squares with a two-way within transformation, removing both country-level and year-level fixed effects. Standard errors are computed under homoskedasticity.
