# Sunday Tea with BNPP: Hydration break in Hormuz

. 21/06/2026 1

.

## KEY MESSAGES

- While the situation remains fluid, developments in the Middle East are supportive of our resilient outlook for the global economy and we see further downside for oil prices in the coming days assuming talks progress and evidence builds of Hormuz flows normalising.
- However, a sustainable return to pre-war price levels looks unlikely given structural supply loss and a likely sustained demand boost as inventories are withdrawn. Inflationary pressures are likely to be tempered but not tamed.
- Last week’s FOMC meeting increases our conviction that the next move from the Fed is a hike and that risks are skewed towards an earlier move than our December base case. At the same time, less forward guidance implies more market volatility.
- We do not think an end to the conflict means a return of US Treasury yields to their pre-war levels. For Asia FX, we expect flow dynamics to remain bearish and overshadow the impact of lower oil prices. A new phase of play: The memorandum of understanding signed last Wednesday by the US and Iran is good news for the global economy and suggests markets may be able to look ahead to a more benign phase of the conflict. Although the newsflow over the weekend suggests that the process may be bumpy, we expect Brent crude price to fall closer to USD70/bbl in the near term assuming flows through the strait resume in earnest. According to commodities data provider Kpler, 200 laden tankers carrying about 160 million barrels of crude have been trapped in the Gulf and are ready to exit. Upon opening, these barrels should find their way to consumers within a month or so. In our April Global Outlook, we outlined a view that the global economy would slow as a result of the conflict, but that it was a story of resilience dented rather than derailed, thanks to strong pre-conflict growth momentum and structural tailwinds from AI and higher defence spending. The economy has proven resilient over recent months, and a reduced probability of a severe escalation bodes well for the path ahead, further strengthening our view. The coming week’s PMI data will likely capture some of the post-MoU period, but the feed-through to actual activity is likely to be fairly limited at this stage. Extra time required: What happens to prices beyond the very near term will be a function of the logistical constraints to bringing back supply, the strength of the demand bounce-back (considering oil inventories need to be replenished) and the outcome of continuing negotiations between the US and Iran on unresolved elements of the MoU – including the nuclear arrangements and control over traffic through the strait. We assume that after a near-term dip, oil prices will gradually climb to around USD85/bbl by year end (see Middle East, Barrel half full?). Our working assumption is that more time than currently allotted will be required to turn the MoU into a fully-fledged ‘deal’. The 2015 JCPOA deal to restrict Iran’s nuclear programme, for example, took 20 months to complete. The MoU allows for an extension of the initial 60-day period of negotiations by “mutual consent”. The normalisation of flows through Hormuz is no simple task either. We expect a three-step process that starts with the strait being de-mined and fully reopened – and then remaining open. This cannot happen overnight, and we note that the MoU reportedly acknowledges “the traffic of commercial vessels will immediately start, and considering the need for removing the technical and military obstacles in de-mining by the Islamic Republic of Iran, will be instated within 30 days”. Second, once the blocked tankers reach their destinations and unload, they would then need to return to the Gulf in order to receive built-up storage from the region – which we think could take 1–2 months. Third, the full normalisation of flows will only set in when producers can bring back online the about-12mbd of shut-in production, a final step that we believe will require very clear evidence of progress towards a sustainable deal, alongside confidence in an unbroken resumption of flows. Game changers: Although we think the probability of a severe escalation in the conflict has declined, we still see risks as skewed towards higher crude prices. For example, ship owners may not deem the MoU to be sufficiently reassuring to send their fleet back into the strait; the MoU could collapse with no agreement on nuclear in the initially allotted 60 days; some of the production cuts may become permanent, or; demand could bounce back even more strongly than we assume, even if Hormuz flows remain theoretically unconstrained. And, while the MoU allows for a 60-day ‘no charge’ period for commercial vessels, it leaves open how future administration is managed and therefore raises the prospect of transit costs in the strait.. 21/06/2026 2 A final whistle will not reset the score: Oil prices coming off should, all else equal, benefit Asia FX. However, as we outlined in FX: Comparing flow dynamics for the JPY, KRW and TWD on 15 June, we expect flow dynamics to remain bearish and overshadow the impact of lower oil prices. Foreign investor flows skew most bearish for the KRW, while domestic institutional and retail flows skew more bearish for the JPY and TWD. The JPY remains our preferred funder in North Asia, with fundamentals and flows weighing on the currency. What’s more, and for a number of reasons, we do not think an end to the conflict means US Treasury yields would return to their pre-war levels. First, the market’s link to oil prices had already weakened, and yields are already higher than oil prices would suggest by 15 – 20bp, reflecting economic strength. Second, inflation risks have broadened recently and we think a mere cooling of headline inflation will be insufficient for markets to un-price inflation concerns. Third, an end to the conflict may ultimately end up bringing more attention to long-term issues such as fiscal deficits, and investors may come to a realisation that the evolving Treasury buyer base is more price sensitive. Finally, an end to the conflict could add to economic strength as consumer sentiment improves, alongside the possi bility of more wealth effects forming a rally in risk assets, lifting yields (see US rates: End of conflict ≠ End of higher yields, 16 June) Warsh defending 2%: Since early June, we have expected the Fed’s next policy move to be a rate hike, aimed at withdrawing the monetary stimulus provided by last year’s three ‘insurance’ cuts (see US: Changing our Fed call to rate hikes, dated 6 June). The FOMC meeting last week suggests this view is well on track. The Summary of Economic Projections moved even further than we expected, with half of participating policymakers now looking for a hike this year. The statement included a strong assertion that “the Committee will deliver price stability” while new Chair Kevin Warsh made clear that a discussion on the 2% target was not included within the scope of the ‘taskforces’ that have been established to review, and potentially adjust, various aspects of the conduct of monetary policy. Our view that US rate hikes begin in December is based on three factors: an expectation that the labour market will tighten only gradually; the likelihood that policymakers will hesitate to act just ahead of the November mid-term elections; and the potential for a partial repeat of the Fed’s very slow 2021 pivot away from pessimism on the post-pandemic economic recovery as the economy reaccelerated. We remain broadly comfortable with this as a base case, although policymaker views appear to be shifting rapidly, and we emphasise that the risks are skewed towards action arriving sooner, and that every meeting (including July) is live. Twists and turns: While Johan Cruyff, Maradona and Garrincha have all flummoxed opponents with their eponymous turns, last week’s FOMC pivot delivered a more familiar response in markets – albeit with an added twist. We find that turning points in FOMC policy typically trigger positive-gamma events (large moves in FX as markets re-price future expectations) and last week’s was no exception, but with little guidance for where to set future expectations or how to think about the central bank’s reaction functi on, volatility may be supported going forwards. We think future FOMC meetings are likely attract higher risk premium in FX vol, and future data releases could also lead to larger market responses than in the recent past. We think FX vol, which is at cycle lows and was factoring in little risk premium ahead of last week’s Fed meeting, may re-price higher as a turn to a greater FOMC risk premium is digested. We added a long GBPUSD vol position through a capped vol swap on 18 June. This structure ensures the position is always long gamma no matter where spot moves, and we select GBPUSD due to its high correlation with US rates vol, high sensitivity to US inflation uncertainty and very low levels of risk premium. The new chair may h ave marked a turning point in FOMC policy; the shift in investor approaches to US rates risk may prove a turning point in FX vol too. BoE stays on the touchline: The Bank of England’s “active hold” last Thursday shows a central bank caught between two potential policy mistakes. On one hand, an insufficient response to the energy price shock risks entrenching inflationary pressures. On the other, overly aggressively action could amplify negative growth risks. It seems the one thing MPC members can agree on is that they need more time. More time to assess the durability of the progress of talks between the US and Iran. More time to assess second-round effects. And, perhaps, more time to assess even the domestic political situation. We now expect the BoE to follow a risk-management approach and deliver a single ‘insurance’ hike in September, instead of the more aggressive response we previously anticipated. We still th ink some tightening is warranted given our expectation that second-round effects will build despite the recent pull-back in energy prices. The message from Thursday, however, was that the MPC will need greater conviction about these effects before acting. Bend it like Burnham: Andy Burnham’s decisive win in the Makerfield by-election, paves the way for a potential change in the leadership of the ruling Labour Party. We think his surprising margin of victory slightly increases the probability that there is an orderly transition of power, instead of a drawn-out contest. Over the weekend, press reports suggested that UK Prime Minister Keir Starmer could resign as soon as Monday. In either scenario, more clarity is expected by the time of the party’s 27–30 September conference. On fiscal policy, our base case remains a “fiscally responsible shift to the left”, centred on adjustments to the composition of policy rather than a renewed borrowing impulse. For markets, a moderate degree of UK political risk premium is justified as the focus shifts to this kind of policy detail. A more significant rise in risk premium would require clear indications that policy is on a less prudent trajectory, but clarity may not come until the autumn. For more detail, see our full publication – UK politics: A slow Burnham (Part 2). NBH rate cut is an open goal: Against the grain of the tightening bias adopted by a majority of central banks right now, we think the National Bank of Hungary will be in a position to comfortably cut its policy rate by 25bp this coming week. A robust strengthening in the HUF post the mid-April election and a sharp slowing in services inflation since the start of the year should give the central bank more than enough room to kick off a careful cutting cycle, in our view. With a cut already well-priced by markets, it seems clear that Hungary’s positive idiosyncratic story around potential progress towards euro adoption will allow it to continue easing rates without being penalised (see Hungary: Euro vision, dated 4 June).
