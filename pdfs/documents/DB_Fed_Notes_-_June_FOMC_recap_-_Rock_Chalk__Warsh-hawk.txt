# Fed Notes - June FOMC recap - Rock Chalk, Warsh-hawk

Economics Fed Notes Date 17 June 2026

- New Fed Chair Kevin Warsh immediately put his stamp on the Committee. In a return to 1990s-style communications, he significantly shortened the post-meeting statement – it was cut from 341 words to 130 – and removed forward guidance. He also did not submit forecasts for the Summary of Economic Projections (SEP). Those projections, completed by his colleagues, skewed undeniably hawkish, and Warsh did not distance himself from this signal in his press conference.
- The median dot for 2026 showed half a hike, with nine officials projecting higher rates this year (six projecting 50bps of hikes or more). The median dot for next year moved up to 3.6 25%, consistent with the current fed funds rate. The shift higher was motivated by a reassessment of inflation persistence, with core PCE revised up to 3.3% for 2026 and 2.5% for 2027.
- In his press conference, Warsh leaned hawkish, emphasizing the importance of restoring price stability, and noting that the Committee has work to do on inflation. He argued that the evidence is mixed on how restrictive policy currently is. Warsh also presented a positive picture of the labor market and growth and did not emphasize dovish points (e.g., disinflationary forces from AI or trimmed mean).
- Warsh also announced the establishment of task forces in five areas:
1. communications,
2. the balance sheet,
3. the use and reliance on data sources,
4. productivity and jobs in the age of AI, and
5. the Fed’s inflation frameworks. He referenced these task forces repeatedly in the press conference and expects most of their work to conclude by year-end.
- While we maintain our baseline view that the Fed is likely to keep rates steady, today’s meeting crystalized risks for rate hikes. A Fed that does not rely as heavily on forward guidance might desire to move quicker on tightening policy – creating risks of rate hikes over the coming meetings. At the same time, markets may have difficulty digesting today’s signals, leading to a meaningful tightening of financial conditions that limits the scope for higher rates in the near term. FOMC statement The FOMC statement was overhauled, more than halving its word count and reordering the remaining content to place policy decisions upfront. Indeed, the Matthew Luzzetti, Ph.D. succinct three-paragraph statement very much resembled those from the Greenspan-led Fed era starting with the Committee decision at the top: “The Committee voted unanimously to maintain the fed funds rate at 31⁄2 –33⁄4 percent and reaffirm[ed] its policy of maintaining ample reserves in the banking system.” The statement then provided a concise assessment of current conditions, noting that “activity is expanding at a solid pace despite elevated uncertainty that owes, in part, to the conflict in the Middle East.” This resilience was attributed in part to “strong” productivity growth and capital investment. On the labor market, the Committee signalled that recent payroll gains are likely near the break-even pace, stating that “[j]ob gains have kept pace with the workforce” and that the “unemployment rate has changed little.” The closing paragraph focused on inflation, which “remains elevated relative” to the 2% target, in part reflecting the effects of supply shocks. The statement concluded with an unambiguous commitment to restoring price stability: “The Committee will delive r price stability.” That sentiment was repeated throughout the press conference. Summary of Economic Projections (SEP) The SEP was notably hawkish, with the median fed funds projection for 2026 rising by 1.5 hikes to 3.75% (there was a split median given that Warsh did not submit dots). The distribution also shifted meaningfully: nine participants now expect hikes this year—three penciling in one hike, five two, and one three. Eight participants see rates on hold, while one expects a cut. The upward shift extends across the path, with the 2027 median rising by 50bp to 3.6 25% and 2028 increasing 25bp to 3. 375%. Interestingly, another official joined Warsh in not submitting a dot for 2028. The shift was driven by higher and more persistent inflation. Median headline inflation for 2026 jumped nearly a percentage point to 3.6%, while 2027 edged up a tenth to 2.3% and 2028 remained at 2.0%. The core profile now implies an overshoot through at l east 2028: 2026 core PCE was revised up 60bp to 3.3%, 2027 by 30bp to 2.5%, and 2028 by a tenth to 2.1%. Growth and labor market projections were little changed. The 2026 growth median was revised down two-tenths to 2.2%, while the unemployment rate was marked down a tenth to 4.3%, likely reflecting a mark-to-market of recent data. Importantly, the balance of risks around the l abor market forecasts improved, with ten participants characterizing them as broadly balanced versus 16 in March seeing them as weighted to wards a higher unemployment rate. By contrast, all but one participant now sees inflation risks as skewed to the upside. Press conference As expected, Warsh deliberately removed any forward guidance. He was equally dismissive of the informational value of the dot plot in its current form, highlighting the lack of conviction behind participants’ submissions and signaling openness to broader changes to the SEP and communications framework. To that end, Warsh announced “task forces” in five key areas:
1. the “form and function of Fed communications”,
2. balance sheet policy,
3. “new information sources and consideration of methodological changes to improve data-gathering”,
4. productivity and jobs in the age of AI, and
5. the F ed’s inflation frameworks. He stated his expectation that the task forces will begin work in the next couple of weeks, with updates in the Fall and “hopefully most if not all concluding by year-end.” He provided some details about what these task forces would focus on. On the balance sheet, he noted that the task force will review the ample reserves regime and composition of the Fed’s balance sheet. On inflation, the task force will address the drivers of inflation; however, he sees no reason to revisit the Fed’s 2% inflation objective until they have reached that goal. On data sources, they will investigate how the Fed can leverage more contemporaneous, real-time data sources, “with the aim of giving policymakers more accurate, relevant, contemporaneous, and perhaps most important, actionable information on the state of our economy.” On communications, they will assess various tools, including the statement, forward guidance, the dot plot, the SEP, and the minutes. Though skeptical of forward guidance, Warsh could have attempted to counterbalance the hawkish signal from the SEP, and it was significant that he did not distance himself from that view. While he noted the Fed has little ability to combat inflation that arises from supply shocks, he also emphasized that the Fed has an important role in preventing those supply shocks from broadening out inflation via “second and third-order effects.” He also did not lean on any of the potential dovish arguments he has referenced in the past, e.g., the disinflationary forces from AI and productivity or the more benign signals from alternative measures of trend inflation, like trimmed mean. Indeed, Warsh repeated several times the importance of the Fed delivering on its “price stability” mandate, which he mentioned a dozen times. The closest Warsh came to forward guidance was “So what you heard from the Committee today is we’ve got some work to be done on the price stability front. “He also noted that “we’ll be meeting again in six weeks. I think we’re going to know more then, and I think that my colleagues are very attentive to incoming developments between now and then.” A straight read of the press conference is that Warsh took any opportunity he could to emphasize the importance of price stability and did not try to distance himself from the hawkish signal of the dot plot, even if he did not explicitly indicate a need for higher rates. A Fed that does not rely on forward guidance might prove to be nimbler, setting up the potential for earlier rate hikes than anticipated. That said, the hawkish signals from today’s meeting along with a trans ition to a less transparent Fed could prove difficult for markets, potentially leading to a meaningful tightening of financial conditions that limits the scope for higher rates in the near term.
