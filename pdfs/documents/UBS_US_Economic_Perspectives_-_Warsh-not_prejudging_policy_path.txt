# US Economic Perspectives Warsh: not prejudging policy path

Warsh avoids committing to a policy path Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh did not offer guidance on his expectations for the pace of future policy adjustments during his fireside chat with IMF Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva during the IMF's annual conference in Bangkok, Thailand. Rather, he simply reiterated that he would not pre-judge what action would be appropriate at future FOMC meetings. "We removed a dose of accommodation about a month ago... I don't prejudge meetings. I don't believe in saying now what I might do at the meetings that we have in the balance of the year," he noted. Warsh has been loath to offer explicit forward guidance, even refraining from offering his own forecasts for the Summary of Economic Projections, as he reiterated in the press conference following the September FOMC decision (press conference transcript available here). Warsh's comments during the discussion centered in large part around the AI buildout as a near-term driver of demand. He said little regarding the inflation consequences of that buildout, however. At the end of his remarks, he noted simply that he has heard "resolve from my colleagues around the world to deliver price stability." Our base case is for the Fed to maintain its current policy stance at the October 27-28 FOMC meeting, then raising the federal funds rate target range at the December meeting. Bumpy road to AI's promise; Warsh announces field hearing. When asked about his understanding of current economic drivers, Chair Warsh started by focusing on the investment surge, particularly in AI infrastructure. Though he noted that over the medium term the implementation of AI tools will increase the productive capacity of the economy, Warsh said that in the near term " we're seeing increases in aggregate demand" off the back of the AI buildout. He also noted, " I feel that in the United States, that the real economy, because of a resiliency that you mentioned, because of a surge in investment which isn't just AI, that surge of investment is broadening. That means we've got plenty of tailwinds that are giving some strength and durability to this economy." Acknowledging that the path toward increased productive capacity "is likely to be a very bumpy one," Chair Warsh announced that the Fed's Productivity and Jobs task force, led by Marc Andreessen, Professor Charles I. Jones and Asha Sharma, will hold its first field hearing on Monday, November 16 in the San Francisco Bay Area. See our recap of the task forces' leaders here. In recent days, Governor Waller also offered comments on the macroeconomic consequences of the AI buildout and implementation, suggesting it would ultimately raise the long-run neutral rate of interest. This week in Bangkok, Chief US Economist Jonathan Pingle hosted a panel discussion with global central bankers on the key drivers of economic activity in 2026 (replay available here). Warsh comments little on inflation, economic outlook Despite an above-consensus September CPI report, Chair Warsh said very little regarding inflation. While we're hesitant to read too much into his remarks, as they did not specifically pertain to the current economic environment, we suspect his remarks implied that he would not be inclined to put too much weight on any one print. Comments made since the release of the September CPI report, including from FRB of San Francisco President Mary Daly (link here) and FRB of Chicago President Austan Goolsbee (link here), acknowledged that the report was " discouraging," but did not inspire additional urgency. Daly said, " I don't feel urgent that we have to decide with a limited set of information." Our comprehensive analysis of the September CPI release can be found here. Just this evening, FRB of Cleveland President Hammack repeated her warning that a persistent overshooting of inflation could lead to an " inflationary mindset, " and unanchored inflation expectations (reporting available here). A rethinking of when it is appropriate to look through price pressures caused by supply shocks has been a prevalent topic among Fed speakers in recent weeks. On the sidelines of the IMF annual meeting in Bangkok, Thailand, UBS also hosted a conference that included a panel discussion on Rethinking Monetary Policy for a Shock-Prone World (replay available here).
