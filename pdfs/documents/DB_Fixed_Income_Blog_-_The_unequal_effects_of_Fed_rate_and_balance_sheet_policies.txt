# Fixed Income Blog - The unequal effects of Fed rate and balance sheet policies

Rates Fixed Income Blog Date 1 May 2026

In his confirmation hearing last week, Kevin Warsh reiterated criticism of Fed balance sheet policy over the past 15 years. In doing so, he argued that, relative to policy rate adjustments, asset purchases fuel inequality by disproportionately impacting financial asset prices. Though it went unstated, a reasonable reading of the claim is that asset purchases have outsized effects on equity prices. Indeed, as shown in Figure 1, along with private business wealth, equities are the asset category that is most unequally distributed across household wealth percentiles. An important implication of this claim is that a growth-neutral shift in the mix of monetary policy tools, towards lower policy rates and a smaller Fed balance sheet, would be beneficial in reducing wealth inequality (or certainly not amplify it). This is not purely academic. Warsh has previously suggested that further shrinking the balance sheet would open a path for the Fed to cut its policy rate, and there is increasing focus, on the Committee and in markets, on steps the Fed could take to achieve a smaller balance sheet. Figure 1: Equities are more unequally held than other financial assets Source: Federal Reserve Survey of Consumer Finances and Financial Accounts of the US; Deutsche Bank Research Policy transmission The claim on inequality is plausible but not a priori obvious. We see monetary policy as generally working by reducing interest rates faced by households and firms and easing broader financial conditions; e.g., a weaker dollar that spurs net exports and higher equity and house prices that have wealth effects. Matthew Luzzetti, Ph.D. The most immediate difference between the interest rate and balance sheet tools is in the channels by which they lower longer-term rates: rate policy (including forward guidance) acts mainly on the expected path of short-term rates, while asset purchases primarily compress term premia and MBS spreads. (This mapping is not entirely clean; rate policy can move term premia by impacting uncertainty around the short-rate path and QE can drive short-rate expectations through signaling.) While this differential se ems likely to generate disparate spillovers to broader asset prices, it is not clear ex ante that one or other of the tools should have a systematically larger effect on equities. That is fundamentally an empirical question. So, what is the evidence in support of the inequality claim? And, more generally, how would a shift in the mix of tools that combines policy rate cuts with balance sheet reduction impact financial markets? Financial market impacts of rate and QE shocks One way to address these questions is to separately identify rate and balance sheet policy shocks and estimate their effects on yields and broader financial conditions. For this, we draw on a JME paper by Eric Swanson 1 (professor at UC Irvine and formerly of FRB-SF), which identifies fed funds rate (FFR), forward guidance (FG), and QE shocks over 1991–2019 and traces out their effects on the Treasury yield curve, corporate rates and spreads, equities, and dollar exchange rates. The key estimates from the paper are reproduced in Figure 2, which report the impact of one-standard-deviation (easing) shocks to the various monetary policy tools. The estimates reflect asset-price responses measured in 30-minute or one-day (for credit) windows around FOMC statement releases. (Note: in addition to the full sample, the paper examines three subsamples – pre-ZLB, ZLB, and post- ZLB – and does not find major differences in asset price effects; for simplicity, here we focus on the full-sample results. It also considers the persistence of price changes and finds little evidence that they die out significantly over the first six months or so.) 1 Swanson, Eric T. 2020. “Measuring the Effects of Federal Reserve Forward Guidance and Asset Purchases on Financial Markets.” Journal of Monetary Economics. Figure 2: Different monetary policy tools have disparate impacts on financial markets Source: Swanson, JME 2020; Deutsche Bank Research. *Exchange rates are expressed as the foreign-currency price of the dollar. The Treasury yield curve results are intuitive and in line with our thinking outlined above. Specifically, accommodative fed funds rate-shocks bull-steepen the curve, forward guidance shocks have their largest effect on belly rates (at horizons beyond which the ZLB is unlikely to bind), and QE shocks bull-flatten the curve. Looking across the other assets, QE has the most pronounced impact on corporate credit rates, in line with the bigger effect on longer-term benchmark (i.e., UST) rates. The shocks have broadly comparable effects on the euro-dollar and dollar-yen exchange rates. And, contrary to the suggestion that balance sheet policy disproportionately benefits equities, the increase in the S&P 500 from a one-standard-deviation funds rate shock is an order of magnitude larger than the impact from a QE shock. One potential explanation for this is that the effect on equities comes mainly through lower discount rates on future earnings, and for this the expected short-rate path matters more than term premia. Calibrating the shocks and a thought experiment The figure above showed the impact of one-standard-deviation policy shocks. For a cleaner comparison – one that puts the tools on more equal footing – and to speak directly to the question of substitution, we next re-scale the shocks so that each produces a similar easing in financial conditions and thus the growth outlook. To do so, we use the Fed staff’s Financial Conditions Impulse on Growth index (FCI-G), which aggregates changes in a set of financial variables – the fed funds rate, 10y UST yield, 30 y fixed mortgage rate, BBB corporate bond yield, Dow Jones total stock market index, Zillow house price index, and nominal broad dollar index – using weights implied by FRB/US and other Fed models. The financial variables are weighted using impulse response coefficients that quantify the effects of unanticipated changes in each on real GDP growth over the subsequent year. We combine the FCI-G weights with Swanson’s asset response estimates to scale each policy shock so that it delivers 10bps easing in FCI-G. That is, we preserve the pattern of cross-asset moves from Swanson, and scale the rate, FG and QE shocks so that each would be expected to raise GDP growth by 0.1ppt. This scaling inevitably requires some judgment and assumptions given the Swanson paper does not include the exact set of financial assets that comprise FCI-G. In particular, we:

1. assume that the response of the 30y mortgage rate is equivalent to the simple average of the 5y and 10y UST yield response, implicitly assuming no additional impact from monetary policy on MBS spreads or the primary-secondary mortgag e rate spread;
2. substitute the S&P 500 index for the Dow Jones index;
3. replace the broad do llar index with a weighted average of the EUR and JPY, with weights given by the relative share of each currency in the nominal broad index, and
4. omit house prices altogether. The resulting comparison across assets in FCI-G is shown in Figure 3 (lhs). The earlier cross-asset pattern is preserved with this re-scaling: relative to a fed funds rate shock, QE has a smaller impact on short-term rates, a larger impact on long-term rates (including corporates and mortgages), a marginally bigger impact on the dollar, and a smaller impact on equities. Put simply, from the prior empirical analyses we leverage, there is no indication that QE has an outsized impact on equities relative to an equivalently calibrated interest rate shock. Next, we ask a simple question: if the FOMC were to deliver or signal (via guidance) rate cuts and a tightening in balance sheet policy that together leave expected growth unchanged, what would the e xpected impact on asset prices be? The results, shown in Figure 3 (rhs), follow directly from the estimates detailed above. A policy tool substitution of this sort would lower short-term rates, raise long-term rates, and lift equities. In other words, based on these estimates, the type of policy substitution env isioned by Warsh would worsen inequality by raising equity prices. Finally, as a check on these results, we run a separate set of regressions also using intraday asset price reactions to FOMC announcements. Specifically, we regress the change in the S&P 500 on 2y and 2y8y forward UST rates, as well as on DB’s 5s10s term premium (TP) and the change in the 10y UST that is orthogonal to the TP move (as a proxy for the short-rate-expectations component of the 10y move).2 As indicated by Figure 4, the regressions find that front-end rates and the expectations component of 10y UST have a much more significant impact on equities than far-forward rates or term premia do, which broadly aligns with the findings above on rate vs. QE shocks. Market implications Chair-designee Warsh, in his confirmation hearing and other recent remarks, has suggested that a smaller Fed balance sheet could open the door to Fed rate cuts. On top of that there has been growing focus, at the Fed and in markets, on concrete steps that could be taken to reduce the balance sheet. Warsh’s views will clearly matter on this. Any policy actions would of course require majority support on the FOMC, but simply by framing the debate he can shift the so-called Overton Window on balance sheet policy. And there is a significant range of possibilities even within the ample-reserves regime, as reflected in recent speech es and papers by Governor Miran and Dallas Fed President Logan. (For our discussion of possible measures see here and here.) We see no evidence that the Committee would choose to return to scarce reserves but have not heard from Warsh on this. Where the FOMC lands (and any response from Treasury on issuance) will of course have big implications for markets. While a smaller balance could open the door to Fed rate cuts or guidance around them, the work here shows that if the policy mix is adjusted in a way that is growth-neutral it would significantly twist-steepen the curve. A final important note is that such a substitution in tools would be difficult to achieve, beyond the challenges of calibrating it to be growth-neutral. Any of the balance sheet policies under consideration would take considerable time to decide and implement; the Committee will approach this deliberatively and we keep saying the timeline should be measured in years not quarters or months. Forward guidance on future balance sheet actions could be used to bring market effects forward but there is relatively little experience with it and not much that lends itself to empirical analysis. Caveats We find the approach adopted here to be a useful way to gauge the relative impact of rate and QE shocks on equities and, by extension, inequality. That said, there we recognize several limitations to what we’ve done. First, it could be that the financial conditions-channel does not fully capture the transmission of these policies to the real economy. For example, there could be money supply effects that operate independent of asset price changes, and our calibration to FCI-G necessarily omits any impact on house prices. That said, incorporating these would likely widen the disparity between the effects of balance sheet and rate shocks on equity prices. This is because these additional channels are likely to be more impactful for balance sheet policy, and accounting for them would likely reduce, on relative basis, the size of the QT shock needed to deliver a 0.1ppt growth impulse, bringing down the impact on equities. Second, the estimates of asset price reactions that we use could be biased, due to state-dependent effects (e.g., QE has bigger impact when markets are stressed) or because they are based on policy shocks only through 2019. Third, in the substitution thought-experiment, we implicitly assume a QT and QE shocks are symmetric, with effects of the same magnitude but opposite signs. But there are good arguments and evidence that point to significant asymmetries between QE and QT, beyond the possible state-dependence noted above.
