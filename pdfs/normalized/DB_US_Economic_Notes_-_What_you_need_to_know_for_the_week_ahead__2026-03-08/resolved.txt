# US Economic Notes - What you need to know for the week ahead

Economics US Economic Notes Date 8 March 2026

Commentary for Monday: With the Fed in [the Fed's] self-imposed blackout period, the economic data will get a chance to do the talking ahead of the March 18th FOMC meeting. Of particular note will be the inflation data, namely Wednesday’s CPI report for February and Friday’s core PCE reading for January, but there will be a smattering of labor market data to help put context around last Friday’s disappointing February employment report. To recap, [the February employment report] was much weaker than expected with a 92k decline in total nonfarm payrolls (vs. +126k in January) and an 86k decline (vs. +146k) in private payrolls. Job losses were broad-based, and there were notable d rops in construction (-11k vs +48 k) and health care and social assistance (-19k vs. +116k), two sectors which contributed to January’s upside surprise. In addition, the unemployment rate rose by a tenth to 4.4%, almost rounding up to 4.5%, and the labor force participation rate fell to 62.0%, the lowest since December 2021. That being said, [the February employment report] was not all bad news. The U -6 measure of underemployment fell by two tenths to 7.9%, while [the author's] proxy for aggregate nominal wage growth remained healthy at 4.45%, supported by steady hours at the top of [hours'] range over the last almost two years (34.3) and continued solid wage gains (+0.4% vs. +0.4%). Indeed, there was a noticeable increase in hours worked and wage growth for production and nonsupervisory workers in the construction sector, suggesting that employers can adapt to the low-hiring [or] low-firing equilibrium by increasing labor demand on the intensive margin if [employers] are unable to hire. In addition, some of the decline in the labor force can be attributed to the change in population controls and also seems to be focused among older workers; the prime-age participation rate remains near recent multi-decade highs. As some of the weakness (-31k) was a function of workers at a large healthcare company going on strike, [the author's] preferred interpretation of February payrolls is to add back in that temporary strike-related decline and then average that with January’s stronger-than-expected gains. Doing that gives +32.5k for headline payrolls, on average, over January and February and +45.5k for private, in line with estimates of the breakeven rate of payroll growth. For a deep dive into the February employment report and the labor market outlook more broadly, please see [the author's] recent chartbook: “February jobs report: The CES giveth and the CES taketh away“. As to this week’s data, the February CPI report will almost certainly get top billing. Our expectations are for a 1.0% increase in energy prices to boost headline CPI (+0.27% forecast vs. +0.17% previous) relative to core (+0.24% vs. +0.30%). If our forecasts hit the mark, the year-over-year rate in [headline CPI] should stay roughly the same at 2.40% (vs. 2.39% previous), while the latter would tick down by 4bps to 2.46%. Also of note on the inflation front this week will be Friday’s personal income (+0.4% forecast vs. +0.3% previous) and consumption (+0.1% vs. +0.4%) report for January, which will contain that month’s reading on core PCE, the Fed’s preferred inflation measure. Based on the January CPI and PPI data, we are expecting a 0.42% increase (vs. +0.36%), which would take the year-over-year rate up a tenth to 3.1%. The Fed will have to wait until the morning of their March 18th meeting for the PPI data to get a more complete read on February’s core PCE. Based on our component-level CPI forecasts, our prior expectation is for a 0.18% February gain, which would have the year-over-year rate decline to 2.8%. In terms of the labor market data, ADP’s weekly data on Tuesday covering the week of February 21 st will provide a initial view on net hiring trends beyond February’s survey week. Similarly, Thursday’s jobless claims and Friday’s January JOLTs release will give additional context on gross labor market flows. The remainder of the data this week will help forecasters sharpen their views on current quarter growth. Any revisions in Friday’s second release for Q4 GDP (1.4% vs. 4.4%) will update the baseline from which to judge current quarter growth, while Tuesday’s existing home sales (3.81mn vs. 3.91mn) for February and Thursday’s housing starts (1.325mn vs. 1.404mn) and permits (1.450 vs. 1.455mn) for January will provide an update on the residential sector. We will also get a preliminary look into the health of the factory sector with January’s durable goods orders (+0.4% vs. -1.4% headline [or] +0.4% vs. +0.8% core) on Friday as well. Friday will also see the preliminary release of the University of Michigan survey for March. While we expect a decline in sentiment (55.0 vs. 56.6), due to the recent hostilities in the Middle East, also important for the Fed will be consumers’ inflation expectations. As we wrote last week (“Impact of oil price shocks a ‘frack’- tion of what they once were”), the emergence of shale production in the US has helped to limit the impact of oil price spikes on the economy. Indeed, within the Fed’s FRB [or] US model, a $ 20/bbl increase in oil prices only increases unemployment by about 2bps and has almost no impact on core PCE inflation. However, with downside risk s to the labor market while inflation has run above target for almost five years in a row, the response for the Fed to such a supply shock is not clear. Indeed, looking at the Fed’s prior responses to energy shocks does not yield a regular pattern (see “What does history tell us about the Fed’s response to oil price shocks?”). Sometimes, the Fed emphasized the threat to the inflation side of [the Fed's] dual mandate while other times, [the Fed] sought to protect against any deterioration in the labor market. In the current episode, with inflation expected to be on a downward trajectory, the market could give the Fed some leeway to “look through” another supply-side shock. That said, inflation has been too high for too long, and the latest data calls into question how much disinflation can reasonably be expected, especially if there are increases in measures of inflation expecta tions (e.g., Friday’s Michigan data). On the flipside, growth looks strong but there are concerns on a forward - looking basis, for example, due to the potential for AI to disrupt the labor market. In summary, the February jobs report, as well as January’s, makes it clear that one month’s data should never be taken in isolation. San Francisco Fed President Daly made [the point that one month’s data should never be taken in isolation] in the wake of [the February employment report], noting that while February’s data gives [San Francisco Fed President Daly's] some concern, all the moving parts like the strike and the change in population controls make [the February employment report] harder to interpret. While the more dovish members will likely point to the February employment report as justification for more policy support, the Committee, as a whole will, likely need more data to ascertain the underlying state of the labor market. [The author continues] to expect the Fed to cut rates only once this year, should disinflationary pressures become clear in the second half of 2026. However, if February’s weakness is confirmed in subsequent months (not [the author's] base case), [that February’s weakness is confirmed in subsequent months] could open a path to an earlier reduction.
