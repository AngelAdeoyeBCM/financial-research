# The fight against inflation Sunday Tea with BNP Paribas

Sunday Tea with BNPP: The fight against inflation

## OUR KEY VIEWS

- With an escalation of tensions in the Middle East and renewed tariff pressure, there is unlikely to be much relief on the inflation front.
- [The author expects] central banks to continue to lean hawkish. Accordingly, [the author has] entered into flatteners in US rates.
- [The author likes] owning tactical downside risk protection in an environment of hawkish central banks and high expectations going into the busiest period of US earnings season. Expectations for material inflation relief have proven short lived. The escalation of tensions in the Middle East has put renewed upward pressure on energy prices. And [the renewed upward pressure on energy prices] could get worse still. Indeed, a potential blockade on the Red Sea and Bab el-Mandeb strait could impact another 4.5mbd, which would bring the total oil supply outage to around 19mbd. The only workaround would be for flows to travel North through the Suez canal, however [the only workaround] would still exclude the ‘Very Large Crude Carriers ’ unable to transit the canal, and would add another 3–4 weeks to Asian deliveries. [The author thinks] p rices could quickly return to peak war prices (USD120/bbl) unless a swift détente is achieved. Even worse, European natural gas prices have outperformed crude in the past week to breach the highest levels seen since 2023. [The author] attribute [that European natural gas prices have outperformed crude in the past week to breach the highest levels seen since 2023] to Europe’s need to fill gas storage levels, which currently sit at 54%, around 15% below the five-year average (see Gas storage woes: Molecules, spreads and arbitrage, dated 16 July). Meanwhile, after the expiration of the US administration’s temporary Section 122 tariffs, [the US administration’s temporary Section 122 tariffs] have been largely replicated through other measures. Early last week, the administration used Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930 – a provision that has never been used in this way before – to impose a 50% tariff on billions of dollars of Canadian exports. On Friday, more than 80 countries were hit with tariffs ranging from 10 –12.5%, with the administration issuing such duties using Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 – which allows the president to impose tariffs on foreign countries that engage in unreasonable or discriminatory trade practices. And more Section 301 tariffs ma y be on the way in the coming weeks, as the administration has proposed additional duties on 15 countries and the EU to offset what the White House calls unfair practices in [the 15 countries and the EU's] manufacturing sectors. While the administration’s use of Section 301 is likely much broader than the statute intended, and will likely be eventually challenged in court, the bottom line is that [Section 301 tariffs] are coming back at exactly the wrong time. Continuous shocks to spot-inflation risk further destabilizing inflation expectations. In Europe, front-end rates have accordingly surged to new cycle highs. The ECB’s July meeting delivered a hawkish tone that suggests the Governing Council remains squarely focussed on inflation risks (see ECB: A game of two halves, dated 23 July). [The hawkish tone of the ECB’s July meeting] came despite ECB President Christine Lagarde noting that energy prices were close to the central bank’s June ‘baseline’, that the ECB does not see evidence of second-round effects and that [the ECB] believes inflation expectations are well anchored (for now). [The author interprets] [the hawkish tone of the ECB’s July meeting] in a few ways. First, the ECB is not overly concerned about growth – and the strength of Friday’s PMI data supports [the ECB's] view here. Second, [the hawkish tone of the ECB’s July meeting] once again highlights the ECB’s sensitivity to the level of energy prices. The more elevated e nergy prices are, the lower the degree of evidence required on second-round effects to justify further tightening, in [the author's] view. Third, the ECB is probably comfortable with the market pricing in several more hikes, in that [the market pricing in several more hikes] helps to lean against second-round effects already, while leaving optionality to deliver on [several more hikes] later, if required, or for the market to price out hikes should there be another benign shift in energy prices. [That the ECB is probably comfortable with the market pricing in several more hikes] likely helps explain why Lagarde noted the market has “very well understood” the ECB’s reaction function. Overall, [the ECB’s July meeting] increased [the author's] conviction that the ECB will deliver another hike in September. What comes after September is more uncertain. [The author's] base case for September to be the last hike of the cycle has been predicated on an average oil price in H2 of USD75/bbl. Current futures suggest an average oil price around USD10/bbl higher than [the average oil price in H2 of USD75/bbl], and combined with increases in gas prices, suggest risks are clearly skewed for more hikes than in [the author's] central case. That said, the higher the ECB goes in the short term, the more [the ECB] will have to contemplate cuts eventually as the growth impact will become more prominent – something that the market does not currently price. Calvin Tse Head of Americas Macro Strategy and US Economics This week [the author has] three more major central banks on the docket – the BoE, BoJ, and Fed. [The author expects] risks to lean hawkish. Rising inflation concerns have led to a material repricing in the UK front end ahead of the Bank of England’s July meeting, while domestic political risk has weighed on the GBP (see Bank of England preview: A test of patience, dated 24 July). [The author] ultimately expect the BoE to vote 6 –3 to keep rates on hold at 3.75% while signalling that the decision to keep Bank Rate unchanged is becoming an increasingly close call. Although [the author expects] the formal guidance to remain unchanged, emphasising that the BoE “stands ready to act”, [the author thinks] the committee could lay the groundwork for hikes ([the author expects] one in September) through [the BoE's] updated scenario analysis and the comments of individual MPC members. The Monetary Policy Report should also provide insight into the BoE’s September QT decision – [the author expects] a total envelope of GBP50bn with risks skewed toward fewer active and long - end sales. In Japan, [the author expects] the BoJ to hold the policy rate steady at 1.00 % (see BoJ preview: Anticipating a hawkish hold, dated 23 July). However, w e believe the Outlook Report will suggest the justification for maintaining an accommodative financial environment is diminishing. [That the justification for maintaining an accommodative financial environment is diminishing] could consequently imply an acceleration from the previous semi-annual pace of rate hikes. Given there is also a need to keep the foreign exchange market in check, [the author expects] the BoJ’s communication – including the tone of the Governor’s press conference – to become more hawkish. Finally in the US, [the author was] the first bank to call for Fed rate hikes this year, initially pencilling in the start of the cycle in December (see US: Changing [the author's] Fed call to rate hikes, dated 5 June). [The call for Fed rate hikes this year] is rooted in [the author's] unemployment rate forecast (which reaches 4.0% by year-end), by the timing of the midterm Congressional election, by Fed Chair Kevin Warsh’s ‘task forces ’, and by the fact that the Fed historically has made policy pivots slowly in the absence of an abrupt shock. That said, [the author has] continuously flagged risks toward an earlier move (see June FOMC: Every meeting is live, dated 18 June). The possibility of a surprise hike at [the July FOMC] cannot be ruled out (see July FOMC preview: Whether or not to release the Kraken, dated 23 July). The main argument for a surprise hike is to signal a “regime shift” in monetary policy, build inflation-fighting credibility, and in doing these things, perhaps reduce inflationary momentum. If the Fed hikes this week, [the author finds] it difficult to envision that [the Fed] will not then also hike in September. That said, if the Fed stays on hold as is [the author's] modal expectation, w e think the main policy strategy debate at [the July FOMC] will be about whether to increase the intensity of the language around price stability, and to express a willingness to act, possibly soon, if necessary. The FOMC delivered just such a message in May 1999, for example, just before the start of the 1999 –2000 tightening cycle. [The author thinks] t he statement language could resemble this: “Inflation remains elevated relative to the Committee’s 2 % goal, in part reflecting supply shocks that have driven price increases in certain sectors, including energy. The Committee is prepared to act to deliver price stability.” The risk of more assertive language is because [the author thinks] policymakers’ patience with high and persistent inflation is broadly exhausted. Two FOMC voters (Beth Hammack and Lorie Logan) have openly called for rate hikes for the first time this cycle, and another two (Lisa Cook and Philip Jefferson) signaled that a shift in policy could be needed if inflation did not cool down “soon.” Meanwhile, one of architects of last year’s rate cuts (Christopher Waller) flagged the possibility of a July hike if inflation data was hot. [The author thinks] there is a sizeable and sticky cohort of investors who believe Fed rate hikes will not be delivered, who expect that the FOMC will tolerate elevated inflation, and who interpret current pricing as ‘risk premium’. [The author does] not agree with [the view that Fed rate hikes will not be delivered] and continue to think that the risk of an actual series of hikes (more than ‘one and done’) is very real. Consequently, last week [the author] entered into a 2s10s UST flattener, targeting a move to 20bp. In US equities, this week is crucial for risk sentiment. A third of the S&P 500 by market-cap, including four of the ‘Magnificent -7’ report Q2 earnings. With hawkish central bank risks, a reescalation of tensions in the Middle East, a renewed trade war, and lofty earnings expectations, [the author thinks] it makes sense to hedge downside risks. [The author] pre fer hedging downside in SPX over NDX. [That the author pre fer hedging downside in SPX over NDX] is because NDX implied vols remain at extreme highs, while in SPX, implied and realized correlation is at multi-year lows, suggesting that [SPX] is a cheap instrument to hedge a correlated unwind. In a market correct ion, [the author expects] to see much higher vol and vol-of-vol. [The author's] preferred structures are low delta put spreads on SPY and call spreads on VIX. Indicatively, a SPY Aug26 690/680 Put Spread offers 13x max risk [or] reward. Notably, FX vol continues to lag, despite increased risk on the horizon.
