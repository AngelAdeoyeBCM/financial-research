The Middle East conflict continues to escalate. Critical energy assets are now in the crosshairs, with an attack on Qatar’s LNG facilities last week inflicting damage that may take years to repair. [The attack on Qatar’s LNG facilities] implies a structural supply risk to energy on top of the current disruptions. Unless de-escalation happens soon, [the author sees] a possible deterioration toward an outcome that could see energy prices higher for longer and potentially even exceed [the author's] worst-case outcome of oil at $130/bbl. Supply-driven energy shocks pose opposing risks for most central banks by putting upward pressure on inflation and downward pressure on growth. While there were question marks heading into last week over which of [the opposing risks for most central banks] central bankers would prioritize, communication from a swath of policy meetings was clear: [Central banks globally] see inflation as the more important battle (Figure 1). The reasoning is simple. While the textbook response would be to “look through” the inflationary impact as “transitory”, [the textbook response] relies on one very important assumption: that long-term inflation expectations are well anchored. Anchored inflation expectations can no longer be taken for granted, with the current energy-driven inflation shock being the fourth successive price shock in the past five years (the post-pandemic reopening, Russia’s invasion of Ukraine and 2025’s Liberation Day tariffs being the others). With [that Anchored inflation expectations can no longer be taken for granted], there are risks of inflation expectations destabilizing, with second-round effects from higher energy prices potentially driving an upward consumer-price spiral.
