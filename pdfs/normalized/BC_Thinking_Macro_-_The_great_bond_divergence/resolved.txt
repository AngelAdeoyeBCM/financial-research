# Thinking Macro The great bond divergence

Chinese bonds are now 300bp below 10y USTs. But [the 300bp gap between China and US fixed income] is not due to markets passing judgment on the two countries' fiscal profiles. Instead, China's super-low yields reflect [China's] closed capital account, high savings rate, and lack of investable assets. The US 10y Treasury yield is at around 4.7%. Japan's 30y government bond yield has broken above 4% for the first time. French OATs are trading at spreads to Bunds that reflect genuine fiscal concern. Across the developed world, bond markets are repricing – for higher neutral rates, for fiscal deterioration, for a buyer base that is shifting and shrinking. Against that background, China's 10y government bond yield is 1.70%. Read that again. The world's second-largest economy, with a fiscal profile that – properly measured – is comparable to, or worse than, the United States, is borrowing at yields that the US hasn't seen since the Federal Reserve was pinning rates at zero. And [the 300bp gap between China and US fixed income] is not narrowing. [The 300bp gap between China and US fixed income] is widening. [The 300bp gap between China and US fixed income] is a relatively new phenomenon: until April 2022, 10y US Treasury yields were lower than Chinese bonds for over a decade. [The great bond divergence] between the world's two largest economies is not receiving the attention [the great bond divergence] deserves, in [the author's] view. The conventional explanations – inflation differentials, monetary policy divergence – are real but insufficient. [The author believes] the deeper story is about something more structural: the funding model that underpins each bond market. And that funding model tells [the author] something important about where the vulnerabilities lie – for both China and the West. The obvious answer: inflation The first place most analysts look is inflation. And the gap is real. The Federal Reserve has missed [the Federal Reserve's] 2% inflation target for six consecutive years. Core PCE remains stubbornly above target. Three FOMC members dissented in July in favour of a rate hike. The US is dealing with sticky, broadening inflation that monetary policy has not yet extinguished. China is dealing with the opposite problem. The GDP deflator was negative for 12 consecutive quarters before finally turning positive in Q2 26. CPI averaged 1.1% in Q2. The People's Bank of China continues to maintain an easing bias, using a range of liquidity management tools, including one-day to six-month reverse repos, MLF, and government bond purchases. While the Fed is debating whether to hike, the PBOC is debating how much further to ease. Barclays Bank, Hong Kong So yes, there is an inflation divergence, and [the inflation divergence] explains some of [the 300bp gap between China and US fixed income]. But [the inflation divergence] is not a satisfying answer on its own. China's GDP deflator just turned positive. CPI is recovering. PPI rose 3.6% year-on-year in Q2 – [PPI's] first positive reading since late 2022. If deflation were the primary driver, [the author] would expect Chinese bond yields to be rising as the deflationary impulse fades. [Chinese bond yields] are not [rising as the deflationary impulse fades]. The 10y yield has continued to fall even as the price data has improved. Something else is holding yields down. The deeper puzzle: fiscal The fiscal comparison is even more interesting. Because the fiscal argument, rather than explaining the divergence, deepens the puzzle. The official Chinese government debt figure is around 75% of GDP. That looks manageable.But China's fiscal system relies heavily on off-balance-sheet borrowing through local government financing vehicles; the LGFVs that have become the defining feature of Chinese public finance. The IMF's augmented debt measure, which includes LGFV obligations, puts China's true government debt at approximately 135% of GDP in 2026, projected to reach 146% by 2028. Let [the author] put that in context. The United States – the economy whose fiscal trajectory [the author] described in Not yet as unsustainable – has debt held by the public at roughly 100% of GDP. France, whose arithmetic is also challenging (1.5-2% nominal growth against a 5% deficit), is at 115%. Japan, the perennial outlier, is above 250%. China, at 135% on an augmented basis, is closer to the United States than Japan – but deteriorating faster. The IMF projects a 11% increase in just two years. Local governments, which historically derived 40% of [local governments'] revenue from land sales to property developers, have seen that revenue source collapse alongside the housing market. Property prices are down roughly 40% from [property prices'] 2021 peak. LGFV debt alone stands at approximately ¥76trn – $11.3trn – roughly half of GDP. If one were looking only at the fiscal profile, China's bond yields should be higher than the West's, not lower. A deteriorating balance sheet, a collapsing property market that has gutted local government revenue, and off-balance-sheet liabilities that the IMF estimates at nearly 60% of GDP – [China's fiscal profile] is not the profile of a 1.70% borrower. Yet here [the author is]. The real answer: who buys the bonds [The great bond divergence] is not primarily about inflation. [The great bond divergence] is not about fiscal sustainability. [The great bond divergence] is about the funding model – who buys each country's bonds, and why. Start with the West. The US Treasury market depends on external buyers. Foreign central banks, sovereign wealth funds, Japanese life insurers, European asset managers, global reserve managers[, The US Treasury market depends on external buyers]. For decades, [Foreign central banks, sovereign wealth funds, Japanese life insurers, European asset managers, global reserve managers] absorbed the growing supply of US government debt because the dollar was the world's reserve currency, because US Treasuries were the global risk-free asset, and because – in Japan's case – domestic yields offered very little. That buyer base is eroding. Japanese investors sold tens of billions of US Treasuries in the first quarter of 2026 alone. Japan's 10y JGB yield has risen to a 28-year high – and the incentive to hold foreign bonds with currency risk and no matching liabilities is diminishing by the month. Last year was the first in which the share of global FX reserves in gold surpassed that in USTs. The marginal buyer of US government debt is changing, and the new one demands a higher yield. France and the UK face similar dynamics (though France has a 'stickier' domestic buyer baser). [France's] bonds are priced in global capital markets, subject to the discipline of international investors, and exposed to the same forces – rising global yields, a shifting buyer base, and growing fiscal scrutiny from rating agencies and asset allocators who have alternatives. China's bond market operates under a fundamentally different model. [China's bond market] is funded almost entirely by domestic savings. China's gross domestic savings rate is 43% of GDP. Total bank deposits (including those of households, corporates, governments and NBFIs) stand at ¥346trn as of July 2026. Bloomberg reported in April that China's "$51 trillion savings" were driving bond outperformance even during the Iran war, when every other bond market was selling off. Approximately ¥75trn – over $10trn — in household time deposits are maturing this year alone. That money has to go somewhere. And here is the critical point: [the ¥75trn in household time deposits] cannot easily leave. China maintains capital controls that limit how much domestic savings can flow abroad. [The ¥75trn in household time deposits] is, in effect, trapped inside the Chinese financial system. Property — which absorbed an enormous share of household wealth for two decades — has fallen 40% and is no longer favoured. Equities had a rally, but Beijing has actively cooled speculative inflows and the market remains volatile. Wealth management products and bank deposits offer diminishing returns as the PBOC cuts rates. So [the ¥75trn in household time deposits] has flowed into government bonds. Not because China's fiscal profile is strong – [China's fiscal profile] is not[, the ¥75trn in household time deposits has flowed into government bonds]. Not because inflation is low[, the ¥75trn in household time deposits has flowed into government bonds]. Rather, a $51trn domestic savings pool, constrained by capital controls and facing a lack of attractive alternatives, has nowhere else to go. [China's bond market] is not making a statement about China's creditworthiness. [China's bond market] is reflecting the reality of a closed financial system with too much savings. And [China's bond market] is signaling that neither real nor financial assets in China are attractive enough to absorb [the $51trn domestic savings pool]. As a result, [the $51trn domestic savings pool] end up in the bond market, even at very low yields. The Japan precedent [That China's bond market is funded almost entirely by domestic savings] is not without historical parallel. Japan has maintained government debt above 200% of GDP for over a decade without experiencing a bond market crisis. The explanation is the same: domestic savings fund the government. Japanese households and institutions (especially the BoJ) hold the vast majority of JGBs. The money is domestic, the liabilities are in yen, and the savings rate – while now declining – sustained the model for decades. A common argument is that Japan owes money to itself. China is running the same playbook. [China's true government debt] is large and growing, but [China's true government debt] is funded internally, denominated in renminbi, and supported by a savings rate that is far higher any Western economy. As long as the domestic savings pool is large enough to absorb the issuance, and as long as capital controls prevent that pool from leaking abroad, [China's bond market] can remain well-bid even as the fiscal arithmetic deteriorates. [That China's bond market can remain well-bid even as the fiscal arithmetic deteriorates] is why [the great bond divergence] persists. The West's bond markets are subject to external discipline – foreign buyers who are able to leave when the fiscal story worsens. China's bond market is subject to internal capture – domestic savings that cannot leave even if [the $51trn domestic savings pool] wanted to. Both are telling the truth about their respective economies. They are just answering different questions. The West's bond market is answering: what yield do global investors demand to hold this debt? China's bond market is answering: where else can $51trn of trapped savings go? But here is where the vulnerability lies The Japan parallel is reassuring in the short term and troubling in the long term. Because the model works until the savings rate starts to fall. Japan's domestic funding model is now showing cracks for the first time in a generation. JGB yields are rising – the 30y above 4%, the 10y at a 28y high – in part because the Bank of Japan is stepping back, but also because the demographic structure that generated the savings surplus is eroding. Japan's population has been declining since 2008. The savings rate has fallen. The captive buyer base is shrinking. China is perhaps 10 to 15 years behind Japan on that demographic curve – but the curve is steepening faster. China's population has declined for four consecutive years. Fewer than 8mn babies were born in 2025 – the lowest in decades. The working-age population is contracting. The 4-2-1 problem – one child expected to support two parents and four grandparents – is no longer theoretical. As China's population ages, the savings rate will likely fall. Retirees draw down savings rather than accumulate them. Healthcare and pension costs rise. The domestic pool of capital available to fund government debt shrinks precisely as [China's true government debt] continues to grow. China's augmented debt is projected to reach 146% of GDP by 2028. If the savings rate begins to decline in earnest over the same period, the model that keeps yields at 1.70% comes under pressure. [That the model that keeps yields at 1.70% comes under pressure] is not to suggest that a Chinese bond crisis is imminent. [The $51trn domestic savings pool] is vast, the capital controls are firm, and the PBOC retains significant tools to manage yields. But the funding model that insulates China's bond market from global forces is a waning asset. [The funding model that insulates China's bond market from global forces] works today. [The funding model that insulates China's bond market from global forces] will work less well in five years. And in 10 years, China may face the same question that Japan is confronting now – what happens when the savers can no longer fund the borrowers? What the divergence is really telling [the author] The great bond divergence is not a curiosity. [The great bond divergence] is a signal. [The great bond divergence] is telling [the author] that the global fixed income market has fractured into two regimes with fundamentally different dynamics. In one regime – the West – bond markets are repricing for higher neutral rates, fiscal deterioration, and a shrinking external buyer base. Yields are rising because the marginal buyer demands [a higher yield]. In the other regime – China – bond markets are rallying despite comparable fiscal deterioration because a trapped domestic savings glut has nowhere else to go. Yields are falling because the marginal buyer has no alternative. Both regimes carry risk. The West's risk is immediate and visible: who buys the bonds if Japan repatriates, as foreign official holdings fall, as deficits grow faster than nominal GDP? But [the West's risk] should also impose [the West's risk's] own discipline; Western bond markets (eventually) should keep government profligacy in check. China's risk is deferred and structural: what happens when the demographics turn, the savings rate falls, and the captive buyer base can no longer absorb the issuance? For global investors, the implication is that one cannot treat "bonds" as a single asset class any longer. The forces driving US Treasuries, JGBs, OATs, and Chinese government bonds are not just different in degree – [the forces driving US Treasuries, JGBs, OATs, and Chinese government bonds] are different in kind. A view on global fixed income now requires a view on funding models, demographic trajectories, and capital account regimes, not just inflation and central bank policy. The 300bp gap between China and US fixed income is not a mispricing. [The 300bp gap between China and US fixed income] is two bond markets telling two different truths about two different systems. In the West, the external buyers are leaving and yields must rise to attract new ones. In China, the domestic savers are trapped and yields can stay low until the savers themselves start to disappear. One divergence is cyclical. The other is demographic. Both come with their own sets of problems. Investors shouldn't mistake China's low bond yields as a sign of economic health. Rather, [China's low bond yields] are indicative of an economy in which savings cannot find enough attractive assets to invest it.
