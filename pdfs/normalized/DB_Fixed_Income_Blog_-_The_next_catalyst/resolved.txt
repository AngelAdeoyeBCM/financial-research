# Fixed Income Blog - The next catalyst

Rates Fixed Income Blog Date 16 October 2026

[The author] turned neutral duration a month ago (around 5% on UST10y). The decision was driven by the fact that front-end pricing materially exceeded what [the author] could justify (around 2% in real terms, up to 4.25% in nominal terms for the Fed). At the time, [the author] chose to focus instead on cross-market trades (short USD30y vs. JPY30y), as relative external positions were not adequately priced. [The author has] also maintained [the author's] higher DB term premia trades (beta-hedged steepeners), which continue to have structural medium-term upside. In this note, [the author discusses] potential catalysts for considering outright duration trades (bullish or bearish):

1. the forthcoming lending surveys,
2. fiscal policy,
3. AI capex,
4. supply shocks and
5. on a more tactical basis, seasonality. As it stands, c yclical and tactical factors (the impact of monetary policy tightening, implications of the midterm elections on US fiscal policy, pressures on the French budget, downside risks to US AI capex and seasonality) are potentially supportive of a short-term retracement in yields. In contrast, the current geopolitical environment remains structurally supportive of higher rates over the longer term, via upside risks to AI capex in Europe to ensure strategic autonomy and the potential for new negative supply shocks. Some context on Fed pricing Estimates of neutral are time-varying, have wide confidence intervals and can be substantially revised over time. That being said, [the author has] long argued that the drivers behind the post-GFC decline in equilibrium rates had reversed. In fact, the r* estimate using the bond [or] equity correlation has been consistent for well over a year with a neutral rate around 2% in real terms, i.e. 4 -4.25% in nominal terms, depending on how generous one wants to be with the Fed meeting [the Fed's] inflation target. Given the volatility and uncertainty in r* estimates, the 6-month average is the preferred measure. [The 6-month average] currently stands slightly above 2%. However, it is worth noting that the monthly estimate is closer to 2.5%, i.e. 4.5-4.75% in nominal terms. Thus, the focus is on whether there is evidence supporting upside risks to [the author's] current base case. Polling averages and prediction markets currently expect Democrats to take control of the House and (more narrowly) the Senate at the forthcoming midterm elections. For now, expectations remain that a divided government will not result in tighter fiscal policy. Rather, some see upside risks to defence spending following the Iran war. Thus, any indication of the likely implications of the midterm elections for the fiscal trajectory will be relevant for the medium-term outlook for rates. In Europe, the current market pressure on French debt could result in a more drastic fiscal tightening than currently expected. Finally, as [the author] recently noted, China is one of the major countries with significant policy space. Therefore, there is structural upside to China’s fiscal policy. AI capex Expectations for AI capex in the US have been revised upwards and there is so far no evidence of a slowdown. However, as [the author has] argued in a recent note, it is plausible that the “winner takes all” environment results in (rational) aggregate overinvestment. Moreover, the forthcoming midterm elections could result in greater political hurdles for the construction of data centres. Thus, there is the potential for downside revisions to current AI capex programmes in the US. Conversely, there are arguably significant upside risks to AI capex outside the US. Indeed, earlier this year several European governments moved towards reducing dependencies on US tech following US pressures regarding Greenland and the ICC precedent. It stands to reason that keeping control over data is also likely to be a key sovereignty issue for European governments. [That keeping control over data is also likely to be a key sovereignty issue for European governments] creates upside risks to AI-related capex in Europe. Interestingly, lower AI capex in the US and higher AI capex in Europe would support a narrowing of the gap between US and European rates, very much against current market consensus (and to a lesser extent [the author's] existing USD-JPY cross-market trade). Supply shocks The post-GFC low-rate environment was supported by the positive supply shock generated by shale oil. [The positive supply shock generated by shale oil] led to a notable decline in long-term inflation expectations and the perception of inflation risks. [The positive supply shock generated by shale oil] also enabled significantly more aggressive monetary policy easing, which culminated during Covid. Since then, however, the world economy has been subject to a succession of negative supply shocks: Covid, the Ukraine war, tariffs and the Iran war. While Covid may be an isolated one-off event, the other three shocks are the result of the current geopolitical environment. Moreover, there are arguably more potential flashpoints affecting critical supply chains in Asia or elsewhere. An environment that is structurally conducive to repeated supply shocks will cement higher inflation expectations and therefore have an impact on equilibrium rates and monetary policy. Conversely, a resolution in Ukraine or Iran is likely to be akin to a positive supply shock, which would be supportive of lower rates relative to current market pricing. For now, though, the risks appear to be skewed towards further escalation in the Iran war after the US midterm elections. Tactical consideration: seasonality Leaving aside the structural drivers, from a purely tactical perspective, seasonals tend to favour lower term premia and lower yields from early November to mid - December. While one should be wary of seasonal patterns being the product of randomness, the late-autumn pattern (which reverses in late December) is one of the more robust seasonal signals. Bottom line: cyclical downside risks vs. structural upside risks As it stands, cyclical and tactical factors (the impact of monetary policy tightening, implications of the midterm elections on US fiscal policy, pressures on the French budget, downside risks to US AI capex and seasonality) are potentially supportive of a short-term retracement in yields. In contrast, the current geopolitical environment remains structurally supportive of higher rates over the longer term, via upside risks to AI capex in Europe to ensure strategic autonomy and the potential for new negative supply shocks.
