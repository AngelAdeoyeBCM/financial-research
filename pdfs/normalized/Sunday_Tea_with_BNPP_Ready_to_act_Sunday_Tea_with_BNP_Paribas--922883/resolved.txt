Must-read Deep Dive published last week: Global Outlook Spring 2026: Looking beyond the war The Middle East remains caught in a no man’s land, without war, without peace and (crucially) without energy flows through the Strait of Hormuz. The issue for energy markets is that balances deteriorate further each day [the Strait of Hormuz] stays closed. With most buffers exhausted, onshore oil inventories are declining fast. [That onshore oil inventories are declining fast] leads [the author] to expect crude prices to keep going up before coming down. Despite higher gasoline prices and elevated uncertainty associated with the Middle East conflict, the US economy has remained notably resilient. Data over recent weeks show ed strong employment conditions, gains in leading indicators and healthy consumer spending. [The resilience that the US economy has remained notably resilient] is partly supported by fiscal stimulus, courtesy of tax changes made in the OB BBA. Additional tax refunds have come to roughly $50bn year to date versus 2025 (Figure 1), more than offsetting the extra ~$10bn/month consumers are paying at the pump. Fig. 1: Very robust tax refunds to start 2026 Sources: Treasury Department, Macrobond, BNP Paribas Meanwhile, the corporate sector remains very strong. With Q1 earnings season under way and a quarter of S&P 500 names having already reported, 80% beat EPS headline expectations. [The author has] thus been bullish on the equity market in an environment of strong fundamentals and light positioning. This week will be big for earnings: half of the S&P 500 (by market cap) will report, including five of the ‘Magnificent 7’. [The author remains] constructive on US equities given strong positive EPS revisions, corporate buybacks rekindling again, and Q2 typically being a seasonally strong period for retail inflows (Figure 2). However, positioning has built up rapidly, suggesting that the velocity of inflows is likely to slow relative to recent weeks. As such, [the author suggests] investors switch from long gamma to cheaper and lower theta upside structures.. 26/04/2026 2 Fig. 2: US equities are entering a seasonally stronger part of the year Sources: Bloomberg, BNP Paribas With the economy on a solid footing, financial conditions loose and no signs of inflation returning to target soon, [the author expects] the Fed to move to a symmetric policy bias at [the Fed's] meeting this week, indicating that the next move could be either a rate hike or a cut. Looking ahead, while [the author's] base case is for rates to remain on hold into 2027, [the author sees] a growing tail risk that policymakers could consider hiking in the coming months if the Hormuz closure persists and US labor data remains resilient. [The author's] criterion for a rate hike is a combination of unemployment falling toward 4% (a level at which wage-price amplification becomes easier to envision) and long-term inflation expectations increasing in consumer surveys similar to the 2025 rise. Labor data from both the BLS and ADP suggests that hiring is picking up and is above current estimates of labor force growth, posing a risk that the unemployment rate may decline in coming months. [The author] will take careful note in Chair Powell’s press conference of the extent to which [Chair Powell] foams the runway for hikes should this fact pattern of declining unemployment and rising inflation come to pass. Meanwhile, the Department of Justice announced on Friday that [the Department of Justice] had dropped [the Department of Justice's] criminal investigation of Powell. [That the Department of Justice had dropped the Department of Justice's criminal investigation of Powell] opened the door for Kevin Warsh to be confirmed as [Chair Powell's] successor, taking over the chair position when Powell’s term ends next month. Warsh’s 21 April confirmation testimony left intact [the author's] two core views on the leadership change. The first of [the two core views on the leadership change] is that regardless of who succeeds Powell, monetary policy will continue to be determined by the economic outlook and by the Fed’s existing policy goals and strategy. Second, [the author believes] Warsh is committed to 2% inflation over time and to maintaining the Fed’s institutional credibility. Fundamentals suggest the USD should be trading well: the US is a net energy exporter in an environment of rising prices, recent data has consistently surprised to the upside and American equity markets have outperformed global peers in the last month. Yet the greenback can’t catch a bid. [The author thinks] [that the greenback can’t catch a bid] partly reflects renewed interest by global investors in diversifying away from the USD against the backdrop of the Middle East conflict. [Renewed interest by global investors in diversifying away from the USD] is probably outweighing cyclical inflow, keeping the USD under pressure. Accordingly, [the author remains] short USD, via longs in AUDUSD. In Europe, investors will be paying close attention to how the ECB and BoE respond to the energy price shock. [The author expects] the ECB to keep rates on hold, while retaining full optionality to hike at a later point should the data warrant [a hike at a later point]. Absent a significant and sustained fall in energy prices (not [the author's] central case), [the author] ultimately expect the data to support a 25bp rise at the June meeting. In the UK, a string of stronger-than-expected releases has added fuel to the fire for the BoE to deliver a hike, evidenced by last week’s steep repricing of front-end rates. [The author] ultimately expect [the BoE] to err on the side of caution, voting 7-2 to stay on hold, with two members casting the first votes for a rise. However, [the author anticipates] a distinctly hawkish tone – demonstrated through higher inflation projections, broadly unchanged guidance and the dissent in favour of hiking – that leaves the door open to tighten in the future. [The author continues] to expect two 25bp rate hikes this year, starting in June. The BoJ also has an important decision this week. Considering the ongoing uncertainty surrounding the Middle East conflict and the apparent lack of government support, [the author expects] the policy rate to remain unchanged. However, [the BoJ's important decision] will likely be accompanied by multiple dissenting votes and hawkish framing, as the policy rate remains deeply negative in real terms. [The author anticipates] a rate rise in June and further hikes roughly every four to five months thereafter. In EM, central banks are also responding to the high energy price environment. Indonesia increased the seven-day repo OMO facility rate by 25bp last week, while the Philippines hiked [the Philippines'] policy rate. This week the focus will turn to Latam with central banks in Brazil, Chile and Colombia all scheduled to meet. In Brazil, [the author expects] a 25bp rate cut from the BCB and less transparency in forward guidance as the balance of risks becomes more negative. In Chile, [the author has] revised [the author's] BCCh call to look for a 5% year-end policy rate, and [the author expects] 50bp of hikes at coming meetings. While April is probably too early to start tightening, [the author likes] to be paid 2y CAMARA spread over SOFR into the meeting. In Colombia, whether the BanRep board will be able to convene remains in doubt as the participation of the finance minister (who chairs the meeting) is unclear. Should the meeting take place, [the author expects] a hike of at least 50bp. Finally, [the author] released [the author's] Spring Global Outlook on Friday. [The author believes] the duration and extent of the shock from the Middle East conflict will have a lasting impact on the global economy, regardless of events over the coming days and weeks. Accordingly, [the author now expects] lower GDP growth, higher inflation and more hawkish central banks compared with [the author's] view when 2026 began. However, [the author thinks] [the Spring Global Outlook] is a story of resilience dented, but not derailed. Strong pre-conflict growth momentum and structural drivers such as AI and defence spending [the author sees] offering countervailing support.
