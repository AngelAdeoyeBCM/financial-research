# Focus Europe - Spain snap election-Accelerated change

Economics Focus Europe Date 9 October 2026

Spain will hold a snap general election on 29 November after the minority PSOE government’s parliamentary defeat on a housing bill exposed [the minority PSOE government's] increasingly fragile position. Opinion polls imply a change of government, with the PP and Vox currently on course for a comfortable majority, with a new administration likely to take office in early 2027. The near-term macroeconomic impact should be limited: growth remains robust and a stable majority could reduce policy uncertainty and end the budgetary paralysis of recent years. Over the medium term, a key question will be whether tighter migration policy weighs on labour-force growth. What just happened? On Monday 5 October, PM Pedro Sánchez dissolved parliament and called a snap general election for 29 November, nine months ahead of schedule. The immediate catalyst was Congress’s rejection on Friday 2 October of two housing decrees, which included measures to protect vulnerable tenants by imposing restrictions on landlords and investors. [Congress’s rejection of two housing decrees] came amid nationwide protests over housing affordability. PP and Vox voted against both decrees, as did Junts [voted against both decrees], which backed the Sánchez 2023 investiture but broke with the government in 2025. [Congress’s rejection of two housing decrees] underlined the fragility of Sánchez minority coalition in a deeply fragmented parliament. The government has struggled to pass legislation throughout the term – [the minority PSOE government] has failed to approve a new budget since 2023 – and relations with several of [the minority PSOE government's] parliamentary allies have deteriorated. Corruption allegations involving senior figures around the Socialist party and this summer’s immigration crisis in Ceuta have added to the political pressure. With housing consistently ranking high among Spaniard’s main concerns, the rejection of the housing decrees offers Sánchez a rare window of opportunity. [Housing] is one of the clearest issues on which the government can draw a contrast with the opposition: the PSOE favour greater public intervention, rent controls and a larger stock of social housing, while the PP pu t more emphasis on supply-side measures and tax incentives. Polls point to a change of government Although Sanchez has defied expectations in the past, the electoral landscape looks more challenging this time. The right bloc clearly outpolls the left, with polls suggesting PP and Vox together would clear the 176-seat absolute majority with a comfortable margin (Figure 1, Figure 2). A closer look at the parties shows how the balance has shifted since 2023 (Figure 3, Figure 4):

- The centre-right PP remains comfortably in first place, polling at 31-32% of vote share, roughly in line with [the centre-right PP's] 2023 result. [The centre-right PP] has benefited from the government’s weakened political position but has struggled to turn [the government’s weakened political position] into significant gains of [the centre-right PP's] own, as much of the anti-government vote has gone to Vox. Polls give the PP no more than 150 seats on [the centre-right PP's] own, which would leave [the centre-right PP] reliant on Vox to govern. In the four regions where the two parties have governed together, Vox’s influence has largely been confined to a narrow set of portfolios. At the national level, however, Vox’s greater electoral weight could give [Vox] more leverage over policy.
- Support for the centre-left PSOE has been eroding. Having won 32% of the vote in July 2023 elections, [the centre-left PSOE] is now polling roughly 7 points behind the PP, leaving Sanchez with a substantially weaker electoral base.
- Vox has strengthened [Vox's] position considerably, polling around 18 -19% vs 12% in 2023. [Vox] has capitalised on hardening views on immigration. Projections of 60 -70 seats would almost double [Vox's] 2023 tally of 33. Although not anti-euro, [Vox] would likely use [Vox's] leverage to push for a tougher line on immigration and national sovereignty, and resist further EU integration.
- The political space to the left of the PSOE is fragmented. Sumar, the junior coalition party, which ran on a joint list with Podemos in 2023 (obtaining 12% of the vote share), now polls at 5 -7%. Following Yolanda Diaz’s withdrawal from the electoral race e arlier this year, [Sumar] is trying to regroup left forces under a new “Frente Amplio”, which is due to unveil [the Frente Amplio's] political roadmap and lead candidate on 17 October. Podemos (currently 3% of the vote in polls) has left the door open to joining the alliance but is calling for open primaries, a proposal Sumar has so far rejected in favour of an agreement among the participating parties. The vote will take place on Sunday 29 November. The official campaign will run for two weeks, starting on 13 November. The formation of a new government is likely to slip into Q1 -27. The new parliament will convene on 23 December. Only then, consultations between the King and party leaders would take place. If a candidate looks able to gather a majority in the lower house, the King would then propose that candidate to form a government. There is no specific deadline for [that the King would then propose that candidate to form a government] to occur. The nominated candidate must then win an absolute majority in a first investiture vote. If that fails, a second vote would be held 48 hours later, in which only a simple majority would be needed to form a government. Should the proposed candidate fail that second vote, the lower house has two months from the first vote to elect a PM; otherwise, parliament is dissolved and a new election is held 54 days later. Until a new government takes office, Sánchez cabinet remains in a caretaker capacity. After the July 2023 election, PP leader Feijoo’s investiture failed in late September and it was almost four months later that Sánchez became Prime Minister. A clear PP-Vox majority could shorten [the formation of a new government] considerably into a few weeks, but even a clean result would be unlikely to produce a new government before early-to-mid January. Strong growth has continued in 2026. GDP expanded by 0.6% qoq in Q1-26 and 0.7% in Q2, compared to 0.3% qoq in both quarters for the euro area excluding Ireland. Investment continues to be supported by the deployment of NGEU funds. The latest business surveys points to continued robust activity heading into the autumn, with services PMI at [services PMI's] strongest level since early 2023. [The author projects] annual GDP growth of 2.5% in 2026 with several headwinds likely to slow the pace of expansion next year. Elevated inflation should weigh on household real incomes while tighter monetary conditions will dampen investment. At the same time, the growth impulse from NGEU funds will fade as [NGEU funds] concludes. [The author therefore expects] growth to ease to 2.1% in 2027. Beyond the near term, three key themes are likely to shape the direction of the Spanish economy. Public finances. Spain’s fiscal starting point is stronger than in recent years and better than that of most of [Spain's] peers (Figure 8). Supported by robust economic growth and the fact that the underlying budget has been frozen by political deadlock since 2023, the deficit has narrowed from 3.2% of GDP in 2024 to 2.4% in 2025. Public debt has also followed a gradual downward trajectory, falling from 105% of GDP to around 100%. The still elevated debt ratio explains why Spain remains relatively high on [the author's] index of fiscal vulnerability (Figure 8). On the positive side, Spain is trending lower and in 2026 the index is beginning to fall below the pre-pandemic level, un like France and Italy (France has seen the largest increase since pre-pandemic). Before the outbreak of the Iran war, [the author] anticipated a broadly neutral fiscal stance in 2026, with the deficit remaining around 2.5%. The initial energy-support package, worth 0.3% of GDP, was largely expected to be financed through budgetary savings. However, the additional measures approved in late September could increase fiscal pressure. Upfront tax cuts from a new government could add to [fiscal pressure], slowing the pace of debt reduction unless offset by lower spending or stronger growth. Spain is not at immediate risk of re-entering an Excessive Deficit Procedure (EDP), as the deficit is expected to remain below 3% of GDP. Housing will be a central campaign issue. The underlying problem is insufficient supply. Fewer than 100,000 homes have been completed each year over the past five years (Figure 10), well short of household formation. The Bank of Spain estimates a cumulative shortfall of 750,000 homes over 2021-2025 and expects the gap to exceed one million by 2028. As a result, house prices have continued to rise rapidly. Both the PP and Vox favour supply-side solutions, with the PP pledging to facilitate the construction of one million homes over four years. Migration. The main medium-term downside risk comes from migration policy. Both parties want to reduce irregular migration and tighten border controls. The PP proposes a points-based visa system and temporary migration schemes for specific sectors. Vox takes a hard er line, advocating broader repatriation of irregular immigrants and national priority in access to welfare and public services. Spain’s economic outperformance has relied heavily on a strong labour market, with employment being buoyed by inward migration (Figure 11). A more restrictive policy could significantly reduce migration flows, worsening labour shortages and constraining growth in sectors like tourism, construction and agriculture.
