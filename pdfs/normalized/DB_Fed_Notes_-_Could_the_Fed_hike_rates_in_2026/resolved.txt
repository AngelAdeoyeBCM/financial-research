# Fed Notes - Could the Fed hike rates in 2026

Economics Fed Notes Date 13 March 2026

Yesterday, the 2-year Treasury yield jumped about 10bps to 3.75%, [the 2-year Treasury yield's] highest level since late August and roughly 20bps above the effective fed funds rate. The recent repricing has reflected a significant re-think of the Fed outlook this year in response to recent events in the Middle East, with the market moving from [approximately] 60bps of cuts priced two weeks ago to [approximately] 20bps at present. Most strikingly, the market has moved to price a roughly 25% probability of a rate hike this year. With global central banks shifting in a hawkish direction, a question that was almost unthinkable two weeks ago is now being more heavily debated: Could the Fed raise rates in 2026? From a fundamental perspective, [the author thinks] there are two preconditions for a rate hike this year. (Note that the following only applies to hike conditions for 2026; there are scenarios where the Fed could hike at later dates simply because inflation stay s at elevated levels and does not show signs of returning to target.) First, downside risks to the labor market need to be eliminated. The past two years have shown that the Fed is responsive to weak labor market conditions and downside risks even in the face of elevated inflation. Hikes likely require something more, though. In particular, the labor market likely needs to re-tighten – e.g., with the unemployment rate falling back towards 4%, quits rising, and wages accelerating, among other conditions – and, therefore, return as a source of inflationary pressures. Second, core PCE likely needs to show evidence of accelerating noticeably above 3%. Furthermore, the source of the rise cannot be readily identifiable as a shock that is likely to prove transitory even in the absence of tighter monetary policy (e.g., tariffs or oil). Of course, there may be thresholds for core inflation where the source of the rise is down-weighted. Nonetheless, to get to hikes the Fed would have to discard a strongly held narrative that a disinflationary trend is in place and that once one-off shocks dissipate inflation will be close to 2%. Clearly, realization of the first point (a re-tightening labor market) would be one reason to reconsider fundamental assumptions about the disinflation trend. Matthew Luzzetti, Ph.D. As always, inflation expectations feature as a wildcard in [the two preconditions for a rate hike this year]. If measures of long-run inflation expectations began to rise noticeably, it would lower the thresholds on the labor market and spot inflation required for the Fed to consider a hik e. [The author thinks] most Fed officials are inclined to discount a rise in long-run inflation expectations that is isolated to the University of Michigan survey (indeed [the University of Michigan survey] has been above 3% for nearly two years). To become concerned, market-based measures (e.g., 5y5y breakevens and inflation swaps) would likely need to rise meaningfully, and [or] or the signal from University of Michigan would need to be confirmed by other survey-based indicators (e.g., NY Fed’s consumer survey). How likely do these conditions appear at present? [The author continues] to think [the two preconditions for a rate hike this year]’re unlikely. The labor market remains in a long-prevailing state of low-hiring and low-firing. [The author's] recent work demonstrated that the decline in the hiring rate since 2019 is equally explained by cyclical conditions (i.e., sensitivity to monetary policy) and weak labor supply (i.e., immigration policy) (see “Disentangling the forces behind weak hiring”). Thus, even if constrained labor supply is leading to tighter conditions in immigration-sensitive sectors (see construction), the ongoing oil price shock should dampen demand and hiring intentions, preventing the labor market from reaccelerating. Indeed, FRB [or] US simulations show that oil price shocks tend to lead to higher unemployment (see “Impact of oil price shocks a ‘frack’-tion of what they once were”). Consequently, [the author sees] the more likely path towards rate hikes as operating through accelerating wages and prices alongside a noticeable rise in inflation expectations that forces the Fed’s hand. Market-based measures of long-run inflation expectations tend not to react to supply-driven oil price shocks; t he remarkable stability of [market-based measures of long-run inflation expectations] in response to [the ongoing oil price shock] is a case in point. One final consideration for rate hikes this year is the coming turnover in Fed leadership. [The author has] not believed that Kevin Warsh would be able to quickly push the Fed to cut rates this year. However, [Kevin Warsh] could have more influence in maintaining a status quo policy, e.g., by keeping policy on hold rather than raising rates in borderline cases. At the margin, [the author therefore views] changes in Fed composition as further leaning against rate hikes this year. Looking at market pricing, OIS for the December meeting are currently ~ 3.41, or 2 3bps of cuts, having retraced slightly from pricing at the close yesterday. Alongside the move in forwards, prices of out-of-the-money put options on SOFRZ6 have risen sharply. Based on pricing at yesterday’s close, the implied probability of a Fed rate hike this year was just above 25%, as shown in the chart to the right below.
