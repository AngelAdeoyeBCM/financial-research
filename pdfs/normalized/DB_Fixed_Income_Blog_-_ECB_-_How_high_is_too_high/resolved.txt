# Fixed Income Blog - ECB - How high is too high

Rates Fixed Income Blog Date 27 August 2026

[The author's] economists and the current yield forecast assume a 2.5% terminal ECB rate. Does that mean that the 2.75% priced by the market is an obvious overshoot? From both bottom-up and top-down perspectives, the answer is no. From a bottom-up perspective, there is so far no evidence that monetary policy is too tight. From a top-down perspective, the USD-EUR spread priced by the market is plausible given the structural upside to European fiscal policy. Bottom-up: No evidence that monetary policy is too tight Hiking in response to a negative supply shock is, by definition, uncomfortable. [Hiking in response to a negative supply shock] has led many to conclude that the ECB is repeating the policy mistake of 2011. A couple of months ago, [the author] argued that the comparison with 2011 was misplaced for three fundamental reasons. Firstly, the private sector is more resilient than [the private sector] was in the aftermath of the GFC. Secondly, intra-EU divergences are smaller, as peripheral countries have significantly improved [peripheral countries'] external positions. Thirdly, on a forward-looking basis, fiscal policy is likely to ease rather than tighten, as [fiscal policy] did [tighten] post-2011. The data and market pricing have generally supported [the author's] view. Since then, the market has added [approximately] 25bp of additional tightening by the end of 2027. Yet, the eurozone composite PMI has risen by [approximately] 4pt. [The author's] economists’ eurozone surprise index has reached a multi-year high. German GDP has been revised up. Bank lending conditions have tightened less than expected. The credit impulse has remained resilient. Last, but not least, one of the most cyclical indices, European banks, has outperformed [European banks'] US peers. Top-down: The USD-EUR terminal rate differential is not particularly tight The USD-EUR terminal rate differential (proxied by Dec-27 SOFR versus €STR) is currently around 130bp. Is that too tight? Historically, the USD-EUR r* differential has ranged from -50bp to 250bp (HLW estimates). [The USD-EUR r* differential] tends to be tight during financial crises, as the US is more prone to boom [or] bust cycles, or when fiscal policy is relatively more expansive in Europe, as during the GFC, the 2001 dot-com bubble and the 1990s double whammy of German reunification and the S&L crisis in the US. Conversely, [the USD-EUR r* differential] tends to be wide during a capex boom, usually US-driven, or when the US has comparatively more expansive fiscal policy, as during the Reagan years. Taken together, it would be reasonable for the r* differential to be above historical averages, but not at the wides. The current spread of 130bp falls within such a range. Moreover, one could argue that the medium-term risks are towards a tighter spread, given that US capex booms tend to be followed by busts. In contrast, the shift in German fiscal policy is likely to last for many years, while there are upside risks to AI capex in Europe.
