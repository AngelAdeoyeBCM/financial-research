# US Economic Notes - What you need to know for the week ahead

Economics US Economic Notes Date 22 March 2026

Commentary for Monday: Iran war headlines are likely to continue to dominate investor sentiment as this week’s economic calendar will be relatively light. At the time of writing, only three Fed officials are scheduled to appear this week, beginning with Vice Chair Jefferson’s outlook speech on Thursday. As [the author] noted last week (see “Fed Notes: March FOMC recap: No (core goods) inflation progress, no rate cut “), the message from last week’s FOMC meeting was consistent with our baseline expectations that Powell delivered his final rate cut last December and that the Fed is likely to deliver only one reduction this year in September. Indeed, the SEP forecast for 2026 was very close to our own expectation. That said, as long as the labor market remains broadly stable, inflation concerns could push the Fed to remain on hold for longer. We expect Jefferson to largely reprise the themes from Chair Powell’s press conference. Recall that Powell focused heavily on inflation developments and the outlook, more than potential concerns around the labor market. This skew gave a hawkish tone to the discussion. On inflation, Powell continued to focus on tariff-driven inflation in the core goods category. He noted that tariffs have likely added one-half to three-quarters of a percentage point to inflation and maintained the view that these effects are likely to begin to wane around the middle of the year. Powell argued that seeing core goods disinflation is “really important” for determining the potential for rate cuts in the back half of the year. Notably, Powell removed a line on disinflation for core services in [Chair Powell's] prepared remarks, potentially reflecting the strengthening in super core inflation that has occurred in recent months. When pressed on how the Fed is thinking about [core services], Powell attributed the stickiness to a “bunch of idiosyncratic things”, and that [the Fed] continue to expect progress to eventually return to [core services]. For [the author's] top charts for understanding the inflation outlook, see [the author's] latest chartbook: “US Inflation Outlook: Signs of stickiness even before oil shock”. That being said, the December 2026 SOFR contract has moved by 27bps since last Wednesday, reflecting market expectations of no rate cuts this year as well as some probability of a hike. Indeed, expectations for the fed funds rate have swung by nearly 75bps relative to where SOFR Z6 closed on the eve of the US [or] Israel attack on Iran. In turn, [the author] would not be surprised to see [Vice Chair Jefferson] push back somewhat on market pricing – or at least downplay the potential for rate hikes as early as this year. Recall last week, Powell noted that a “vast majority” of officials do not anticipate rate increases, consistent with only one official anticipating a rate hike next year in the March dot plot. [Chair Powell] also did not suggest that more officials argued for a balanced description of the policy outlook relative to January, where the minutes noted “several officials” could support that description. That said, Powell pushed Matthew Luzzetti, Ph.D. back on the notion that downside risks to the labor market exceed upside risks to inflation. Together, [the author] interpreted [Chair Powell’s press conference] as suggesting the case for rate cuts has weakened but remains stronger than the case for rate hikes. In short, any deviation in Jefferson’s messaging from Powell, if [Vice Chair Jefferson] does deviate at all, would likely be aimed at tempering rising rate-hike expectations, at least in the very near term. The same can be said for San Francisco’s Daly (non-voter) and Philadelphia’s Paulson (non-voter), who will both be giving outlook speeches on Friday. This week’s data docket is very light, beginning with Monday’s construction spending (+0.1% vs. +0.3%) release for January, which should see some negative impact from harsh winter weather. On Tuesday, [the author expects] Q4 productivity growth (2.4% vs. 2.8% prelim) to be revised down alongside an upward revision to unit labor costs (+3.4% vs. +2.8% prelim) due to the downward revision to Q4 real GDP growth. Despite the revisions, the upward trend in productivity growth over the last two years and downward trend for ULCs would remain largely intact. Thursday’s jobless claims (210k vs 205k) will remain a focus for dovish Fed officials who continue to see downside risks to the labor market. However, barring any major surprises, the trend in claims would point to what last week’s FOMC statement characterized as an unemployment rate that “has been little changed in recent months.” Friday’s final reading on University of Michigan consumer sentiment (55.0 final vs. 55.5 prelim) is likely to undershoot [University of Michigan consumer sentiment's] preliminary reading as more surveys will have captured the Iran war. However, the inflation expectations series will be of greater focus for Fed officials as both 1-year and 5-10-year inflation expectations tend to be closely correlated with energy prices. As [the author] noted recently, (see “US Economic Perspectives: How big of an oil shock can the Big Beautiful Bill handle? “) mapping the impact of potential energy price shocks to consumers, we find that at a sustained $100/bbl oil price (near current levels), the projected tax benefits to consumers from the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA) would still exceed the drag from the implied “energy tax” increase. However, at $150/bbl, the increase in energy costs would present a more serious threat to the outlook for consumer spending. That said, as [the author] also noted (see “US Economic Perspectives: Into the danger zone? Nonlinearities between oil prices and growth “), important nonlinearities may be present in the relationship between growth and oil prices, at least over history. In particular, while an oil price shock that is within 20% of the peak oil price over the prior three years does not appear to have a material impact on growth, larger shocks are associated with significantly weaker growth on average over the following four quarters as well as a higher probability of negative growth in at least one quarter. In summary, If WTI holds at $100 per barrel or more over the next quarter, it would enter this “danger zone” of non-linear effects on growth. [The author's] analysis suggests that [the author] should be aware of the potential for nonlinearities, even if [nonlinearities] are not guaranteed.
