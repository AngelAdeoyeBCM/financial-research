## KEY MESSAGES

- A strong US nonfarm payrolls print this week could push the market to price in more risk of the Fed hiking rates.
- [The author does] not believe the Fed should take comfort from what’s priced into TIPS. Inflation expectations look fragile.
- [The author anticipates] market volatility headed into the first round of Colombia’s presidential election tonight. Since January, [the author has] looked for the Fed to keep rates on hold all year based on expectations for resilient US growth, a stable labor market and sticky inflation. Over the course of 2026, and especially since the Middle East conflict began, the challenges for the Fed in returning inflation to 2% have become more evident. The probability of rate hikes starting later this year has increased markedly. [The author's] longstanding view is that policymakers would hike rates if either of two conditions were met: unemployment seem ing likely to move below 4%, a level at which wage-price amplification becomes easier to envision, or long-term inflation expectations (LTIE) show ing further evidence of de-anchor ing. The likelihood of both conditions being met has increased over the past month. For this week’s US jobs report, [the author's] expectation is for an 85k gain in nonfarm payrolls, a steady unemployment rate and a mild bounce in earnings growth. Looking further out, [the author's] base case has the unemployment rate averag ing 4.3% this year and fall ing to 4.1% at the end of 2027, below both consensus and Fed estimates. However, the details of [the US jobs report] will be especially important. [The author] will be watching for ongoing signs of cyclical strength, which would tend to presage further momentum ahead. One key area to monitor is youth unemployment, which [the author sees] as a ‘canary in the coal mine’ for the job market. The rise in youth unemployment into 2025 was notable but has reversed swiftly this year (Figure 1). [The author sees] younger workers as a key gauge of the net effect of two cross currents in the US economy: the diffusion of artificial intelligence tools (which looks set to displace e mployees in some industries) and cyclical resilience in activity coupled with AI o ptimism (which [the author thinks] is likely to increase demand for labor). While last year’s US labor market narrative focused on downside risks for new entrants, [the author's] read of incoming data is that the chance of better-than - expected outcomes is underappreciated. To be sure, demand for new software engineers may prove to be lower than in the past (though [the author notes] job postings are rising), but [new software engineers] accounts for less than 1% of workers aged 16- 24. Rather, [younger workers] mostly work in leisure, retail, construction and in-person services jobs. Given strong recent labor data and the rising level of real monetary stimulus entering the US economy, another ‘boomy’ jobs print for May would likely lead [the author] to expect unemployment to follow a declining trajectory over the year, potentially taking [unemployment] to 4% by year end. The Fed would have difficulty overlooking [that unemployment to follow a declining trajectory over the year, potentially taking unemployment to 4% by year end].. 31/05/2026 2 Meanwhile, on the inflation side, oil prices saw downward pressure last week due to expectations of a US-Iran memorandum of understanding potentially being signed. [The author remains] skeptical concerning both the prospect of genuine de-escalation (but a halfway house solution to continue the ceasefire and keep talking with no real solution in sight does seem quite possible) and the likelihood of shipping flows through the Strait of Hormuz normalizing anytime soon. With [shipping flows through the Strait of Hormuz] curtailed, global energy balances remain very tight, as evidenced by strong draws on US crude stocks and slow gas storage injections in Europe. Absent material change, [the author expects] significant pressure points to develop in both oil and gas markets around the end of Q2/early Q3. Regardless of how [the Middle East conflict] plays out, with the ongoing energy price shock be ing the fourth successive jolt to inflation in the past five years, [the ongoing energy price shock] is already beginning to impact expectations. The latest University of Michigan long-term inflation expectations print shot upward to 3.9% from 3.4%, making a move above 4% – returning to last year’s levels – increasingly likely. While Fed officials have cited the stability of long-term breakevens (Figure 2) as a sign of anchored LTIE, last week [the author] wrote a note detailing why [the stability of long-term breakevens] is misleading and creates false optimism in [the author's] eyes. [The author thinks] long-term breakevens are less swayed by evolving inflation risks and more by risk appetite and liquidity-driven investor flows in the TIPS market. Moreover, b ecause [the TIPS market] only opened for business in 1997 and became reasonably liquid in 2003, there is no historical evidence to demonstrate how (or if) US breakevens would respond to a broad-based loss of confidence in the Fed. In contrast, the Michigan consumer survey was conducted through the 1970s and there is literature on how the de-anchoring in LTIE played out through [the Michigan consumer survey]. As such, should surveys of inflation expectations continue to rise, [the author thinks] [that surveys of inflation expectations continue to rise] should materially increase market pricing of rate hikes as well. In doing so, [the author] would expect the Fed to follow [the Fed's] playbook for the unwinding in 1999 of similar cuts that follow ed the failure of Long-Term Capital Management, which Chair Greenspan feared would create a recessionary shock to investor confidence. Like today’s environment, policymakers subsequently faced an unexpectedly resilient economy amid the internet boom, with a tightening labor market and upside inflation risks. If the Fed were to hike later this year, [the author thinks] the most likely outcome would be withdraw ing last year’s ‘insurance ’ cuts at sequential meetings. While [the author acknowledges] that new Chair Warsh will likely seek to avoid raising rates in [Chair Warsh's] early days of leading the Fed, [the author's] view is that regardless of who heads the central bank, monetary policy will be determined by the US economic outlook and by the Fed’s existing policy goals and strategy, in whatever direction the data directs. Outside of monetary policy, with Warsh sworn in just a week ago, market focus will also likely turn to [Chair Warsh's] other major objectives – communication and the balance sheet. Last week, [the author] published [the author's] first deep dive on how Fed balance sheet policy could look under the new leadership. [The author expects] a gradual, predictable and well-signposted effort to reduce the balance sheet. Changes to supervisory and regulatory requirements are a necessary prerequisite for [a gradual, predictable and well-signposted effort to reduce the balance sheet] to happen, in [the author's] view. In [the author's] base case, the balance sheet shrinks by ~$700bn, starting in 2028. [The author] will be hosting a call on [Fed balance sheet policy] on Wednesday at 8AM EST (register here).
