Therefore, international investors often use the NDF [or] NDS market to express [international investors'] views on INR movements. What flows drive the NDS market? The NDS market is highly flow-driven, especially in the 3–10y part of the curve, with the main flows as follows:

- Indian corporates and banks that raise money abroad typically pay Mumbai Interbank Forward Offer Rate (MIFOR) swaps to hedge [Indian corporates and banks that raise money abroad's] forward USDINR risk and meet [Indian corporates and banks that raise money abroad's] USD needs when the bonds or loans mature. The [Mumbai Interbank Forward Offer Rate] and NDS markets in India are highly correlated because [the Mumbai Interbank Forward Offer Rate and NDS markets in India] reflect the same underlying economic variables – the interest rate differential between India and US rates, and the perceived future value of the INR. Therefore, when the [Mumbai Interbank Forward Offer Rate] goes higher due to [the corporate hedging flows], so do NDS rates [goes higher].
- Supranational agencies issuing high-quality, INR-denominated bonds offshore (INR-Supras) also impact the NDS market. [The high-quality, INR-denominated bonds offshore (INR-Supras)] allow foreign investors to hold Indian local currency interest rate risk without navigating onshore restrictions, and banks handling [the INR-Supra issuance] receive NDS to hedge currency and rates risk, pushing NDS lower.
- Interest rate expectations also drive the NDS curve. As the NDS curve deviates further from the onshore Overnight Indexed Swaps (OIS) rates rates, arbitrageurs engage in relative-value trades to exploit the valuation differences. Views on rates, FX matter too: Flows aside, the NDS curve reflects a combination of interest rate expectations and investors’ views on USDINR, including [USDINR's] volatility. A bearish or bullish view on INR directly dictates NDS pricing by altering offshore demand, risk premiums and hedging flows. Because the [Reserve Bank of India] tightly regulates domestic derivatives, the offshore NDS market acts as a primary barometer and driver of currency sentiment.

[The Indian rupee non-deliverable forward (NDF) and swaps (NDS) market] exists because the Indian rupee (INR) remains only partially convertible; onshore forward access requires documented underlying exposure and approval from the Reserve Bank of India (RBI). Therefore, offshore corporates, global banks, foreign hedge funds, macro traders, and others use the NDF market to hedge or speculate on INR movements. What drives the NDS market? The NDS market is highly flow-driven, especially in the 3-year to 10-year part of the curve, with the main flows as follows:

1. Indian corporates and banks that raise money abroad typically pay Mumbai Interbank Forward Offer Rate (MIFOR) swaps to hedge [Indian corporates and banks that raise money abroad's] forward USDINR risk and meet [Indian corporates and banks that raise money abroad's] USD needs when the bonds or loans mature. The [Mumbai Interbank Forward Offer Rate] and NDS markets in India are highly correlated because [the Mumbai Interbank Forward Offer Rate and NDS markets in India] both reflect the same underlying economic variables – the interest rate differential between India and US rates, and the perceived future value of the INR. Therefore, when [Mumbai Interbank Forward Offer Rate] go higher due to [the corporate hedging flows], NDS also go higher.
2. When supranational agencies issue high-quality, INR-denominated bonds offshore (INR-Supras), [the INR-Supra issuance] allows foreign investors to hold Indian local currency interest rate risk without navigating direct onshore restrictions. The banks handling the supra issuance on behalf of the agencies receive NDS to hedge the currency and rates risk, which pushes NDS lower.
3. Interest rate expectations also drive the NDS curve. Investors monitor the gap between NDS rates and onshore Overnight Indexed Swap (OIS) rates. When rate hike or cut expectations heavily skew one market, arbitrageurs engage in relative value trades to exploit the valuation differences.
4. The view on USDINR also impacts the NDS curve. A bearish or bullish view on the USDINR directly dictates NDS pricing by altering offshore demand, risk premiums, and speculative flows for INR. Because [Reserve Bank of India] tightly regulates domestic derivatives, the offshore NDS market acts as a primary barometer and driver of currency sentiment.
