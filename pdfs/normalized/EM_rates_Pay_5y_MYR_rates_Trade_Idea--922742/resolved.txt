# EM rates: Pay 5y MYR rates

Chandresh Jain, EM Asia FX and Rates Strategist | MAR | Joey Li, FX and Rates Strategist, APAC | BNP Paribas Singapore Branch. [The author is] adding a paid position in MYR rates, as [the author views] that Malaysia's strong economic growth will continue and that markets underestimate the fiscal implications of energy prices staying high. Robust growth: [The author expects] economic growth to remain robust despite a slight disappointment in Q1 GDP (advanced estimate: 5.3% y/y, consensus: 5.5% y/y). Barring energy prices rising on a severe re-escalation in the Middle East conflict, investment and exports will support full-year growth, in [the author's] view. [The author anticipates] electronics exports remaining strong due to the resilience of the AI cycle, as highlighted by the region's strong export performance (Figure 2). [That Electronic exports will likely remain strong due to the resilience of the AI cycle] should keep overall growth supported, given supply-chain links across the economy. On the investment front, data center investment looks set to continue, while ongoing public infrastructure projects will also boost growth. Framework of rates during oil shock: In a higher oil price shock, one of the two things tend to happen. First, if governments allow higher prices to be passed on to the economy, markets price in more rate hikes in the front-end. [The author has] seen [that markets price in more rate hikes in the front-end] in most EM Asian markets, where front-end rates have sold off on rising oil prices and inflation risks (Figure 3).

[The author thinks] the moves in MYR rates thus far may be explained from two angles. First, markets have been more inclined to pay front-end rates in countries that are large net oil importers and highly exposed to the Middle East, and Malaysia ranks relatively low in this regard. Second, Malaysia has so far maintained fixed petrol prices at RM1.99/liter for Malaysian residents during [the Middle East conflict], whereas most other countries in the region have started to allow some pass-through to the consumer. However, [the author notes] a few caveats to both arguments. First, despite being an energy exporter, Malaysia remains a net oil importer and exposed to the ME (Figure 5), with a quarter of [Malaysia's] imports coming from [the Middle East] (see FX: Asia's sensitivity to supply-driven energy shocks, dated 11 March). Second, since Malaysia's BUDI95 fuel reform last December, petrol subsidies are only available for eligible Malaysians, not the entire population. Third, with energy prices expected to remain structurally elevated in the medium term, second-round effects will start to filter through to Malaysia via neighboring economies, putting upward pressure on inflation over time. In fact, [the author is] already seeing inflation momentum picking up before the oil shock (Figure 4). Fiscal costs appears underappreciated: Although RON95 petrol subsidies may be kept in place, [that RON95 petrol subsidies may be kept in place] comes at significant fiscal cost – fuel subsidies are estimated to cost the government about RM7bn in April, a tenfold increase from the pre-conflict monthly average of RM700mn. Most recently, Malaysia has also announced RM5bn of measures to aid MSMEs affected by [the Middle East conflict]. If oil prices remain close to $100 per barrel for another three months, [the author] could see subsidy bill increasing by another RM20bn. Although a potentially larger dividend payment from Petronas due to higher energy prices may provide some offset later this year, [a potentially larger dividend payment from Petronas] may not compensate fully for the increased subsidy costs.
