Bigger than consensus The size of the Iran operation so far is likely to catch the consensus by surprise. Indeed, risk premiums ahead of the weekend remained very low outside of crude oil. In [the author's] conversations during recent weeks most investors were asking about scenarios and hedges but did not want to depart from the bullish core views in EM. Any hedges in EM seem to have been concentrated on the GCC, particularly via CDS, while investors did not want to sell EMFX. The strong consensus was that an operation, if any, would be relatively quick and thus didn’t warrant a change in the baseline. Max long EM Indeed, positioning ahead of [the Iran operation] was exceptionally bullish on EM FX and equities, according to [the author's] two monthly surveys and [the author's] aggregate EMFX sentiment indicator which had triggered a contrarian sell signal several weeks ago, for the first time since 2022. Nevertheless, inflows in EM in recent weeks continued to be very strong. Cross-over investors into EM assets also became very bullish early this year. A big speed bump, at the least Given [the exceptionally bullish positioning on EM FX and equities] before [the Iran operation], the initial market reaction is likely to be pronounced. A combination of risk-off with USD and energy price spikes is the worst case for EM and indeed the likely near-term outcome. In the short term [the author expects] most pressure on EM markets which are crowded and vulnerable to higher oil prices. In local markets [the EM markets which are crowded and vulnerable to higher oil prices] include CNH, KRW, BRL (rates vulnerable but FX should hold up better given positive oil beta), CLP, MXN, HUF, ZAR, TRY and EGP. EXD fallout less sharp The GCC credits will be in focus, but [the GCC credits] also benefit most from [the GCC credits'] strong balance sheets - except for Bahrain. Egypt is fundamentally exposed and crowded. Ukraine positioning is heavy and at risk from global geopolitical tensions. Argentina is also crowded though further removed from the action. Angola, Colombia or Nigeria may seem to benefit as oil exporters, but risk-off will adversely affect [Angola, Colombia or Nigeria] as well. Can Iran become a roadblock for EM? Whether [the Iran operation] become a danger to the structural long EM trade hinges on how inflationary the energy implications will be, which depends on the length of [the Iran operation] and the ability of Iran to disrupt regional supplies. The Iran events of 2025 trained markets not to sell EM on headlines, as energy prices came back quickly. But then there was also the 2022 case of protracted high inflation, which was very bad for EM through tighter financial conditions, lower growth and balance sheet pressures. Specifically, THB, KRW, SGD, and INR should be sensitive to any sustained rise in oil prices as crude oil imports are estimated at roughly 6.8%, 4.6%, 4.5% and 3.8% of [THB, KRW, SGD, and INR's] respective GDPs. More specifically, sustained higher energy prices pushed Korea’s trade balance to deficit in 2022. Meanwhile, even if China’s crude oil dependency ranks lower (1.8% of GDP), about a third of China’s crude supply comes from ships transiting the Strait of Hormuz. This said, China dislikes excessive volatility and will focus on ensuring CNY depreciation will be orderly. [Orderly CNY depreciation] could be defined as a move in USD [or] CNY spot (currently around 6.8621) back towards the USD [or] CNY fixing of around 6.922 over the course of a week. Regional geopolitical implications are also key Beyond the transmission of shocks through economic and financial channels, the implications for Asia’s own regional geopolitics will also be key. The New York Times reported late Friday that the US is delaying arms sale to Taiwan as not to upset China, ahead of the Trump-Xi meeting scheduled at the end of this month. [That the US is delaying arms sale to Taiwan as not to upset China] could reduce the selling pressure on NE Asia FX. Additionally, South Korea President Lee is urging North Korea to resume dialogue. [The development that South Korea President Lee is urging North Korea to resume dialogue] could be seen as an effort from the US and [the US's] allies to calm geopolitical tensions elsewhere in world. What if energy prices remain high? One analogue of comparison is the Russian invasion of Ukraine of February 2024. Asian currencies were broadly weaker in the following 4-months: INR 3.5%, THB 5.8%, PHP 4.5%, and SGD 2%. However, there are important differences. It should be noted that India (INR) benefitted from cheaper Russia oil, which is not applicable here. A second issue will be impact on shipping and insurance costs, which maybe different due to differences in geographies. A sustained rise in oil prices would represent a negative supply shock for Asia, while at the same time, the economies of Taiwan, Korea, Singapore, and Malaysia are seeing sustained demand above [the economies of Taiwan, Korea, Singapore, and Malaysia's] trend growth due to AI capex demand. Markets are already anticipating a tightening cycle in [the economies of Taiwan, Korea, Singapore, and Malaysia] to varying degrees, and sustained oil prices may see more paying pressure in [the economies of Taiwan, Korea, Singapore, and Malaysia]. The back end may underperform relative to the US, due to the safe-haven appeal of US Treasuries. The impact on Philippine and Indonesian fixed income rates could be more negative relative to Asian peers, as the negative supply shock could have a bigger negative impact on [the Philippines and Indonesia's] respective growth and fiscal outlooks. Growth in the Philippines and Indonesia has been underwhelming. Indonesia is a net crude oil exporter but is still reliant on refined oil imports, while foreign investors are increasingly concerned over [Indonesia's] fiscal credibility. Equity outflows [and] energy dependence [is] KRW [and] TWD to weaken An outbreak of war in the Middle East would likely weaken the KRW in the term near as global investors take profit and pull back from Korean equities. The KRW would face additional pressure because Korea is heavily exposed to energy price shocks: mineral fuels including oil accounted for 22.2% of Korea’s total imports in 2025. A conflict driven spike in crude prices would reverse Korea’s recent improvement in [Korea's] terms-of-trade which has recently surged because of the rising price of memory chips. Combined with portfolio outflows, [the conflict driven spike in crude prices] would likely push the KRW weaker and raise short term volatility. The TWD would similarly come under depreciation pressure in a Middle East conflict scenario as global investors retreat from risk and reduce exposure to Taiwan’s tech heavy equity market. At the same time, Taiwan’s dependence on imported energy magnifies the macro impact of higher oil prices: crude oil imports make up 10% of Taiwan’s total imports in 2025. A sharp rise in oil costs would squeeze margins for Taiwan’s export-oriented manufacturing sector and narrow Taiwan’s growing the trade balance. The combination of foreign equity outflows and higher energy import costs would likely weigh on the TWD, increasing [the TWD's] sensitivity to geopolitical risk episodes. EEMEA: Energy-sensitive and crowded markets at risk LDM: ZAR and HUF assets likely to be hit hardest All EEMEA assets are vulnerable to the escalation in Iran, but [the author expects] South African and Hungarian markets to underperform within the liquid universe. Positioning in both FX and rates is crowded, increasing the risk of disorderly unwinds. Both countries are also net oil importers, and higher crude prices represent a negative terms-of-trade shock. Rising oil should push inflation expectations higher and amplify fiscal concerns. In addition, South Africa and Hungary have a high beta to EM risk, making [South Africa and Hungary] particularly sensitive to risk-off sentiment. [The author remains] long USD [or] ZAR (current: 15.92; entry: 16.13). For the original trade rationale, see report Long USD [or] ZAR: EM sentiment is too bullish. Risks are a dovish Fed and a continued gold rally. Israel: Not so supportive for the shekel this time [The author expects] Israeli assets to weaken in [the Iran operation], unlike during the previous escalation when the shekel sold off briefly before appreciating. The shekel currently carries no meaningful risk premium, whereas in the earlier conflict [the shekel] appeared undervalued. Moreover, the economic costs of the current situation are likely to be higher than those associated with the previous conflict. That said, the Bank of Israel may intervene in the FX market if [the Bank of Israel] sees excessive ILS volatility. Receivers likely to suffer amid higher oil and weaker FX Higher oil prices and weaker FX across the region are likely to push inflation expectations higher. Fiscal risks may also increase in some countries as growth slows in response to the geopolitical shock. As a result, [the author expects] EEMEA rates to reprice higher, including in more DM-like markets such as Czechia and Israel. [The author] closed [the author's] CZK and PLN 5y receivers, as well as the Poland ASW tightener on February 26 (see report Geopolitical risks: close ASW tightener in Poland [and] 5y CZK and PLN receivers, 26 Feb). Frontier: NGN to weaken least, EGP likely to sell off most Among frontier EEMEA currencies, the EGP is likely to come under the most pressure, reflecting crowded positioning, the negative terms of trade shock from higher oil, and [the EGP's] elevated sensitivity to EM risk sentiment. Positioning in the NGN is also crowded, but higher oil prices and potential central bank intervention should provide some relative support, making the NGN one of the more resilient frontier currencies in [the Iran operation]. Despite being an oil exporter, Kazakhstan is likely to come under more pressure than the NGN, as oil is less supportive for the tenge; [that oil is less supportive for the tenge] may reflect the high degree of dollarization among exporters. EXD: A tale or risk premium, positioning and risk appetite MENA: Common regional risk premia to rise – but divergences are key Risk premia in MENA credits are, as expected, likely to remain elevated while the closure of the Straits of Hormuz and attacks on energy infrastructure remain a distinct 4 possibility. In addition to disruptions to oil supply, attacks on urban infrastructure in markets like the UAE are likely to weigh on investor sentiment. However, positioning in MENA has lightened considerably over 2026, which is likely to cap any selloffs. Among specific credits, BHRAIN is especially fragile to any substantial escalation, given [BHRAIN's] pre-existing fiscal issues, [BHRAIN's] dependence on the Straits of Hormuz, and a prolonged regional conflict potentially reducing or delaying an already-uncertain GCC bailout necessary to stabilize debt dynamics. QATAR and KUWAIT are similarly exposed to closures in the Straits of Hormuz, given [QATAR and KUWAIT's] lack of access to the Red Sea. Large oil importers – Egypt, Türkiye – are likely to face inflationary pressures from higher oil prices and cross-asset pressures from [Egypt, Türkiye's] especially well-positioned local currency assets. On the other hand, KSA’s East-West pipeline may enable [KSA] to partially bypass any closure of the Straits of Hormuz, while elevated oil prices in the medium-term will bolster the Saudi fiscal position. Indeed, prior Iran-related conflicts have seen KSA outperform other GCC credits in the month after the beginning of the escalation. Positioning in KSA is also especially light relative to the rest of GCC. However, Saudi remains vulnerable to any direct targeting of [KSA's] energy infrastructure, or disruptions in the Red Sea near Yemen. CEE: Positioning leaves [CEE credits] especially fragile CEE credits are especially exposed to sustained higher oil prices, especially given [CEE credits'] outperformance over the last month over the broader EM IG complex. ROMANI will be especially fragile given the heavy long positioning in [ROMANI], as well as [ROMANI's] negative supply technical. REPHUN is also likely to come under pressure, especially heading into elections, as higher energy prices are likely to introduce further volatility into the election campaign (even though higher energy prices are likely to increase anti-incumbency sentiment). SSA: At the mercy of global risk appetite [The author's] prior analysis has suggested that, during risk-off episodes, SSA credits trade more in line with global vol indices rather than the expected fundamental pass-through from commodity prices. Thus, while SSA countries like ANGOL and NGERIA will benefit from a sustained increase in oil prices, [SSA countries like ANGOL and NGERIA] tend to see [SSA countries like ANGOL and NGERIA's] beta to commodity prices reverse during global shocks like Liberation Day or the 12-Day War. Especially vulnerable will be SSA oil importers like KENINT and ZAMBIN, which will face both fundamental and risk-premia-related pressures. While SOAF faces similar risks as other Southern African countries, [SOAF] has historically demonstrated a lower beta to global vol shocks than other SSA countries, and in the medium-term, may benefit from continued higher gold prices in the event of a sustained conflict. South Asia: Noisy neighborhood PKSTAN will likely be one of the most negatively impacted markets from the latest Iran tensions. The country’s total energy imports bill is close to $15-16bn or 4% of GDP. Therefore, any major price jump may inflate fiscal costs and weaken the current account, further delaying the lagging recovery. [The author also notes] that most of the energy is imported from the Gulf nations, which may add security concerns to the energy supplies. The country is also in the immediate proximity to [the Iran operation], while also carrying [PKSTAN's] own geopolitical risk in the form of continued tensions with Afghanistan, which may add to sentiment pressures. SRILAN exposure is similar to Pakistan on energy, but geopolitical concerns are much less in [SRILAN]. Both SRILAN and PKSTAN were also one of the key beneficiaries of broader EM rally earlier, so [the broader EM rally's] reversal could potentially put additional pressure on EXD valuations. LatAm: Beware positioning and exposure LDM: A classical risk-off shock in FX and rates The recent US attack on Iran has introduced a meaningful geopolitical risk premium into global markets, with oil prices likely to move higher in the near term. In LatAm, the initial impact should be a classic risk-off shock to currencies and bond markets. Most currencies tend to weaken in broad risk-off episodes as capital flows back into safer assets. That said, commodity exporters may outperform other emerging markets on a relative basis, as higher oil and metals prices support terms of trade. Brazil’s real has the strongest positive correlation with oil prices in the region and is therefore likely to outperform on a relative basis. By contrast, Chile’s peso could underperform given [Chile’s peso's] dependence on energy imports. Peru’s sol may show relative resilience, benefiting from higher gold prices and ample international reserves. Positioning also matters. Countries that have attracted large recent inflows, such as Colombia and Chile, may be more vulnerable to capital outflows than Mexico, which has seen more modest inflows and therefore appears less exposed to abrupt reversals. Higher oil prices will also feed into inflation expectations and tighten financial conditions, complicating central bank outlooks. Brazil’s central bank is still likely to begin easing given high real rates and ongoing disinflation, but [Brazil’s central bank] may proceed more cautiously, favoring the belly and long end over the very front end. Meanwhile, central banks in Mexico and Chile may tone down guidance on further rate cuts as inflation breakevens move higher. 6 EXD: Risk-off weighs on HY, but oil shock reshapes LatAm Risk-off negative for Argentina and crowded high-yielders Risk-off would negatively affect crowded positions, particularly in select high yielders such as Argentina (though [the author believes] positioning in Argentina is not as crowded as in late 2024, and that local investors that reduced positioning during last year’s electoral volatility could use a correction to increase holdings). Prolonged oil shock: CACs, Venezuela, Colombia in focus Over time, [the author thinks] the relative outperformers and underperformers will be driven by exposure to oil. A prolonged period of high oil prices would be particularly negative for oil importers in Central America and Caribbean (CACs). For DomRep, which was already facing a period of low growth, higher oil prices could affect tourism flows due to higher airfare, and tourism so far has remained a bright spot. For Colombia, high oil prices would bring some fiscal relief and give the next administration more room to manoeuvre to consolidate very wide deficits. In Venezuela, [the author thinks] the initial market reaction will be negative due to risk-off, but a prolonged period of high oil prices would increase the recovery value of [Venezuela's] defaulted bonds and could encourage more investment to increase oil production.
