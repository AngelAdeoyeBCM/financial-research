Headline inflation is down by 40bp, meeting consensus and [the author's] expectations, aided by a weak food inflation print. Core inflation is at [core inflation's] lowest since September 2021. That said, [the UK Inflation Data] was a messy print – live music, hotels, hospital services and car insurance came in stronger than [the author] expected, but the first two categories are very volatile, and the second two look like a one-off level shift. All in all, the direction of travel is down, and [the author sticks] with [the author's] call for a March cut. But for more easing thereafter, (underlying) services inflation ought to start subsiding from the next inflation print. Services inflation | You didn't think this would be easy, right? Services inflation surprised to the upside on a few different factors: I) base effects in hotel prices; II) an odd-looking pop in live music prices, where the category does depend a lot on the monthly composition of acts performing, and is thus prone to volatility; and III) what looks to [the author] to be a level-shift adjustment higher in car and health insurance, and hospital services prices. [The level-shift adjustment higher in car and health insurance] is in line with the direction of travel for insurance fees that [the author] had assumed for this year – [car and health insurance] are unlikely to contribute to disinflation moving forward, after [car and health insurance's] weakness through 2025. However, [the level-shift adjustment higher in car and health insurance] looks a bit too steep for [the author] to take [the level-shift adjustment higher in car and health insurance] on board as a signal for the scale of likely price pressures moving forward. On the hospital services side, [the author] had wondered whether the category looked a bit too low following July and October data, but again, did not model such a severe readjustment. At close to 3%Y, hospital services inflation now looks more plausible and, again, [the author] struggle to see such a sharp price hike in April again. Finally, [the author does] note that the ONS captured, it seems, a touch lower discounts in the catering category than last year. [That the ONS captured, it seems, a touch lower discounts in the catering category than last year], [the author thinks], does not correspond to the actual picture on the ground (see, for example, here), but does perhaps imply a smidge lower boost to the category from any reversals in the measured sales come February. On the whole, aided by live music, hospital services and health insurance, underlying services inflation inflected higher. Given the nature of the uptick, [the author] would not (yet) take too much of a signal from the inflection. That said, due to the catering category in particular, underlying services inflation really ought to start declining from February onwards in order for more than one cut to be delivered. Elsewhere, at the time of writing, the ONS is in the process of correcting the private rents data (per [the author's] correspondence with [the ONS]), which seems to have been miscalculated – albeit with no impact on headline figures. [The author] will report back with the analysis of rents data in [the author's] regular Inflation Watch later today, as the ONS adjusts the underlying numbers. Goods [and] food inflation | In a better place: At -1.1%M, core goods prices were close to [the author's] and BoE forecasts. Between October and January, core goods prices corrected by 1.2%, which is not as steep as was the case at the turn of 2024, but is a touch more aggressive than what [the author] saw in the immediate pre-Covid period and at the turn of 2025. [The author] struggle to get overly excited about core goods inflation from here, and see [core goods inflation] bobbing around at just below 1%Y. [The author] accept that should retail M Update 2 volumes disappoint, there is ample scope for discounting, with risks to [the author's] forecasts thus skewed to the downside. Food inflation, meanwhile, collapsed. BRC and WorldPanel, as well as higher-frequency data, suggested scope for a touch higher numbers, but that did not materialise. [The author] will reassess what the risk of a snap-back is moving forward, although from here on out the ONS will start using [the ONS's] new scanner data, which offers a bit more chance for discounts-related volatility. On the whole, food inflation should end the year lower, but [the author is] cautious about potential volatility on the introduction of the new methodology. BoE implications: [The UK Inflation Data] was a messy print to parse through, and [the UK Inflation Data] has something for everyone to take on board. Ultimately, [the author does] think the key message is a drop in core inflation and a 40bp correction in headline – base effects in education and a decline in fuel prices helped with the sharp move lower. The BoE expected a 2.9%Y headline reading, and so [the UK Inflation Data] surprised [the BoE] to the upside, largely on services. That said, Governor Bailey did point out several months ago that the MPC deals with "10bp or 20bp misses all the time", and [the author finds] the uptick in underlying services inflation not convincing enough to take a real steer from. [The drop in core inflation and a 40bp correction in headline], combined with the higher jobless rate than the BoE had expected, means the path to a March cut looks clear. [The author expects] two more thereafter, albeit at a slower pace, in July and November. [The author sees] risks as skewed towards delivery a touch earlier – perhaps June – largely on the labour market weakness.
