# Economics [European Central Bank]: Changing Times

## CITI’S TAKE

An apparent change of emphasis in [European Central Bank] communication from the first pillar of [the European Central Bank's] decision framework (inflation outlook) to the other two (underlying inflation dynamics and policy transmission) justifies market expectations of a more moderate path for policy rates. [The apparent change of emphasis in European Central Bank communication] also highlights what [the author sees] as a relative instability of the [European Central Bank] reaction function recently. [The relative instability of the European Central Bank reaction function] may change soon. [The author believes] the President of the [European Central Bank] may announce [the President of the European Central Bank's] early departure soon (Bloomberg, 30-Sep), given that [the early departure of the President of the European Central Bank] would facilitate government decisions on the quasi-simultaneous replacement of half of the Executive Board in early 2027. It does matter then to understand how candidates to [the President of the European Central Bank's] succession believe price formation to take place, as [price formation] is the key to the reaction function. [The author takes] this opportunity to explain what “hawk” and “dove” really mean in the context of monetary policy. n | [European Central Bank]: Changing Times? It is customary for sell-side institutions to publish a preview note ahead of each central bank policy meeting. [The author does] not deviate from tradition, but [the author does] [a preview note] this month in an unusual way. Rather than focusing on immediate decisions, [the author discusses] below whether the reaction function of the European Central Bank (ECB) may change - and why - when [the President of the European Central Bank] leaves [the President of the European Central Bank's] position. The reason [the author does] this now is threefold: first, [the author believes] that the past few months illustrate that the [European Central Bank] reaction function has not been stable. Second, it seems to [the author] rational to expect that the President of the [European Central Bank] might announce very soon [the President of the European Central Bank's] early departure from the [European Central Bank], which will immediately raise questions as to what [the early departure of the President of the European Central Bank] implies for the direction of policy. Third, it gives [the author] again an opportunity to explain what a “hawk” or a “dove” really means in the context of monetary policy. 1. Three pillars, but not equally weighed [The author] start this short discussion with the “framework” that the leadership of the [European Central Bank] regularly quotes as the guide to [the European Central Bank's] decisions. [The European Central Bank “framework”] is based on three pillars: nThe outlook for inflation; nThe underlying dynamics of inflation; nThe transmission of monetary policy. In practice, the outlook for inflation refers to a relatively short horizon. [The outlook for inflation] includes near-term inflation developments as captured by the [European Central Bank] staff’s projections, and risk thereto, described in the staff scenarios. In [the author's] reading of past policy decisions, [the outlook for inflation] also includes a backward-looking element, because [the author believes] that realised inflation does affect the interpretation of [the outlook for inflation], and [the author] explain below how [the author] rationalise [the observation that realised inflation does affect the interpretation of the outlook for inflation]. The second dimension of the framework is the underlying dynamics of inflation. [The author sees] [the underlying dynamics of inflation] as encompassing all the information that allows to distinguish between endogenous and exogenous price shocks. In the current circumstances, for instance, [the underlying dynamics of inflation] relates to the emergence, or lack thereof, of second round effects. [The emergence of second round effects] means in particular the question of whether higher energy prices will feed into higher wages. [The question of whether higher energy prices will feed into higher wages] should be the critical point of interest, as second round effects must include wages. [The author does] not believe there can take place a price-price loop (where higher prices feed into higher prices) without there being a wage-price loop (where higher prices feed into higher wages). The third pillar – the transmission of monetary policy – has to be an assessment of the degree of accommodation or restriction generated by policy, in relation to what is required at a given moment in time. [The transmission of monetary policy] therefore has to include a comparison of policy rates and the central bank estimate of the neutral range, but also go further. [The transmission of monetary policy] has to include a judgment on how overall financing conditions affect demand and thereby eventually prices. At the moment, [the assessment of the degree of accommodation or restriction generated by policy] has to take account of the global uptrend in real rates, and the consequences of the idiosyncratic increase in sovereign spreads caused by a loss of confidence in French fiscal dynamics. The emphasis on each of [the three pillars] has not been stable over time. At the time of the September meeting of the Governing Council, for instance, [the author has] been surprised that in [the President of the European Central Bank's] press conference, [the President of the European Central Bank] focused almost exclusively on the first pillar, and seemed place less weight on the other two, for instance by saying that the Governing Council is not “attaching great importance in the current circumstances to the neutral rate”. If one does not know whether policy rates are neutral or restrictive, it is not clear to [the author] how policy transmission is assessed. More recently, the balance of weight put on the three pillars seems to have changed. In [the President of the European Central Bank's] hearing at the European Parliament on 28 September, [the President of the European Central Bank] emphasised the second and third pillar, underlying the lack of evidence that higher energy prices feed into wages, and the rise in long-term yields, which slow growth and reduce pass-through from an exogenous price shock. [That the President of the European Central Bank emphasised the second and third pillar] seems to place the [European Central Bank] now broadly in the position [the author] mistakenly thought [the European Central Bank] would be in September: a situation where, with policy rates at the upper end of the neutral range, the bar for further tightening becomes higher. [The author's] expectations then were that a minority of “dovish” Council members would be able to reduce the appetite for additional rate hikes, even in the context of a deterioration of near term inflation projections, because [the minority of “dovish” Council members] would demand evidence of the emergence of second round effects to take policy into properly restrictive territory. The policy statement and press conference that followed [the September meeting of the Governing Council] contradicted [the author's] expectation. Communication since seems to bring [the author's expectations then] back into the field of probable. [The author has] accordingly not changed [the author's] baseline expectation that the Governing Council will leave policy rates unchanged this week, probably hike [policy rates] by 25 bps in December, and possibly hike [policy rates] again in March of next year, conditional on there being no further deterioration in the French sovereign bond market, and no global financial tensions, related for instance to confidence in AI investment. [The author] recognise of course that, given the possible exogenous influences that [the author] just mentioned and the two-way risk to energy prices rooted in a still unstable geopolitical situation globally, [the author's] expectations for policy rates are better described in the form of a distribution rather than a modal forecast. 2. Hawks and doves: the definitive definition The point, which [the author] wanted to make in this note, however, is not that the outlook for rates is contingent upon the “data”. [The point] is that [the outlook for rates] is also dependent on which data one looks at, and why. [The author] highlighted what seems to [the author] to be a shift in emphasis between the three pillars that guide [European Central Bank] decisions because in [the author's] mind [the shift in emphasis between the three pillars that guide European Central Bank decisions] illustrates that the [European Central Bank] reaction function has not been stable. [The relative instability of the European Central Bank reaction function], however, may change. The [European Central Bank] President has several times conveyed publicly that [the President of the European Central Bank] would not leave [the President of the European Central Bank's] position before 2027, but may do so several months before the end of [the President of the European Central Bank's] term. It seems to [the author] plausible to expect that the announcement of [the early departure of the President of the European Central Bank] might be imminent. There are several reasons why such an announcement could be in [the President of the European Central Bank's] own and governments’ interest. An early announcement may allow [the President of the European Central Bank's] to join the World Economic Forum in time for the Davos meetings in January 2027 (if it is indeed [the President of the European Central Bank's] intention to do so, as reported in the press (see Reuters 15 October). [An early announcement] could also allow governments to approach the replacement of the three key positions on the Executive Board of the [European Central Bank] (President, chief economist, board member in charge of markets) in one single discussion. Isabel Schnabel’s decision to leave the [European Central Bank] in early January adds urgency to [the replacement of the three key positions on the Executive Board of the European Central Bank]. Other considerations may point in the same direction. If [the President of the European Central Bank] announces [the President of the European Central Bank's] resignation, the attention of the market will evidently turn very quickly to who will replace [the President of the European Central Bank's]. [The author has] long thought [who will replace the President] was an important question, for two reasons: first, because the next President likely will exert considerable influence over the course of policy; And second, because [the author expects] that the next President may have more clearly articulated views on the reaction function of the central bank. [The author's] first assertion is substantiated by the already mentioned fact that in the next few months, the three most important positions on the six-member Executive Board of the [European Central Bank] will change hands (and even possibly four positions). [The author's] current expectation is that the new President will be one of the two candidates most often cited in the media, i.e. Pablo Hernandez de Cos and Klaas Knot (Bloomberg 23October). Both have considerable expertise, experience and reputation. Both have been closely involved in monetary policy and other [European Central Bank] policies for decades. Both know the [European Central Bank] itself and the Eurosystem closely. By contrast the German and French Executive Board members, who will replace Isabel Schnabel (in January) and Philip Lane (in June) are likely to be relative newcomers. The same observation applies to some extent to the Governing Council. Few Governors (with the possible exception of Fabio Panetta) appear to combine the depth and breadth of experience that both Pablo Hernandez de Cos and Klaas Knot have accumulated over the years. [The author's] expectation therefore is that if either candidate is appointed President, [either candidate's] leadership will be perhaps even more pronounced from the outset than that of any previous President of the [European Central Bank]. How [Pablo Hernandez de Cos and Klaas Knot] understand the reaction function of the central bank then becomes relevant for markets. [That How Pablo Hernandez de Cos and Klaas Knot understand the reaction function of the central bank then becomes relevant for markets] takes [the author] again to the discussion of what constitutes a “dove” or a “hawk”. [A “dove” or a “hawk”] are often used but rarely explained. In a recent interview (La Croix, 30 September), Christine Lagarde described hawks as individuals “who favour tightening monetary policy to fight inflation”, while doves would be those “who are keen to support economic activity with accommodative monetary policy”. [Christine Lagarde's recent interview] seem to imply that the doves are more willing than the hawks to compromise on the central bank’s primary objective. [The author's] own definition is entirely different. [The author's own definition] recognises that both hawks and doves are equally committed to the central bank mandate, but that [hawks and doves] have different understanding of how prices are formed, and that [the different understanding of how prices are formed] translates into noticeably different reaction functions. “Doves”, in [the author's] interpretation, are central bankers who believe that inflation is primarily a matter of the confrontation of supply and demand. Economists of [the obedience that inflation is primarily a matter of the confrontation of supply and demand] will be very sensitive to the origin of a shock to prices. [The doves] will be very keen to respond if the shock is endogenous (a shock to demand, or a persistent shock to supply), but will show substantial patience if the shock is exogenous (such as is the case now). In [the doves'] mind, because monetary policy works by steering demand, and because transmission lags can be long, [monetary policy] can only affect inflation at a relatively long horizon. In the three-pillar framework used by the [European Central Bank] currently, [the doves] would place relatively more emphasis on the last two. “Hawks”, by contrast, are central bankers who put much more emphasis on inflation expectations in the formation of prices. If one believes that inflation expectations impact on future prices, and if one recognises (reasonably) that current inflation has a bearing on inflation expectations, then one will be much less patient in the face of a shock. Hawks are more backward-looking, more willing to consider that any deviation of realised inflation requires a policy response, because of the risk of de-anchoring of expectations that [the hawks] assume. In the three-pillar framework of the [European Central Bank], [the hawks] would pay more attention to the first. At this stage, [the author] underline that neither constituency is “hawkish” or “dovish” in the traditional sense. Both are equally committed to price stability, but [hawks and doves'] horizon is different because of how much [hawks and doves] believe the expectations channel matters in relation to other channels. Where [the different understanding of how prices are formed] translates into the commonly accepted understanding of “hawks” and “doves” is via the aversion for error that each constituency will rationally exhibit. [The aversion for error that each constituency will rationally exhibit] is important, because as [the author has] argued from time to time, human behaviour is not motivated solely by what [the author wants] to achieve, but also by what [the author] most want to avoid. [That human behaviour is not motivated solely by what the author wants to achieve, but also by what the author most want to avoid] is true of all of [the author], irrespective of [the author's] walk of life. [That human behaviour is not motivated solely by what the author wants to achieve, but also by what the author most want to avoid] is certainly true of central bankers. [Central bankers] cannot avoid policy mistakes, and so will naturally have a bias – [the bias] is to be put to [central bankers'] credit – to prefer a policy mistake whose consequences [central bankers] can control to one whose consequences [central bankers] cannot [control]. Since doves generally believe inflation to be a matter of supply and demand, [the doves] know [the doves] can always bring inflation down from levels above target. [That the doves can always bring inflation down from levels above target] can be achieved by destruction of demand, which monetary policy can always deliver. If inflation were to fall towards zero, however, the experience of the 2010s in Europe and the US, that of the US in the 1930s and that of Japan over much of the past three decades is less encouraging. [The experience of the 2010s in Europe and the US, that of the US in the 1930s and that of Japan over much of the past three decades] shows that the zero lower bound is binding, and that reflationary policies then become difficult, and possibly unavailable to the central bank without assistance from governments. So central bankers of [the doves] may be marginally less adverse to inflation being a bit too high than a bit too low. The weight put by hawks on expectations leads to a diametrically opposed conclusion. If expectations matter, then the consequences of inflation undershooting are less worrying than those of inflation overshooting. Downward nominal rigidity means that deviation of inflation from target on the downside can be persistent but are unlikely to be very large. Indeed, during the 2010s, inflation hovered fairly close to zero in Europe but outright deflation never materialised. There is no similar stickiness of prices on the upside and so, if as in 2022 there occurs a very large exogenous shock in that direction, inflation can quickly rise far above target. If the size of deviation of inflation from target is relevant to expectations, and if expectations drive prices, then the conclusion has to be that it is less dangerous for the central bank to undershoot the target than to overshoot [the target]. In [the author's] reading, whether any individual central banker is closer to the definition of a hawk or a dove cannot be identified solely from [any individual central banker's] inclination to hike or cut rates at a given moment in time. [Whether any individual central banker is closer to the definition of a hawk or a dove] is better assessed by looking at the frequency of references to the output gap or to inflation expectations in [any individual central banker's] communication and in [any individual central banker's] description of the economy. The reason why [the author] gave this (stylised) explanation of what it really means to be a dove or a hawk is that when a new President takes up the position at the [European Central Bank], [the author expects] the central bank reaction function to be relatively more stable than [the reaction function of the European Central Bank] has been recently, if the new President has a more clearly articulated view on what matters most to price formation.
