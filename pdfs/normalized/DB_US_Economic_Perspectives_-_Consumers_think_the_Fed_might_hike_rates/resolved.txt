# US Economic Perspectives - Consumers think the Fed might hike rates

Economics US Economic Perspectives Date 29 April 2026

Consumers are increasingly hawkish about monetary policy As [the author's] preview note outlined, [the author expects] the Fed to keep rates unchanged today and delay adopting explicit language in the statement about two-sided risks to the policy outlook (see “FOMC preview: Powell’s last dance?”). Beyond today, [the author] recently updated [the author's] Fed forecast and see the Fed on hold at neutral indefinitely (see “Outlook update: The (oil) drumbeat of war”). That said, while rate hikes are no longer a trivial probability, [the author sees] a lower policy rate as more likely than higher this year (see “Could the Fed hike rates in 2026?”). Yesterday’s Conference Board (CB) data suggest that households are more hawkish about the interest rate outlook over the coming months. 62.8% of respondents expect interest rates to be higher over the next year, the highest share since August 2023. Conversely, only 13.6% of respondents expect interest rates to be lower over the next year, the lowest since October 2023. [The recent reading on net interest rate expectations] is a roughly 50pp spread between higher and lower interest rate expectations. Figure 1: Conference Board shows increasing household conviction around higher rates Source: Conference Board, Haver Analytics, Deutsche Bank Research Historically, this measure of net interest rate expectations has performed remarkably well in capturing interest rate decisions from the Federal Reserve. As Matthew Luzzetti, Ph.D. the following chart shows, even though the monthly version of [the measure of net interest rate expectations] is particularly volatile, [the measure of net interest rate expectations] still has a 77% correlation with the year-over-year change in the effective fed funds rate since 1987, with a five-month lead. Equally as compelling, the correlation and lag is remarkably stable over time – the correlation was 75% in the 1990s, 89% in the 2000s, and 69% in the 2010s, a period where you would have expected a breakdown given the Fed was stuck at the zero lower bound for a large portion of that period. Since 2017, the correlation is 67%, very close to the co-movement between the two series during earlier decades. Figure 2: Households have historically been good at predicting interest rate changes Source: Conference Board, FRB, Haver Analytics, Deutsche Bank Research What does the recent reading on net interest rate expectations imply for the Fed? Visually, it appears the recent spike in [the measure of net interest rate expectations] signals an increased likelihood of rate hikes over the remainder of the year. More formally, a regression of the year-over-year change in the fed funds rate on the lagged net interest rate expectations variable suggests that the current reading on [the measure of net interest rate expectations] is consistent with 47bps of rate hikes. [That the current reading on the measure of net interest rate expectations is consistent with 47bps of rate hikes] is the first time [the regression of the year-over-year change in the fed funds rate on the lagged net interest rate expectations variable] has implied a year-over-year increase in the fed funds rate since early 2024.
