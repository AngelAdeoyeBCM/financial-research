- Recent events in Venezuela raise the risk of lower oil prices. [The author sees] Brent falling to a trough of USD51/bbl in Q2.

Geopolitics in the driver’s seat: Recent events in Venezuela and rising tensions elsewhere have put geopolitics at the center of market discussions to start the year. The US December payrolls print was not as impactful to markets, while the Supreme Court’s IEEPA tariff ruling stands delayed to the coming weeks. The US strike on Venezuela bolsters [the author's] long-held approach of taking President Donald Trump both literally and seriously. In the immediate aftermath, oil prices were choppy with call skew bid. European and US defense stocks moved sharply higher and gold rallied. Duration and FX were relatively muted in response, suggesting other factors matter more than geopolitics for now (see Global Macro: Impact of Venezuelan regime shift, dated 8 January). Near-term bearish for oil ...: The most affected asset class was oil. Even though crude prices ticked up last week, [the author's] base case now assumes relief of sanctions on Venezuela, which w ould add meaningful supply to the market. [The author] therefore cut the expected trough in Brent by USD4 to USD51/bbl in Q2 2026 and envisage a USD15/bbl uncertainty range. Any boost to Venezuelan output would likely be gradual, but high storage levels might mean sanct ions relief is bearish for oil in the near term. Mixed message from US payrolls, CPI ahead: The December US jobs report tests [the author's] call for a March rate cut. With the unemployment rate declining and being revised down for November, concerns about a rapid accumulation of labor slack should diminish at the Fed. With monetary policy in accommodative territory in [the author's] view, and fiscal tailwinds providing a lift in H1 2026, [the author's] base case is that unemployment stays at the current 4.4%, with increasing risk that [the unemployment rate] falls amid constricted labor supply growth (see US December jobs: Glass-half-full for labor market, fuller for growth, dated 9 January). Yet the Fed opting to skip a Q1 cut would likely be a painful choice, as other aspects of [the December US jobs report] were weaker. Low headline job gains – especially if reduced by Fed Chair Powell’s claimed 60k mismeasurement – and a decline in hours suggest less-than-robust labor demand. High wages, which would ordinarily be worrisome at first glance, are probably not a problem because of extremely high productivity pushing unit labor costs sharply lower. Looking ahead to next week, the December CPI report will be closely watched, given many Fed policymakers have expressed concern about inflation being too high. [The author sees] December core CPI at 25bp – a rebound from the soft patch in October-November, but below consensus expectation of 30bp. A CPI print in line with [the author's] expectations is likely to keep the UST front end anchored, and the curve under a steepening bias. Still waiting on IEEPA: The US Supreme Court did not announce a ruling on IEEPA tariffs on Friday, delaying [the Supreme Court’s IEEPA tariff ruling] to a future date. Should IEEPA be struck down, [the author thinks] the administration has the legal authority to largely replace [IEEPA tariffs] via other paths, namely sections 122, 232 and 301. However, [that the administration has the legal authority to largely replace IEEPA tariffs via other paths, namely sections 122, 232 and 301] w ould take time, increasing trade uncertainty and creating volatility. A ruling against IEEPA could result in higher back-end yields and a steeper curve, mild broad-based USD weakness, and bullish price action in the Materials, Industrials and Consumer Discretionary sectors in equities. Early election in Japan? Japan’s largest newspaper reported on January 10, that Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi has begun considering the dissolution of the Lower House and holding a general election possibly on February 8 or February 15. In the election camp aign that could follow, the administration will likely highlight a ¥18 trillion supplemental budget. Nevertheless, even if the ruling party gains additional seats in the Lower House, a divided government would persist. The Bank of Japan’s policy outlook following [the Bank of Japan's] December rate hike is unlikely to be materiall y affected by [the early election in Japan]. However, if market expectations strengthen for Prime Minister Takaichi securing a decisive victory, [that market expectations strengthen for Prime Minister Takaichi securing a decisive victory] could exert renewed de preciation pressure on the ye n, and drive the market to bring forward the pricing of BoJ rate hikes. DM rates – supply supports steepeners: 2026 kicked off with record-high European government debt issuance, EUR65bn in the first week (versus EUR61bn in 2025). Still, [the record-high European government debt issuance] was well absorbed with yields remaining stable and spreads tightening. Demand traditionally peaks in Europe in January-February, so 2026 should follow that pattern. 10y EUR benchmark yields may stay within a narrow band, with peripheral spreads tightening, which would be aligned with [the author's] Q1 2026 forecast. Geopolitical tensions also add supply risk in the US. Trump has floated an extra $500bn for the defen se budget, which could lift 10y and 30y UST term premiums. Any dovish tilt by central banks amid [geopolitical tensions] would modestly dent [the author's] higher-yield view. [The author continues] to like 1s30s swap steepeners in the US. Over the course of the year, [the author continues] to expect higher yields and steeper curves.
