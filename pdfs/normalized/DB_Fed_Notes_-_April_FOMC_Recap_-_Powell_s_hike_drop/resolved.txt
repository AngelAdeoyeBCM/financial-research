# Fed Notes - April FOMC Recap - Powell's hike drop

Economics Fed Notes Date 29 April 2026

- The Fed held rates steady at the April FOMC meeting, and, in a close vote, maintained the mild cutting bias in the post-meeting statement. In [Chair Powell's] last press conference, Chair Powell confirmed that [Chair Powell] will stay on the Federal Reserve Board as a Governor “until it is appropriate to leave.”
- The press conference focused on two topics: the Committee’s evolving discussions around adopting a more balanced policy outlook and Powell’s future plans on the Board. On the former, Powell cited a “vigorous” discussion at the meeting and indicated that it is possible the guidance language becomes more balanced in June. On the latter, Powell emphasized the legal actions against the Fed and the associated risks to Fed independence as key reasons for [Chair Powell] deciding to stay on as a Governor.
- The Fed inching closer to a more balanced description of the policy outlook reinforces [the author's] baseline view that the policy rate is likely to stay unchanged this year. As [the author] outlined in [the author's] recent outlook update, [the author sees] the Fed on hold near neutral indefinitely (see “The (oil) drumbeat of war”). While a rate hike is no t a trivial probability, [the author continues] assess lower policy rates as more likely than higher over the course of this year. Meeting Statement Changes to the meeting statement language skewed in a slightly hawkish direction. However, the more overtly hawkish development was the three dissents to the statement’s unchanged forward guidance language. Regarding the statement language, first, the characterization of recent job gains was simplified to “remain low” from “remain low on average”. Second, the Committee upgraded [the Committee's] description of inflation from “somewhat elevated” to simply “elevated” – a risk that [the author] had highlighted in [the author's] preview. To be sure, the Committee made clear that elevated inflation was “in part reflecting the recent increase in global energy crisis”. The only other change to the meeting statement came in the second paragraph where the Committee simplified the language to reflect that developments in the Middle East were “continuing to contribute to a high level of uncertainty about the economic outlook.” There were four dissents. Governor Miran, who will be leaving the Board when Kevin Warsh is confirmed as Chair by the full Senate, registered [Governor Miran's] sixth, and likely final, dissent in favor of policy easing. More importantly, as noted above, three officials – Cleveland’s Hammack, Minnesota’s Kashkari and Dallas’ Logan – dissented to the slight easing bias in the forward guidance language of the Matthew Luzzetti, Ph.D. meeting statement. The dissenters may have objected to the continued inclusion of “additional adjustments” in [the meeting statement], language that was added when the FOMC began cutting rates. Recall that the minutes of the past two meetings had pointed to a growing chorus of officials who wanted the statement language to have a more neutral bias, allowing for the possibility that the next move could be a rate hike. While Powell noted in [Chair Powell's] press conference that “nobody’s calling for a hike right now”, [Chair Powell] also commented that “the center [of the Committee] is moving toward a more neutral place”, implying that the support for a more balanced expression of the risks to the dual mandate is broader than just the aforementioned three dissenting voting members. Press Conference Much of the press conference focused on two topics: the Committee’s evolving discussions around adopting a more balanced policy outlook and Powell’s future plans on the Board. With respect to the latter, i [Chair Powell's] prepared remarks, Powell stated [the press conference] is [Chair Powell's] last press conference as Chair, with [Chair Powell's] term ending May 15, but [Chair Powell] intends to remain on the Board as a Governor “for a period of time to be determined.” While [Chair Powell] noted that [Chair Powell] was “encouraged” by the steps taken towards the conclusion of the DoJ’s criminal investigation, Powell said that [Chair Powell] will stay on un til [the DoJ’s criminal investigation] “is well and truly over with transparency and finality”. Powell explicitly framed [Chair Powell's] decision singularly in terms of pushing back against attacks on the Fed’s independence. Indeed, [Chair Powell] noted that there were widespread concerns that the legal att acks on the Fed would continue. [Chair Powell] also expressed worry that these “attacks are battering the institution and putting at risk the thing that matters to the public, which is the ability to conduct monetary policy without taking into consideration political factors.” In terms of what [that Chair Powell intends to remain on the Board as a Governor] would look like after Warsh is confirmed, Powell rejected any notion of being a “shadow Chair”, noting that there’s “only ever one Chair of the Federal Reserve Board” and when Warsh is “confirmed and sworn in he will be that Chair.” As such, Powell intends to keep a “low profile” as Governor and “not interfere”. [Chair Powell] commented that it was difficult to get consensus among “nineteen strong minded people” and so would not want to make things unnecessarily more difficult for a new Chair. After [Chair Powell] steps down as Chair, [the author] would expect few, if any, speeches by Powell while [Chair Powell] remains on the Board and a high bar to dissent at any Warsh-led FOMC meetings. On the policy outlook, Powell cited a “vigorous” debate around the guidance language in [the meeting statement]. [Chair Powell] noted that the group of officials supporting an adoption of more balanced language continued to grow between the March and April FOMC meetings. Nonetheless, a majority of officials supported keeping the mild cutting bias in the policy statement at [the April FOMC meeting]. Powell noted [Chair Powell] was in [the majority of officials], and for [Chair Powell] and other officials in [the majority of officials] [keeping the mild cutting bias in the policy statement] was supported by several factors: [keeping the mild cutting bias in the policy statement] maintained continuity in the statement during a period of uncertainty, [keeping the mild cutting bias in the policy statement] a voided a significant change to guidance ahead of a transition in Fed leadership, and there was little cost to waiting another month before making [the adoption of more balanced language]. [That there was little cost to waiting another month before making the adoption of more balanced language] was best summarized by [Chair Powell's] question “why do we need to do that now?” Implicit in [the framing that there was little cost to waiting another month before making the adoption of more balanced language] were hints that the language could change in June. On the potential for rate hikes, Powell reiterated that “nobody is calling for a hike right now” but that, “if we need to hike, we will certainly signal that and we will certainly do it.” Amongst the factors that will determine any upcoming change in language and the direction and timing of the next move, Powell sounded relatively upbeat on economic fundamentals but cautious about the inflation outlook. On the former, Powell emphasized th at the labor market is showing “more and more signs of stability” and cited the current 4.3% unemployment rate as still low and near estimates of NAIRU. The resilient labor market is being supported by solid domestic demand growth (PDFP), including “resilient” consumer spending, business fixed investment expanding at a “brisk pace”, and an “insatiable” demand for data centers. On inflation, Powell cited March core PCE at 3.2% year-over-year and sounded more cautious about the outlook, stating that inflation has been “misbehaving”. [Chair Powell] noted again that the Fed expects tariff-driven inflation effects to begin to wane over the next two quarters and that the Fed would need to see [tariff-driven inflation effects to begin to wane over the next two quarters] occur before considering a rate cut based solely on inflation. Moreover, [Chair Powell] suggested that the “prospects are real” that the energy price shock could spill over to core inflation, though [that the energy price shock could spill over to core inflation] can only be determined over time. Powell continued to describe policy as “well positioned”. [Chair Powell] also seemed to signal that [Chair Powell's] estimate of neutral continues to c reep higher. While [Chair Powell] described policy as “at the upper end” of neutral ([Chair Powell] cited a range of 3 -4%) or slightly restrictive, at one point [Chair Powell] stated policy is “pretty close to the neutral rate”. With the economy continuing to prove resilient to significant shocks, and inflation remaining sticky at elevated levels, it would not be surprising for more officials to move closer to [the author's] view that the current policy rate is essentially at neutral. Powell was also asked about communications issues. [Chair Powell] noted that last year officials had started to di scuss potential changes to [officials'] communications framework but could not reach a consensus on any meaningful adjustments. While Powell mentioned [Chair Powell] “was never the world’s biggest fan of the dot plot”, [Chair Powell] also noted that “you can’t beat something with nothing.” [Chair Powell] also said that the Fed is the only major central bank that does not produce a consensus forecast. Together, these comments provided soft support for the Fed’s communications strategy, which [Chair Powell] labeled “fine”. Nonetheless, Powell supported a further review of communications under Warsh, noting “looking at doing [communications] in a different and better way is the most natural thing in the world.” Powell’s tenure ends as Fed chair with a resilient economy, a labor market near target, but inflation still too high.
