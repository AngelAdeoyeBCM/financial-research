# Fed Notes - Warsh and Peace

Economics Fed Notes Date 26 May 2026

“We can know only that we know nothing.” – War and Peace, Leo Tolstoy Introduction This weekend’s headlines indicate some further progress in reaching a deal to extend the ceasefire with Iran and potentially reopen traffic in the Strait of Hormuz. Uncertainty remains around the details, and the more contentious issues around Iran’s nuclear program are unresolved. Nevertheless, markets have interpreted recent headlines optimistically, with Brent oil futures falling below $100 per barrel to the lowest level in a month. Treasury yields also dropped, with 10-year Treasury yields erasing most of the rise over the past week. What would a near-term breakthrough in negotiations that lead to a reopening of the Strait and a further sharp drop in oil prices mean for the Fed outlook? [The author discusses] three scenarios for negotiations and what [the three scenarios for negotiations] mean for the Fed. Scenario 1: Peace deal – Near-term hike risks subside, but hike potential remains further out In [Scenario 1: Peace deal], oil prices continue to retrace some of the recent sharp increase but stay persistently above prices observed prior to the war. Treasury yields also partially retrace some of the increase observed over the past few weeks and broad financial conditions ease further as risk asset markets remove a tail risk around re - intensification. Pressure on the Fed to hike rates over the coming meetings will diminish somewhat, as headline inflation comes in soft and near-term inflation expectations reverse some of the recent increase. Fed officials will likely be inclined to “look-through” near-term core inflation pressures that might be tied to the recent run-up in oil prices, treating elevated energy prices as another temporary shock lifting inflation. [The author suspects] new Fed chair Warsh will reinforce [the inclination that Fed officials will likely be inclined to “look-through” near-term core inflation pressures that might be tied to the recent run-up in oil prices]. The default narrative that elevated inflation is unlikely to be persistent will take time to be disproven and will likely require some combination of a tightening labor market, further rise in inflation expectations, or continued elevated inflation readings beyond the period where tariffs and energy pressures should subside. But rate hike risks will remain in [Scenario 1: Peace deal], as [the author sees] reasons that neutral is higher than the Fed anticipates, inflation could prove to be stickier, and the labor market is likely to be resilient. Risks of higher policy rates are more likely to be realized in 2027. Scenario 2: Peace deal fails and muddle through continues – Greater risk of 2026 hikes In [Scenario 2: Peace deal fails and muddle through continues], [a deal to extend the ceasefire with Iran] fails, and the Strait of Hormuz remains closed for a longer period, but there is no re-intensification in the war. Oil prices are likely to remain elevated but not spike materially higher. [The author sees] [Scenario 2: Peace deal fails and muddle through continues] as presenting the greatest risk of rate hikes this year. Persistently elevated oil prices could spill over more significantly into core inflation. Moreover, inflation expectations are at greater risk of unanchoring the longer oil prices remain elevated. At the same time, oil prices in [Scenario 2: Peace deal fails and muddle through continues] are not high Matthew Luzzetti, Ph.D. enough to significantly damage demand and force the Fed to focus on the labor market side of the dual mandate objective. 2026 rate hikes are a real risk under [Scenario 2: Peace deal fails and muddle through continues]. [The author suspects] the Fed would be unlikely to hike rates by the September FOMC meeting – it will take time for the Fed to remove the cutting bias (June), have some officials argue for rate hikes (July - September), and then have the Committee agree that a rate hike is needed. However, Governor Waller’s comment that rate hikes could be warranted if “inflation does not abate soon” (emphasis added), suggests that the Fed may be willing to move quickly to tighten policy in response to continued upward pressure on oil and broader prices. [The author] therefore would not rule out (multiple) 2026 rate hikes in [Scenario 2: Peace deal fails and muddle through continues]. Scenario 3: Re-escalation – Two-sided risks to the policy outlook The final scenario envisions a re-escalation of tensions in Iran, which leads to a further sustained spike in oil prices. While one could argue [Scenario 3: Re-escalation] presents the greater risk of Fed rate increases, [the author thinks] [Scenario 3: Re-escalation] is likely to impart two-sided risks to the policy outlook. On one hand, a re-escalation in tensions will lead to a more prolonged and severe spike in headline inflation that risks a more significant and sustained pass-through to core inflation. As a result, an un-anchoring of inflation expectations would be a real risk. The Fed would need to be vigilant against [an un-anchoring of inflation expectations] by communicating a willingness to tighten policy in response to these emergent price stability risks. On the other hand, a significant further spike in oil prices that is sustained would increase the risk of non-linear effects on the real economy that could ultimately spill over into meaningfully weaker labor market conditions. Consumers can weather energy prices near current levels for some time as tax cuts offset higher prices at the pump. But that buffer would be eliminated with a further step up in oil and gas prices. [A further step up in oil and gas prices] could tip the labor market out of the current uncomfortable equilibrium of low hiring and firing, with labor demand potentially falling further or layoffs finally picking up. The Fed would therefore also need to be attentive to risks to the labor market. Indeed, [the Fed] have shown themselves to be proactive in responding to labor market weakness in recent years. Ultimately, the Fed’s response would come down to the sequencing of the realization of [the two-sided risks to the policy outlook]. A still resilient economy faced with real risks of an un-anchoring of inflation expectations would require a robust tightening response. Conversely, if non-linear effects became quickly evident with the labor market cracking, the Fed could point to soft forward-looking price pressures as [the Fed] lean towards cuts.
