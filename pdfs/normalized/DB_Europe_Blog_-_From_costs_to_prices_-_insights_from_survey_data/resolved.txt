# Europe Blog - From costs to prices - insights from survey data

Economics Europe Blog Date 15 April 2026

The escalation of the conflict in the Middle East has prompted a renewed build-up of inflationary pressures. From a central bank perspective, the key question is whether higher input costs will be absorbed by margins or passed through into final output prices. The latter would have implications for the path of core inflation. In this blog [the author] examine the latest signals from survey-based price indicators to assess the extent of current cost pass-through in manufacturing. The economic costs from the energy shock are already visible. Headline inflation in the euro area rose by 0.6pp in March to 2.5% YoY – the highest since Jan-25 – largely driven by higher fuel prices. Survey-based indicators point to a similar picture, with the composite PMI input prices index increasing sharply across the board (Figure 1). The rise in output prices, however, was smaller and mixed across countries, with France even recording a slight decline (Figure 2). [That the rise in output prices, however, was smaller and mixed across countries] suggests that firms in both services and manufacturing are currently absorbing part of the increase in production costs through [firms in both services and manufacturing's] margins (Figures 3 and 4). Across sectors, the rise was more pronounced in manufacturing. In the euro area, input prices jumped by 10.8pts in March while output prices were up by 3.2pts. In services, input prices saw a 5pt increase while output prices were broadly unchanged. A similar pattern is evident in the European Commission’s survey data, where selling price expectations rose by 3.9pts for consumer goods and by 0.8pts for services. Taken together, survey-based price indicators point to further upside risks to headline inflation (Figures 5 and 6). Across the largest member states (Figures 7- 10), [upside risks to headline inflation] appear more pronounced in Italy and Spain. [The author has] previously shown that [Italy and Spain] are the most exposed in terms of the energy import bill. In consequence, [Italy and Spain] are also the countries where the largest fiscal support measures have been implemented. Assessing the passthrough from input to output prices in manufacturing To help contextualize the magnitude of monthly movements in the PMI price indicators, [the author uses] simple linear regression models to assess the passthrough from input to output prices. In a first step, [the author models] input costs as a function of key commodity prices (oil, gas and metals), the exchange rate and new orders as a proxy for demand. [The author] then estimate output prices as a function of input costs (including lags), while controlling for new orders and delivery times. Figures 11 and 12 show that the March increase in both input and output prices was broadly in line with what [simple linear regression models] would predict given the sharp moves in commodities – somewhat stronger in the case of input prices. The figures also illustrate how [the March increase in both input and output prices] could evolve over the coming months, based on a simple scenario in which commodity prices follow current forward curves, with all other factors held constant. Under [the assumptions that commodity prices follow current forward curves, with all other factors held constant], [simple linear regression models] suggests that input cost pressures could rise somewhat further in April, before gradually moderating, albeit remaining well above pre-war levels. Output prices would continue to trend higher for some months, stabilising also above pre-war levels by the end of the year. That said, both indicators would still be below the 2022 peaks. The implied increase in manufacturing output prices poses clear upside risks to goods inflation over the next 6 -12 months (Figure 13). If [upside risks to goods inflation] were to materialise, core goods HICP could rise to an average of 0.9% YoY in 2026 vs [the author's] current forecast of 0.5%. Beyond energy-driven cost pressures, c oncerns around the emergence of broader supply chain disruptions are rising. The March PMIs already provided some evidence of mounting stress, with supplier s’ delivery times lengthening to the greatest extent since September 2022. [Broader supply chain disruptions] could amplify [the energy shock] (Figure 14). In a recent note, [the author finds] evidence for the UK that the passthrough from PMI output prices to inflation is stronger when supply chains are tighter.
