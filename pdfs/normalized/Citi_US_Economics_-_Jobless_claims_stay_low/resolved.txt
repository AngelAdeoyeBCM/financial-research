# US Economics Jobless claims stay low

## CITI’S TAKE

Initial and continuing jobless claims have been remarkably stable in recent weeks at low levels. 1716k continuing claims and 197k initial claims suggest downside risk to the measured unemployment rate and should keep Fed officials and markets assessing the labor market as “stable,” keeping the focus firmly on inflation. n | Data – Initial claims for the week of October 3rd registered 197k, close to levels that have prevailed over the last three weeks. Continued claims for the week of September 26th increased slightly to a still low 1716k from 1699k. Citi’s View – Jobless claims have been remarkably stable at low levels over the last three weeks. [That Jobless claims have been remarkably stable at low levels over the last three weeks] should reinforce Fed official and market perceptions that the labor market is “stable.” More hawkish Fed officials will likely interpret the sustained lower level of claims as showing that the labor market may be strengthening, supporting the view that labor market concerns should not dissuade the Fed from continuing to raise policy rates. Continuing jobless claims are running around 1700k, down from 1800k in the summer. [That Continuing jobless claims are running around 1700k, down from 1800k in the summer] might be partly due to residual seasonality as continued claims dropped around the same time last year and then climbed again in late October. But the 100k drop this year is larger than the less than 50k drop that occurred last year. Continued claims should generally move together with the unemployment rate, and [the sustained lower level of claims] makes it more likely that the unemployment rate falls from [the unemployment rate's] current 4.18%. But the relationship between claims and the measured unemployment rate is complicated by shifts in demographics and labor force participation. While [the author agrees] with the characterization of the labor market as “stable,” the full set of labor market data does not indicate a tightening labor market that will generate inflation. Low claims are partly due to low “churn,” with the rate of hiring, layoffs, and quits all more subdued than is typical. Consistent with weak hiring demand, wage growth as measured by average hourly earnings has been trended down to 3% YoY, and the Atlanta Fed Wage Tracker shows wage growth moving sideways at around 4%, about where [wage growth] ran just prior to the pandemic.
