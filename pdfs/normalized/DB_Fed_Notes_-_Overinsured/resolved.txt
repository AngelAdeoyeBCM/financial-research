# Fed Notes - Overinsured

Economics Fed Notes Date 22 May 2026 Overinsured? [The author has] written extensively about the potential for Fed rate hikes this year and the recent repricing in Fed expectations (see “Could the Fed hike rates in 2026?”, “The curious curves in SOFR”, “Has the Fed taken it too easy?”). In [the author's] view, it is entirely appropriate for the Fed to contemplate rate hikes. [The author has] long thought that the neutral real fed funds rate is materially above Fed estimates, and with growth solid, the labor market showing increasing signs of stabilization, inflation accelerating, and fiscal policy and financial conditions supportive, the Fed’s policy stance might be miscalibrated. Over the past two years, the Fed has enacted 175bps of rate cuts even as inflation has remained well above target. The recent round of [the 175bps of rate cuts] was framed as “insurance” or “risk management” cuts1, with the Fed bringing the policy rate closer to neutral in response to elevated downside labor market risks that emerged in the second half of 2024 and 2025. The insurance aspect of [the insurance or risk management cuts] was intended to forestall weaker labor market outcomes and better position policy to respond to risks should they emerge. A reasonable pushback to this framing – that the Fed has become overinsured against downside risks to the labor market – is that [the elevated downside labor market risks] are still prominent. As such, it might be prudent for the Fed to shade the policy rate at least somewhat 2 [The standard policy rules] are cited in the Fed’s biannual “Monetary Policy Report”, e.g., see Monetary Policy Report June 2025 below policy rule prescriptions to manage th ese risks. However, recent commentary from the Fed suggests that that justification has weakened in recent months. At the April FOMC press conference, Chair Powell noted “The labor market shows more and more signs of stability, whereas inflation is kind of misbehaving.” In addition, the minutes to [the April FOMC] noted that the Fed staff viewed persistent inflation pressures as a “salient risk”. Fed policy may therefore have become overinsured against downside risks to the labor market. As a result, prospects for future rate hikes might best be interpreted as a prudent reversal of [the insurance or risk management cuts] as the balance of risks tilts towards inflation.
