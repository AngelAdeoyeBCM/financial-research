# Europe - Greenland crisis - Escalating rapidly into a new trade war

Economics Europe Blog Date 19 January 2026

– The Greenland crisis has escalated rapidly. On Saturday President Trump announced a 10% tariff on the eight European countries that last week committed additional military resources of the security of Greenland and the Arctic. The tariff applies from 1 February until a deal is done for the US to purchase Greenland and will step up to 25% from 1 June. – In this note [the author] give an initial assessment of the importance of [the 10% tariff on the eight European countries] from the European point of view, with focus on the economics – there will be a complementary note from [the author's] Geopolitics team. Critically, the tariff threat means the Greenland crisis now moves into the economic arena in addition to the military and diplomatic arenas. On the one hand, introducing a trade nexus increases the potential cost of a shock to geopolitical uncertainty. On the other hand, unlike on the military side, the EU has some trade leverage and a new tool to project [the EU's trade leverage]: the Anti-Coercion Instrument (ACI). – How [the Greenland crisis] plays out is highly uncertain. [The author thinks] about the factors that will determine the path forward from a European point of view. Ultimately, [the author would] see the mutually optimal outcome as a cooperative solution in which the US gets access to Greenland for [the US's] strategic needs (defence, minerals, shipping), sovereignty is preserved and both sides benefit from an improvement in North Atlantic security. To get there, the EU will need to be strong. That is, be politically cohesive, willing to accept some cost and willing to take some risk. The threat to the sovereignty of a region of the EU could be the game-changer for Europe’s response. President Trump announced on Saturday that the US will apply 10% tariffs to the eight European countries who last week committed additional military resources to the security of Greenland and the Arctic (Denmark, UK, Norway, Sweden, France, Germany, Netherlands and Finland). Trump said the 10% tariff will apply to all goods from 1 February until a deal is reached for the “complete and total purchase of Greenland”. [The 10% tariff on the eight European countries] will step up to 25% from 1 June. [The 10% tariff on the eight European countries] moves the next stage of the Greenland crisis very clearly into the economics arena in addition to t he military and diplomatic arenas. [That the 10% tariff on the eight European countries moves the next stage of the Greenland crisis very clearly into the economics arena in addition to t he military and diplomatic arenas] is important in a number of ways which [the author] discussed in the note [the author] published on Friday evening. There [the author discusses] the EU’s options for dealing with the Greenland situation, the potential impacts of the crisis and the longer-term ramifications for Europe from more frictional geopolitics. 1/ Costs of geopolitical uncertainty shocks can be much larger. Greenland is the latest geopolitical shock under Trump 2.0. Geopolitical shocks on their own are not necessarily that economically costly. [The author estimates] that the increase in political risk since December (prior to the Greenland tariff threat; part of the increase will relate to Venezuela and Iran) imply a 0.2pp of GDP cost to the EU, if the shock is sustained for a year. The economic costs escalate when there is an amplifier for the geo political shock to transmit more broadly through an economy. Classic geopolitical amplifiers are energy, financing and trade. With the US using tariffs to create geopolitical leverage, the costs of the rise in geopolitical uncertainty are likely to be higher. 2/ EU has some economic power – and tools to project [the EU's economic power]. The US is the world’s dominant military power, as recent events in Venezuela demonstrated. But by moving the Greenland crisis into the economic arena, [the Greenland crisis] becomes more balanced between the EU and US in terms of power and leverage. Importantly, tariffs are a form of economic coercion. [That tariffs are a form of economic coercion] brings the EU’s ‘trade bazooka’ into play – it was more ambiguous whether the EU could use the Anti-Coercion Instrument (ACI) in a purely military situation. The implementation is slow, but powers under the ACI are wide-ranging, from imposing tariffs to import [or] export restrictions to revoking protection of intellectual property rights or [intellectual property rights'] commercial exploitation (e.g., software downloads and streaming services. Action could also include blocking foreign direct investment or restricting market access for banking, insurance and other financial services groups. For more on the ACI, see [the author's] previous analysis here. 3/ Does the EU still fear US ‘escalation dominance’? The EU did not use the ACI in response to last year’s US tariffs. As [the author] detailed in Friday’s note, the EU has certain points of economic leverage over the US. The EU dominates chemical (including pharma) imports into the US, for example. The Single Market is also a significant market for US exports. Restrict ing access would be costly to the US and is possible under the ACI. But the US has leverage too. The US dominates tech service imports into Europe and has an increasingly significant role in European energy imports. [The author also knows] from how President Trump reacted to other countries who pushed back last year that if the EU retaliates, the US will [retaliates] too, and the costs could quickly rise for both sides. 4/ Ukraine security a constraint on EU response. Arguably it was the EU’s concern about the stability of NATO and in particular US backing for Ukraine security (access to military equipment and defence intelligence in particular) that dominated [the EU's] decision-making last year and steered [the EU] away from using the ACI – [the EU’s concern about the stability of NATO and in particular US backing for Ukraine security] continues to apply today. However, there is an additional element that could work in the EU’s favour: the new US tariffs would likely be implemented under IEEPA legislation and the re is a good chance that the US Supreme Court rules against IEEPA tariffs in the near future. 5/ Greenland sovereignty a game-changer. The EU has powerful tools like the ACI. Greenland’s sovereignty might be the game-changer in the sense that [Greenland’s sovereignty] should make the EU more willing than [the EU] was last year to use the ACI even though [the Anti-Coercion Instrument (ACI)] likely means additional costs alongside the leverage [the Anti-Coercion Instrument (ACI)] creates. Antonio Costa, the President of the European Council, says the EU will be “very firm in defending international law...starting with the territory of the European Union’s member states”. There has been criticism of Trump’s recent foreign interventions from leaders on Euro pe’s far-right, including AfD’s Alice Weidel and RN’s Jordan Bardella (“Trump’s Greenland play is a step too far for Europe’s far right”, Bloomberg, 17 January 2026). [The criticism of Trump’s recent foreign interventions from leaders on Euro pe’s far-right] signals that Europe could be more cohesive in how is responds to the Greenland crisis. Bernd Lange, the head of the European Parliament trade committee and Valerie Hayer, the head of the Renew Europe group in the EP (centrist, liberal), have both expressed support for ACI. President Macron says [President Macron] will be contacting [President Macron's] European Council counterparts to ask for the ACI. The head of the VDMA, the German engineering federation, has also come out in favour of ACI – signalling that important and exposed business sectors can be onside with the decision. 6/ Europe needs cohesion... The first thing to watch is the political cohesiveness of the EU. For Europe to be able to take a strong stance against the US and use strong policy options [Europe] needs political cohesion. The EU Ambassadors meeting on Sunday 18 January will be the first cha nce to start judging [the political cohesiveness of the EU]. European Finance Ministers are meeting this week too – Eurogroup on Monday, EcoFin on Tuesday (the US tariffs could contribute to delaying the Eurogroup announcement of a candidate to replace Luis de Guin dos as ECB Vice President when [Luis de Guin dos'] term ends in May). Procedurally, ACI is slow moving: a formal assessment of the coercion needed, followed by attempted dialogue and de - escalation. The policy recommendations need to be targeted and proportionate. [The Anti-Coercion Instrument (ACI)] was designed as a deterrent and the multi-stage app roval process creates time for negotiation to avoid [the Anti-Coercion Instrument (ACI)]. However, the ACI only requires only a qualified majority vote, not a unanimous vote. 7/ ...and a strategy. The focus between now and 1 February will be on trying to find a diplomatic solution to [the Greenland crisis] that avoids tariffs and maintains Greenland’s sovereignty. Formally initiating the ACI process might not be the first step in Europe’s strategy. According to the FT, the initial step could be implementing the EUR93bbn of retaliatory tariffs that had been prepared last year. Delaying the final ratification of last year’s EU-US trade deal is another possibility. [Last year’s EU-US trade deal], which reduces tariffs on US imports to zero and pledges hundreds of billions of dollars of investment in the US and energy imports from the US, has not been approved by the European Parliament yet. The European Commissioner for trade says that approval looks “very complicated” given the latest events. Europe is likely to view [the Greenland crisis] as a classic prisoner’s dilemma. There is a cooperative outcome in which the US gets sufficient access to Greenland to implement [the US's] Golden Dome defence strategy and access to critical mineral deposits, Arctic securit y improves, Greenland’s sovereignty is preserved and NATO cooperation (and by extension North Atlantic security) is strengthened. Whether the choices made by the US and EU in the coming days and weeks will lead to [the cooperative outcome in which the US gets sufficient access to Greenland] is highly uncertain – and of course the US may view the situation in different terms to the EU. To try to get to [the cooperative outcome in which the US gets sufficient access to Greenland], the EU is likely to try a carrot-and-stick strategy: stronger commitments to Greenland and Arctic security on the one hand balanced with threats to retaliate against US tariffs should [the 10% tariff on the eight European countries] go ahead on the other (suspend US-EU trade deal, initiate ACI, etc). 8/ EU needs deep-seated change to be fit for frictional geopolitics. The EU was built on trade, openness, integration, multilateralism and the rule of law and is ill - suited to a world of great power rivalry. The EU cannot compete – does not have the power to assert itself and protect [the EU's] interests – economically or politically in [the world of great power rivalry]. Several policies are needed to correct the imbalance, including raising defence spending, accelerating strategic autonomy, rethinking Europe’s security strategy and solving the collective action challenge. None of [the policies needed to correct the imbalance] can happen quickly, not least when there is political fragmentation and elections are approaching in key member states.
