# UK Weekly Digest - How high will it go - A look at the 2026-27 gilt remit revision

Economics UK Weekly Digest Date 16 October 2026

As [the author] inch closer to the 28th of October, investors will be keeping a close eye on another fiscal announcement on Budget Day: the 2026/27 gilt remit revision. By all accounts, cash borrowing will almost certainly be higher. In this Weekly, [the author] set out [the author's] expectations for the government's central government net cash requirement (CGNCR) in the current fiscal year. [The author] also examine how the Debt Management Office's gilt remit could change as a result. How is cash borrowing evolving in 2026/27? Five months into the fiscal year, cash borrowing (i.e. CGNCR) is tracking nearly GBP 3bn ahead of the OBR's forecast. Figure 1: Cash borrowing receipts are running a little ahead of expectations Figure 2: [central government net cash requirement] gap sitting at GBP 3bn following the August public finances report The good news? It's not weaker receipts that are pushing borrowing higher. In fact, outturn data show HMRC cash receipts running nearly GBP 3bn ahead of the OBR's projections, as the economy continues to grow faster than the OBR anticipated for 2026. What's lifting receipts? PAYE receipts alone are running GBP 3.1bn above the OBR's April-August projections. NIC receipts are also tracking slightly higher, coming in GBP 1.5bn above the fiscal watchdog's forecast. Corporation tax cash receipts are also ex ceeding the OBR's projections by around GBP 1.4bn. Together, [HMRC cash receipts running nearly GBP 3bn ahead of the OBR's projections] have more than offset weaker VAT and self-assessment receipts. What's the bad news then? Spending has exceeded projections. While interest costs (on a cash basis) have come in a little below the OBR's forecast, higher departmental spending and recently announced policy measures have likely contributed to [the current GBP 3bn central government net cash requirement overshoot]. Net social benefit payments have also risen, as evidenced by the detailed central government spending data on an accruals basis. What's changing? Four factors impacting [central government net cash requirement] for 2026/27 To assess where [central government net cash requirement] could go this fiscal year, [the author looks] at four different factors. Underlying spending increase. First, [the author estimates] the seasonally adjusted "persistent" element of the current [central government net cash requirement] overshoot. [The seasonally adjusted "persistent" element of the current central government net cash requirement overshoot] gives [the author] a sense of how much of the current overspend is likely to persist through the remainder of the fiscal year. [The author does] this by stripping out policy measures announced since the Spring Statement that are already in place. On [the author's] calculations, around GBP 2bn of the current GBP 3bn [central government net cash requirement] overshoot reflects underlying trends, driven mainly by higher departmental and investment spending, alongside stronger growth in net social benefit payments. Extrapolating [around GBP 2bn of the current GBP 3bn central government net cash requirement overshoot] across the remainder of the fiscal year would imply around GBP 2bn of additional cash borrowing. Policy announcements. Since the Spring Statement, eight key policy measures have been announced, including a temporary fuel duty extension, the HGV VED holiday, an increase in Mileage Allowance rates, food tariff suspensions, the Great British Summer Savings scheme, a Chemicals and Ceramics Fund, and the unfunded portion of the Defence Investment Plan. In addition, [the author expects] several further measures to be announced in the Budget that would come into effect this fiscal year, including a second fuel duty extension, an increase i the Warm Homes Discount, a potential VAT cut on gas bills, and some form of business energy support. [The author] convert accrued and planned spending into cash spending for the current fiscal year to estimate the impact on [central government net cash requirement]. In total, [the author estimates] that policy support will add GBP 4.4bn to borrowing this fiscal year. Market conditions. There are two channels at play here. Higher interest rates will lead to higher borrowing, even in cash terms. [Higher interest rates] will come through higher financing costs on T-bills, NS&I liabilities and new gilt issuance relative to what the OBR assumed in the Spring Statement. [The author estimates] [higher interest rates] will add around GBP 0.7bn to cash borrowing over the remaining seven months. From a macro perspective, however, higher energy prices should boost VAT receipts and Energy Profits Levy revenues. Stronger inflation also implies faster nominal GDP growth. Based on current market conditions, [the author estimates] [higher energy prices] will reduce cash bo rrowing by around GBP 1.3bn. Net-net, [the author estimates] market conditions will lower [central government net cash requirement] by around GBP 0.6bn. QT. The BoE's latest QT decision will have important implications for cash borrowing, particularly this fiscal year. For one, the size of the APF will be larger than the OBR expected, with no sales taking place between October 2026 and March 2027. [That the size of the APF will be larger than the OBR expected] increases the carry cost of the APF. [The author estimates] the additional interest cost at around GBP 1.8bn. The offset, however, comes through lower valuation losses. In the Spring Statement, the OBR estimated valuation costs of just under GBP 10bn in 2026/27. Given the OBR's assumption of GBP 32bn of active sales beyond the October 2025-September 2026 QT year, [the BoE's latest QT decision] implies around GBP 16bn fewer gilts sold in Q4-26 and Q1-27. Applying a blended loss rate (40/40/20 across short, medium and long maturities), [the author estimates] valuation costs will be around GBP 2.4bn lower. Where does this leave [central government net cash requirement] for 2026/27? Putting [the four different factors] together, [the author estimates] the net effect would increase cash borrowing by around GBP 6.5bn this fiscal year. [The net effect] would take [central government net cash requirement] from an unrevised GBP 137.2bn to GBP 143.7bn. Figure 3: How high do we think [central government net cash requirement] can go? Source: Deutsche Bank, OBR How will the gilt remit change on 28 October? How should investors think about the remit? An upward revision to the gilt remit seems inevitable to [the author]. But gilts need not absorb the entire increase in cash borrowing. The DMO has another funding tool at [the DMO's] disposal: T-bills. With the T-bill consultation now concluded, and the DMO expected to increase T-bill issuance as part of future funding plans, [the author thinks] a modest upgrade is likely at the Autumn Budget. [A modest upgrade] would represent a small stepping stone towards a larger contribution from bills within the 2027/28 funding remit. How far can the DMO push T-bills? With GBP 5bn already allocated towards funding the 2026/27 borrowing requirement, [the author sees] a plausible range of GBP 1 - 3bn in additional T-bill issuance being announced on 28 October. [GBP 1 - 3bn in additional T-bill issuance] would also help offset the slowdown in NS&I inflows seen so far this year ([the author currently expects] net NS&I financing to reach GBP 11 -14bn by the end of the fiscal year, although recent increases in savings rates should support stronger take-up). Figure 4: NS&I savings tracking well below target so far Figure 5: T-bill changes likely to be marginal, we think, for this fiscal year What does this mean for gilts? [The author assumes] NS&I financing expectations are revised only marginally lower, towards the top end of [the author's] range (GBP 14bn), reflecting higher savings rates. Indeed, HMT has rarely revised [HMT's] NS&I assumptions during a fiscal year. The only change in the last seven years came in the previous Autumn Budget, when HMT increased [HMT's] NS&I target by GBP 1bn. [The author also assumes] T-bills issuance will increase by GBP 2bn, the midpoint of [the author's] estimated range. Where would this leave the 2026/27 gilt remit revision? Based on [the author's] assumptions, [the author expects] projected gilt sales to increase by a relatively modest GBP 5.5bn this fiscal year, taking total expected gilt sales for 2026/27 to GBP 251.8bn.
