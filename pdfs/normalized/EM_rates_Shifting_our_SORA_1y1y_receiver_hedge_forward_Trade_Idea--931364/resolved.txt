# EM rates: Shifting our SORA 1y1y receiver hedge forward

[The author is] running a SORA M27,1y receiver with a payer in FOMC Dec [or] Mar spread (see EM rates: Receive SORA 1y1y against paid FOMC Dec [or] Mar, dated 13 July). [The author] now shift [the author's] FOMC Dec [or] Mar payer to an Oct [or] Jan payer, as the risk of an earlier start to the Fed hiking cycle has risen. [The author now forecasts] three rate hikes from the Fed, starting in September (Sep, Dec and Jan), although the upcoming PPI [or] CPI prints remain a key risk (see Sunday Tea with BNP Paribas – CPI of the storm, dated 6 September). The existing Dec [or] Mar spread payer has hedged unfavorable rises in SORA rates, while [the author's] received position in SORA rates has earned about 8bp in carry and rolldown since entry (Figure 1). However, with the possibility of the Fed starting [the Fed's] hiking cycle in September, [the author sees] a risk that [the author's] existing Dec [or] Mar FOMC hedge may not be the best hedge. As markets front-load rate-hike expectations, the Dec [or] Mar spread could flatten, undermining [the Dec or Mar spread's] protection against further sell-offs in SORA rates. To mitigate [that the Dec or Mar spread could flatten, undermining the Dec or Mar spread's protection against further sell-offs in SORA rates], [the author believes] it is prudent to roll [the author's] hedge forward to the Oct [or] Jan FOMC spread. This Oct [or] Jan spread covers the December and January FOMC meetings, which occur right after the US mid-term elections. [The Oct or Jan FOMC spread] is trading at 21bp and has more scope to steepen with Fed starting the hiking cycle soon, in [the author's] view. [The author] opt to exclude the September FOMC meeting, as the risk-reward appears less attractive at current levels – markets are already pricing in 15–16bp for [the September FOMC meeting], while August CPI remains a risk. Continue to favor front-end SORA receivers: [The author continues] to like [the author's] receiver in SORA rates, as the curve remains steep. Although the loan-to-deposit ratio has increased in recent months, [the loan-to-deposit ratio] has not translated into higher SORA fixings, which suggests that systemic liquidity remains ample. Given that the rise in front-end SORA rates over the last two months has been largely driven by the increase in USD rates (Figure 2), [the author still favors] front-end SORA receivers at these levels, hedged with US rates.
