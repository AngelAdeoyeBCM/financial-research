# UK Economics A soft start to the year for GDP

## CITI’S TAKE

Data this morning signals a fragile start to 2026, with underlying momentum possibly even weaker than the headline figures imply. The economy is increasingly reliant on a narrow set of growth drivers, while post-shock normalisations continue to flatter the data even as headwinds start to show. The balance of risk now tilts below [the author's] 0.3% QQ growth for Q1 as exogenous shocks likely materialise in the coming months. Services headwinds build. Services output stalled in January (0.0% m/m), undershooting expectations for a modest expansion and masking a notable loss of breadth across the service economy. Beneath the flat headline, performance was highly uneven: seven of fourteen sectors grew, but six contracted, and a single category—administrative [and] support services, down 2.3% m/m—was large enough to offset gains elsewhere. The bulk of [administrative and support services, down 2.3% m/m] came from employment services, which fell 5.7% m/m and constituted the largest negative contribution to both services and total GDP, suggesting a meaningful softening in labour-demand intermediation, potentially reflecting firms’ caution in hiring which could yet feed slower growth moving forward. There were some positive points in the data: retail sales and IT were both positive, but the wider trend of a consumer-facing weakness continues with hospitality – particularly food and beverage services – still struggling to gain any traction in output growth and real estate activities still dragging. Production remains volatile. Production output slipped 0.1% m/m in January, continuing the choppy monthly pattern seen since autumn and reflecting a sector still in transition between one-off rebounds and underlying cyclicality. Beneath [Production output], this month’s outcome was shaped by a narrow manufacturing gain alongside broad drags from mining [and] quarrying and a small decline in electricity [and] gas. The 3M/3M print looks compelling at 1.5%, but [the 3M/3M print] is largely underpinned by a 17.5% 3M/3M rise in motor vehicle production that is better described as industry normalisation in response to a cyber incident than a growth narrative based on either demand or aggregate supply growth. What strength [the author] did see was offset by weakness across the machinery-electronics complex which suggests that the capital-goods cycle remains soft. Trade improves. The silver-lining in this morning’s data was a net improvement in the UK’s trade position. Goods exports rose +6.7% m/m, while imports –0.6%, reducing the monthly goods deficit and contributing to a £5.1bn narrowing in the total trade deficit to £1.8bn on a 3-month basis. [The net improvement in the UK’s trade position] was not merely arithmetic: the composition of flows was materially more constructive. Export strength was led by machinery [and] transport equipment, with gains spread across both EU and non-EU markets—reflecting higher shipments of office machinery, mechanical power generators, ships, and crude oil. The exception to [the net improvement in the UK’s trade position] remains trade with the US, where the deficit widened. Services exports remains a bright point in the national accounts with the surplus widening to £54.8bn on a three-month basis. A disappointing start to the year. The data this morning is a somewhat disappointing performance relative to expectations. Softness appears to be concentrated in the parts of the economy that matter for near-term momentum: consumer facing services are, weighed down by a concerning drop in employment-related services, while the headline stability in manufacturing appears to be a product of the accounting around previous output disruptions, and so should be considered normalisation rather than additive to potential supply. At the same time, headwinds are set to build over the quarter as energy prices pick up and the narrowness of growth drivers become exposed to external shocks. The risk now tilts clearly toward undershooting the 0.3% q/q growth [the author] had pencilled in for Q1, particularly if February and March fail to deliver a rebound in the labour-intensive parts of services. The one offset is the improvement in the trade balance, but [the net improvement in the UK’s trade position] alone is unlikely to counteract the domestic softness.
