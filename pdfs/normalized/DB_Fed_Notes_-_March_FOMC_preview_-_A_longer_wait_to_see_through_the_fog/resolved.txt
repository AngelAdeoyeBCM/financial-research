It is likely that the Committee will average the January and February reports and therefore state that “On average, job gains have remained low, and the unemployment rate has been stable.” Second, [the author expects] the Committee to acknowledge the latest geopolitical developments with similar language used at the onset of the Ukraine [or] Russia crisis. In turn, the Committee could add a line to the second paragraph stating “The implications of recent geopolitical developments for the US economy are highly uncertain. In the near term, these events are likely to create additional upward pressure on inflation and weigh on economic activity.” As Chair Powell is likely to expound upon in [Chair Powell's] post-meeting press conference, it is too early to assess how the Iran war may impact the overall outlook beyond a mechanical mark-to-market forecast adjustment to near-term headline inflation due to the recent rise in energy prices. Chair Powell will most likely emphasize that significant uncertainty remains about how recent events could impact the economy and monetary policy. [The author suspects] Powell will note that [the Fed] are watching events closely and that the primary transmissi on channel is through financial markets [or] conditions and especially oil prices. In terms of the implications for interest rates, Powell will note that policy remains “well positioned” to respond to the consequences of [the ongoing conflict in the Middle East]. Fundamentally, the latest oil price spike represents another adverse supply shock that would, at the mar gin, create further tensions between the Fed’s dual mandates, all else equal. While markets have interpreted [the ongoing conflict in the Middle East] as leaning hawkish for the Fed – an interpretation [the author agrees] with directionally – Powell is unlikely to give a strong signal abou t how near-term policy has been affected, if at all (see “Impact of oil price shocks a ‘frack’ -tion of what they once were”). As [the author] argued recently, the Fed’s response to geopolitically-driven oil price shocks has been context-dependent historically (see “What does history tell us about the Fed’s response to oil price shocks?”). At the moment, with inflation entering [inflation's] sixth year above target, [the author thinks] that reinforces the Fed’s “wait-and-see” approach. In the fog of current geopolitical tensions, it would be surprising for Powell to send a signal that deviated meaningfully fr om near-term market pricing. Given the potential for a few dissents, Powell will be asked about the level of consensus around the decision to keep rates steady. [Chair Powell] could indicate that “a significant majority” of officials supported [the decision to keep rates steady], as [Chair Powell] emphasizes the importance of a ro bust debate about policy, particularly around an uncertain outlook buffeted by several supply shocks. Powell could also be asked about indications from the January FOMC minutes that “several” officials “would have supported a two-sided description of the C ommittee’s future interest rate decisions, reflecting the possibility that upward adjustments to the target range for the federal funds rate could be appropriate if inflation remains at above-target levels.” It would be informative to get a sense of how th is count might have changed since the January meeting, but Powell will likely continue to avoid comments about rate hikes – here [Chair Powell] could repeat that [a rate hike] is not part of any official’s base case. How [the disagreement across the Committee that “several” officials “would have supported a two-sided description of the C ommittee’s future interest rate decisions] gets resolved will depend on how economic fundamentals evolve. Extreme volatility in the two jobs reports since the January meeting leave questions about risks to the labor market. [The author thinks] the truth is somewhere in the middle – smoothing through the last two jobs reports private and headline payrolls have averaged in the 30-50k range and ADP private sector employment has picked up to 60k, all near breakeven estimates. [The two jobs reports since the January meeting] are consistent with labor market slack broadly steady in recent months and could be viewed as consistent with some evidence of stability. However, officials will interpret [the two jobs reports since the January meeting] differently. Indeed, dovish leaning officials are likely to point to the latest data point as supporting a need to take out greater insurance against downside risks to the labor market. The (mild) economic drag from the oil price shock could add to worries about downside risks (see “US Economic Perspectives: How big of an oil shock can the Big Beautiful Bill handle? “). Powell and [Chair Powell's] colleagues are likely to feel less sanguine about inflation developments since [the January meeting], even as [Powell and his colleagues] maintain an expectation for disinflationary pressures to re-emerge. Core PCE inflation has printed back-to-back 0.4% monthly prints, lifting the year-over-year rate to 3.1% -- [Core PCE inflation's] highest since early 2024 – and [the author is] tracking a third consecutive 0.4% print for core PCE in February. The ongoing oil price shock will significantly lift headline inflation. The key will be how officials interpret any passthrough to core prices. No doubt Powell will aim to treat [the ongoing oil price shock] as another adverse supply shock that the Fed should look through if [the Fed] are able. Here [Chair Powell] will emphasize the need to keep inflation expectations stable. But it is also worth noting that Fed staff modeling leads to different conclusions about the extent of [the passthrough to core prices], with FRBUS suggesting an oil price shock can lower core inflation through the aggregate demand channel (see here), while other work focused on the 2022 energy price spike concludes it could lift core inflation for a sustained period (see here). With inflation still well above target, it makes sense for another supply shock to argue for some caution in easing policy imminently. Amongst other topics, [the author expects] Powell to be quizzed on the potential effects of AI on the economy – a topic that dominated market attention prior to the events in the Middle East. While [the author expects] [Chair Powell] to emphasize the uncertainty about [the potential effects of AI on the economy] at this point, [Chair Powell] could also echo some of the views from [Chair Powell's] colleagues in recent speeches, including: AI-driven investment should boost real GDP growth this year, the potential for AI to lift the neutral rate, possible downside risks to the labor market from AI ov er time, and the potential for AI to impact inflationary pressures further out. [Chair Powell] could also be asked about financial stability topics, including risks posed by sectors like private credit. [The author expects] Powell will indicate that officials are closely watching developments in that area but provide limited additional information beyond what was discussed in the Fed’s financial stability report last November. Finally, Powell is likely to be asked about issues related to Fed personnel, including Kevin Warsh, [Chair Powell's] potential replacement. Like [Chair Powell's] colleagues, Powell is likely to welcome Warsh’s nomination. However, [the author expects] Powell to add little on other related topics, including the ongoing DoJ investigation into [Chair Powell's] handling of the Fed building renovations, Lisa Cook’s outstanding case at the Supreme Court, and the potential that [Chair Powell] could decide to remain on the Board as a governor after [Chair Powell's] chair term ends in mid-May.
