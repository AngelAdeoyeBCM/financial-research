With long-end Treasury yields rising un-anchored in line with [the author's] view (see High Fives for 30y USTs, 12 May), the US Treasury intervened on Wednesday, by raising long-end Treasury buybacks. However, after a brief pullback, yields are back near [long-end Treasury yields'] recent highs. [That the US Treasury intervened on Wednesday, by raising long-end Treasury buybacks] is bond vigilantes vs. bond vigilant administration – part two. Part one was in 2025 (see Bond vigilant vs. bond vigilantes, 10 February 2025). Treasury secretary Scott Bessent stated in early 2025 that [the US Treasury] were “watching the 10y”, and had a large “toolkit” to address Treasury market issues. Through a combination of
1. biasing issuance in bills (see, T-bill and chill),
2. buybacks,
3. regulation changes, and most importantly,
4. controlled fiscal deficits (helped by tariffs), the bond vigilant administration prevailed over bond vigilantes in 2025. But the current setup in 2026 is different, and [the author thinks] bond vigilantes have the upper hand this time. Fed credibility is being questioned by the market, a wave of hyperscaler issuance is adding to duration supply concerns, amid strong growth, sticky infl ation, and worsening deficits – all driving yields higher. Additionally, [the author sees] buybacks only removing a small fraction of the net duration supply in Treasuries (see Figure 1). Raising buybacks may have felt necessary but was not going to be sufficient to quell the rise in yields. Fed credibility concerns are a key part of the price action in US yields, with 2y yields falling since the July FOMC, and 30y yields rising – a K shaped bond market (see Figure 2). Such markets are rare, with only six such instances since the GFC. The most comparable episode is Q3 2023, where 30y yields rose by nearly 100bp in Q3 20 23. [The author continues] to suggest positioning for higher long-term yields via 6m30y 1x1.5 payer spreads. Vis-à-vis credibility, investors will be closely watching the Jackson Hole appearance from Chair Warsh next Friday (10:00 EDT), though [the author] don’t expect much, given that the theme of this year’s Jackson Hole symposium is “Financial Innovation: Implications for Payments and Policy”. If Warsh does speak on monetary policy, [the author thinks] the language is likely to be similar to the June and July press conferences. For views on monetary policy, [the author thinks] that listening to the wider FOMC is more informative. The Fedspeak docket is sparse as of writing, but [the author expects] [the Fedspeak docket] to fill out next week. Another key data point will be core PCE (Wednesday 8:30 EDT), [the author's] estimate is on a knife’s edge at 0.246%, rounding to 0.2%. [The author thinks] that:
1. there could be upward revisions to June based on upwardly revised PPI services prices; and
2. July and August prints are unlikely to change the overall narrative of sticky, moderately above-target inflation. As [the author has] previously written about, the upcoming BEA revisions in September will likely lower PCE inflation on net. Regarding a key inflationary driver – the Middle East conflict – [the author] observe that while crude prices have strengthened, the primary pressures on the energy system reside in gasoil and non-US natural gas. With gasoil and jet cracks at historically elevated levels, it appears that demand destruction is being driven through product pricing rather than crude oil prices to balance the system amid supply constraints. [The author] don’t think the end of the rise is near, which is why [the author] bought the Oct26-Dec26 ICE gasoil crack spread. EU gas prices show similar strains, having risen above EUR65/MWh on Friday. [That EU gas prices show similar strains, having risen above EUR65/MWh on Friday] is mainly driven by a need for European operators to buy LNG to refill historically low storage levels before winter, while Middle Eastern LNG remains scuppered. [The author sees] furthe r upside in the next two months and see a relative easing after [the next two months], given warm, wet and windy weather forecasts and more US LNG coming to the market. Last week’s PMIs bolster confidence in eurozone momentum, with manufacturing surging – notably in Germany – driven by defence spending and data center buildouts. Additionally, the Indeed wage tracker rose to 3.3% in July, up 100bp since March. Expected inflation releases this week will likely signal rising pressures, particularly in Spain, where [the author forecasts] 4.7% y/y, up from 3.9% in July. Overall, [incoming growth and inflation data] reinforces [the author's] conviction in a September hike by the ECB.
