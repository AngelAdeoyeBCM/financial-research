# UK Weekly Digest - Market Expectations for the Autumn Budget

Economics UK Weekly Digest Date 23 October 2026

Chancellor Healey’s inaugural Budget is nearly upon [the author]. Expectations are low, with many – including [the author] – expecting a ‘do no harm’ Budget (see [the author's] preview here). What do market participants expect? In [the author's] weekly, [the author publishes] the results of [the author's] client survey (many thanks to those that participated).

1. How much headroom do you think the Chancellor will leave in 2029/30? The modal outcome for participants was for a fiscal headroom of between GBP 10bn and GBP 15bn. [The author's] survey, interestingly, showed an upside skew to fiscal headroom expectations – matching [the author's] marginal upside risk of just under GBP 16bn in headroom. The modal outcome was for a GBP 5bn to GBP 10bn increase, per annum, relative to the Spring Statement projections. 3. What amount of tax rises do you expect to be announced in the Budget? In terms of tax rises, the majority of respondents though t that the Chancellor would raise somewhere between GBP 5bn and 7.5bn in taxes at the Budget. 18% thought the Chancellor would raise somewhere between GBP 7.5bn and 10bn, and 8% saw the Chancellor raising more than GBP 10bn at the Autumn Budget. Figure 3: How much will tax rise in the Budget? Source: dbDIG Research
4. Do you expect any spending cuts beyond those already announced to be included in the Autumn Budget? The majority of respondents (55%) saw no additional spending cuts beyond those already announced coming as part of the 2026 Autumn Budget. 33% thought there would be some spending cuts and 12% of respondents were undecided. Figure 4: Any spending cuts expected in the Budget? Source: dbDIG Research

By how much do you expect the 2026/27 gilt remit to change relative to the current remit projection? 33% of respondents expect a gilt remit revision of GBP 5bn to GBP 10bn this fiscal year. 25% saw “up to GBP 5bn” in extra gilts issued in the current fiscal year. 10% saw a reduction in the gilt remit. And 8% expected more than GBP 10bn. Figure 5: Gilt remit expectations for 2026/27 Source: dbDIG Research
6. By the end of the week, what impact do you expect the Budget to have on 10-year gilt yields relative to pre-Budget? The modal outcome for market participants was a fall of up to 5bps in 10y gilt yields (30%), with 60% of [the author's] respondents thinking the Budget would result in some fall in gilt yields by the end of the week. Only 39% of respondents thought that the Budget would result in 10y gilt yields rising by the end of the week. Figure 6: Expected budget impact on 10y gilt yields Source: dbDIG Research

Figure 10: Key events in the week ahead Source: Deutsche Bank Research, Bloomberg Finance LP Credit: After a record-breaking August, we expect net consumer credit to cool a touch in September (August: GBP 2.5bn). [The author expects] net consumer credit to have slipped to GBP 2bn in September. Sales volumes fell by 0.4% m-o-m, and by more than expected. The GfK consumer confidence data also slipped by 10pts to -24 in September – marking the largest single month drop since the pandemic. [That the GfK consumer confidence data also slipped by 10pts to -24 in September], [the author thinks], should take some of the heat out of credit card borrowing. But don’t expect a collapse. Car dealership finance should keep “other” consumer credit ticking along. The September plate change was the strongest since 2017, with registrations up 12% y-o-y and private demand up close to 14%. Chunky manufacturer discounts, the Electric Car Grant and high pump prices pushed buyers towards new EVs. Fuel volumes also rose 2.2% m-o-m at elevated prices, which adds to card balances at the margin. Net consumer credit has beaten expectations in every month so far this year. And with real wages squeezed by the energy shock, the risk is that households lean on credit more. On mortgage approvals, [the author expects] another drop to 5 4k. Swap rates climbed through September as markets priced in a hike, and lenders kept repricing. The average two-year fix jumped by around 25bp over the month, and fixes priced below 5% have all but vanished – from nearly 1,500 at the start of September to just nine by early October. The surveys tell the same story. RICS new buyer enquiries fell back to -22, the first deterioration since March, and agreed sales slipped to -18. Rightmove reported sales agreed fell to 9% down on a year ago, with the stock of homes for sale at a 12-year high. Surveys: There will be a few key surveys to watch in the week ahead. The big one? The Bank’s DMP and Agents survey [The big one]. On [the Bank’s DMP], [the author will] be looking for the usual suspects: price expectations, wage expectations, and inflation expectations. On price expectations, [the author expects] the 3mma to edge higher to 3.8% with the single month stuck at 3.9%. On wage growth expectations, [the author expects] no change here at 3.4% (3mma). But [the author now sees] some upside risk building, especially given the publication of the ASHE data ([the author now thinks] the National L iving Wage growth could push to 4.6% in April 2027 – a big jump from the 3.7% estimate touted in April). Lastly, on CPI expectations, [the author sees] the 1y single month rise picking up to 3.4% (taking the 3mma to 3.3%). On the 3y ahead measure, [the author sees] [the 3y ahead measure] pulling lower to 2.8% on the single month estimate, keeping the 3mma at 2.8%. Lastly, keep an eye on the CBI and BRC Shop Price data. On [the CBI], [the author expects] some marginal improvement in October. And on [the BRC Shop Price data], [the author thinks] some further upside may be likely owing to firming energy prices. Budget: [The author expects] Chancellor Healey to opt for a relatively low-key fiscal event, with limited tax and spending measures and a focus on providing targeted, temporary support to households and businesses as higher energy prices and borrowing costs weigh on the econ omy. The Budget is likely to revolve around three themes: devolution, cost-of-living and business support, and investment, particularly through public sector corporations and the Government’s housing, defence and industrial strategies. While unlikely to be a market-moving Budget in [the 2026 Autumn Budget's] own right, [the 2026 Autumn Budget] remains an important waypoint for the public finances. [The author expects] fiscal headroom to narrow (to just under GBP 16bn), borrowing to remain on a downward path but prove stickier than previously forecast, and cash borrowing requirements to drift higher over the medium term.
