- Doing away with press conferences would create a vac-uum for other Fed speakers to fill After this week’s vote in the Senate Banking Committee, Kevin Warsh is all but assured of becoming the next Fed chair in time for the June FOMC meeting. In this note, [the author discusses] several potential policy implications of the handover from Powell to Warsh. The structure of the Federal Reserve System will constrain Warsh, particularly with respect to the near-term direction of the funds rate. Most immediately, rate cuts anytime soon will be an extremely tough sell to the FOMC, as underscored by the three recent dissents in favor of more hawkish forward guidance. Warsh may find more success in prioritizing the funds rate over the balance sheet as a monetary policy tool, though here [Warsh] may be pushing on an open door. [Warsh] may also get a recep-tive committee audience regarding [Warsh's] desire for a smaller balance sheet, though the important issues of how [a smaller Fed balance sheet] is done and over what timeline may be the bigger constraints. Reduc-ing banks’ demand for reserves could be a multiyear process. The committee will also likely entertain any ideas Warsh has for reforming Fed communications. But the dead end that Powell recently arrived at on [Fed communications] should warn against easy changes to committee-level changes. And unilateral changes by Warsh to diminish the post-meeting press confer-ence would likely only weaken the chair’s ability to shape the policy narrative. Whither the funds rate? The first question market participants ask of a new Fed chair is what their pre-appointment statements tell [the author] about their policy leanings. [Pre-appointment statements] isn’t always a perfect guide. In the first half of 2006, “Helicopter Ben” Bernanke led the Fed in a more hawkish direction than the market expected. The ques-tion becomes even trickier for Warsh. As a Fed governor, Warsh was quite hawkish after the GFC. More recently, [Warsh] has offered a more dovish view of monetary policy. Coinci-dentally or not, [a more dovish view of monetary policy] also aligns with the president’s views. So it may take a while to learn who the real Kevin Warsh is. If, as most seem to presume, [the author] start with the dovish Kevin Warsh, [the author thinks] [Warsh] will have a hard time selling the FOMC on that policy leaning. If Warsh is unable to persuade the FOMC to cut rates anytime soon, would he dissent against his own committee? [The author] don’t think so, as both contemporary observers and historians would likely see [dissent against his own committee] as a sign of a failed chairmanship. Simi-larly, while [Warsh] has advocated for more dissent, [the author suspects] that after [Warsh] becomes chair, Warsh will begin to see the case for collegiality. A better reception on the balance sheet In [Warsh's] testimony, Warsh indicated [Warsh] preferred to use interest rates over the balance sheet to steer the macroeconomy. Here [Warsh] will find little debate from the rest of the FOMC, though [Warsh] may want to hone [Warsh's] reasoning. Warsh said, “My view is the interest rate tool gets in the cracks. It’s fairer. The balance sheet tool disproportionately helps those with financial assets.” The “cracks” reference seemed to be a misquotation of former Governor Jeremy Stein, who said that rate policy gets into the cracks of the financial system in places that reg-ulatory tools miss. Moreover, most Fed officials see balance sheet policy as just interest rate policy by other means when the short rate is constrained by being close to zero. In recent years, Warsh has indicated [Warsh]’d also like to see a smaller balance sheet, saying [the Fed balance sheet] currently has too large an “imprimatur” on the economy. Although [a smaller Fed balance sheet] has been debated before, [the author thinks] many on the committee will wel-come giving the issue another look. The bigger challenge will be reducing banks’ demand for reserves such that reducing the size of the balance sheet does not lead to a disruptive shortage of reserves. The Fed has several options to reduce banks’ demand for reserves. See, for example, the compre-hensive list from Darrell Duffie. However, for almost all of [several options to reduce banks’ demand for reserves] there would likely need to be a period of study and debate that could last at least several months. As such, [the author] don’t see [a smaller Fed balance sheet] as much of an issue for 2026 or even 2027. Communication: easier said than done At several points in [Warsh's] testimony, Warsh raised various issues regarding Fed communication. A common theme was a desire for less forward guidance, in part because [forward guidance] may lock in poli-cymakers’ subsequent decisions. [The author believes] the FOMC will be receptive to revisiting how [the FOMC] communicate, though it’s not easy to see where [Fed communications] debate concludes. Just last year, in conjunction with [the FOMC's] framework review, the FOMC undertook a comprehensive review of [the FOMC's] communications. But as Chair Powell related this week, “we couldn’t come up with anything that had really broad support on the Committee, and so we just moved on.” It seems that any modification to the SEP or the dot plot would require a committee vote. [The author suspects] many on the FOMC are in favor of keeping the dot plot— despite mixed reviews from the public—because [the dot plot] allows [many on the FOMC] to have a say in the Fed’s overall suite of communica-tions. The modalities of the press conference, in contrast, are likely at the discretion of the chair. Given Warsh’s remarks that [Warsh] would like to see less forward guidance, there has been spec-ulation as to whether Warsh would cancel the press confer-ences, reduce [the press conferences'] frequency, or limit questions from the press. In the end, [the author suspects] Warsh will keep the press con-ferences as [the press conferences] are. The press conference allows the chair to be the “first out of the gate” to shape the narrative of where policy is heading. Warsh’s experience with press conferences will get an early test with the June FOMC meeting. Part of the reason the press conference has received so much attention is that all the Fed chairs in the press conference era (Bernanke, Yellen, and Powell) were believed to be faithfully conveying the sense of the committee. If Warsh is opposed to keeping policy unchanged next month, [that Warsh is opposed to keeping policy unchanged next month] could complicate what [Warsh] says. Any perception that [Warsh's] comments reflect some of [Warsh's] person-al bias could confuse markets and eventually diminish the power of the press conference.
