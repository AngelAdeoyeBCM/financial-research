Federal Reserve Monitor | North America

M Update 2 January FOMC: A dovish hold In justifying the decision to stay on hold in January, Chair Powell pointed to incoming economic data, which was stronger than expected, “some signs” of stabilization in the labor market, and continued confidence that tariff-induced inflation would be transitory. [Chair Powell] said that downside risk to employment had likely diminished — which was the original reasoning behind the risk-management rate cuts — but also said upside risk to inflation had declined. The broad consensus at [the January FOMC] was to remain on hold and most FOMC members see the current policy stance as appropriate to stabilize the labor market and guide inflation lower. [The author interprets] Powell’s comments as indicating the Fed still has an easing bias and, as a result, just executed a “dovish hold.” [Chair Powell] said that the current funds rate target was at the upper end of the plausible range of estimates for neutral and that the policy rate was appropriately set to help guide inflation lower. On net, [that the current funds rate target was at the upper end of the plausible range of estimates for neutral] suggests the Fed still sees the monetary policy stance as imparting some restriction or at the very least not boosting the economy. [Chair Powell] was noncommittal about whether further cuts would come because of labor market softness or inflation moving lower, but the Fed’s forecast and [the author's] interpretation of the press conference is that the Committee likely expects to reduce policy rates only after [the Committee] sees evidence that inflation is decelerating. Powell said [evidence that inflation is decelerating] would likely become clear in the second half of the year. [The author] expected greater unanimity on the outlook going into [the January FOMC], and [the author] got [greater unanimity on the outlook]. Powell said the outlook for the economy was stronger than when the Committee met in December. The FOMC did not bring new forecasts for the January meeting, but it sounded like the forecast had been upgraded. [Chair Powell] said the economy had “solid” momentum entering 2026 and pointed to resilient consumer spending, expanding business investment, expectations for a fiscal boost, favorable financial conditions, and ongoing AI-related capex spending. The only weak spot Powell identified was housing, where [Chair Powell] said activity was still weak. What would bring no rate cuts in 2026? Lower risks to both sides of the dual mandate suggest patience. When asked about the future path for the fed funds rate, [Chair Powell] provided the standard answer that decisions are “meeting by meeting”, but [Chair Powell] also hinted that the Fed is no rush: “Haven’t made any decisions about future meetings but the economy is growing at a solid pace, the unemployment rate is broadly stable and inflation remains somewhat elevated”. What would bring rate cuts? In [the author's] view, and assuming the outlook evolves as [the author] and the Fed expect, further rate cuts are likely inflation-based. Since evidence of disinflation is likely to show up later in the year, [the author retains] [the author's] outlook for rate cuts in June and September. What would bring no rate cuts in 2026? Little to no evidence of disinflation. [The author thinks] [little to no evidence of disinflation] could happen if the economy realizes [the author's] animal spirits upside scenario, where the fiscal stimulus is larger-than-expected and the policy backdrop produces a stronger — and early M Update Morgan Stanley Research 3 — acceleration in demand. [The author thinks] an early upswing in activity is needed since tariff pressures are likely to subside after 1Q 26 and strong demand side pressures would be needed to prevent inflation from decelerating. If that happens, and [the author] don’t see a material deceleration in monthly inflation prints, then [the author thinks] the Fed would remain on hold instead of normalizing [the Fed's] policy stance further. AI, productivity, and the labor market. On productivity, Powell has been generally optimistic during recent meetings about the productivity backdrop, and recent economic data are likely to reinforce that view. That said, [Chair Powell] implied that productivity was running above fundamentals and refrained from saying the economy was receiving a strong AI-related boost to activity at present. [Chair Powell] pointed to solid growth in activity alongside a sharp slowdown in hiring as the likely explanation. If the economy remains solid as the Fed appears to expect, Powell said [the economy] should bring some labor demand with [the economy]. [That the economy should bring some labor demand with the economy] would dampen productivity (versus [productivity's] recent readings). In [the author's] view, volatility in trade and inventory data likely play a role here as well. After surging in 1Q 25, imports have retraced sharply, leading to strong contributions from net trade to growth. [The author thinks] the give-back in imports will fade in the coming quarters, and [that the give-back in imports will fade in the coming quarters] may also soften productivity growth. Still, productivity growth is running 1.9% on a four quarter average, which isn’t a bad reading overall. Data quality? On data quality, judging from [Chair Powell's] more optimistic view of the labor market and inflation, it looks like Chair Powell and the rest of the Committee took signal from the recent, noisier data. In terms of data distortions, Powell mentioned that “we are getting to a place where they are not longer material, they are still there, but it is a tweak here and there.”
