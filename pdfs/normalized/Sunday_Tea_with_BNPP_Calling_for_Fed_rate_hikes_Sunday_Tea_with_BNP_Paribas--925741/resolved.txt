Since January, [the author has] projected rates on hold, given [the author's] view of robust US growth and sticky inflation. But last week’s solid payrolls release tilted the scales for [the author]. [The author now expects] the Fed’s next policy move to be a hike, aimed at starting to withdraw the monetary stimulus provided by 2025’s three “insurance” cuts. This year’s strong growth, gradually tightening labor market and persistently high inflation strike [the author] as markedly different from what officials expected at the time of last fall’s easing, and [the author expects] monetary policy to adjust accordingly. [The author thinks] [2025’s three “insurance” cuts] were modeled on similar moves in 1998 after the collapse of Long-Term Capital Management, and with the US economy now similarly proving stronger than anticipated, the Fed will probably follow [the Fed's] 1999 playbook to withdraw th em: three successive rate hikes. [The author sees] [three successive rate hikes] most likely beginning at the December meeting (i.e. after the midterm elections), although an earlier start is possible depending on the course of the Middle East conflict and the labor market’s trajectory. [The author believes] the main argument for eventual tightening will be an assessment that the Fed’s policy stance is miscalibrated. [The Fed’s policy stance] was set last fall against expectations for a cyclically weakening US economy and for inflation that would cool to near 2% thi s year. With inflation now closer to 4%, policy i s much more stimulative in real terms than was expected (Figure 1), and with the economy demonstrating remarkable stability, the need for cyclical stimulus seems much smaller than policymakers had expecte d. Fed officials also seem increasingly concerned that the neutral rate is higher than [Fed officials] believed last fall, meaning policy may have been m ore stimulative than was intended from the start.
