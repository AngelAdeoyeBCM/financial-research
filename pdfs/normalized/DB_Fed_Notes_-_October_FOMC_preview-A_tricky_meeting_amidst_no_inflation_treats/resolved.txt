Economics Fed Notes Date 22 October 2026

- [The author expects] the Fed to hold rates steady at the October FOMC meeting. Chair Warsh’s press conference will be focused on several topics: Why the Committee decided to not raise rates? How has recent inflation data impacted the monetary policy outlook? What is the Committee’s assessment of financial conditions after recent AI-related wobbles?
- Warsh is likely to signal that recent inflation data reinforce expectations from the September meeting that further tightening will likely be needed over time. However, skipping October allows officials more time to assess trends in the data. While a sharp tightening of financial conditions could lead to more dovish outcomes, absent [a sharp tightening of financial conditions], risks are skewed towards more tightening. The most salient watchpoints for [more tightening] are whether rental price disinflation reverses and the labor market returns as a source of inflationary pressures. However, [the author doubts] Warsh will want to add language that could be misinterpreted as a form of forward guidance or a backtracking on the Fed’s commitment to achieve price stability in a timely manner. The easiest course of action is to remove [the second to last sentence] and add [the second to last sentence] back when (if) [the Committee] hike again at the December meeting. Matthew Luzzetti, Ph.D. Press conference Assuming the Fed does not raise rates, the key focus of [Chair Warsh’s press conference] will likely be on why [the Fed] chose to hold. In [the author's] view, the September FOMC meeting set up a strong baseline for skipping October and raising rates again in December. The dot plot showed 16 out of 18 officials expecting at least one more hike this year, but only four anticipating 50bps more in 2026. Communications from several key officials, including Vice Chairs Williams and Jefferson, indicated that there was limited urgency in tightening further, even if more hikes were likely. At [Chair Warsh's] recent IMF appearance, which occurred after the CPI report, Warsh indicated that the economy has evolved broadly in line with [the Fed's] expectations from September, even though the latest inflation data was described by several officials as disappointin g. Consistent with [that the economy has evolved broadly in line with the Fed's expectations from September], Warsh could indicate that [the Fed] received limited new data since the last meeting and believed [the Fed] could benefit with a little more time to assess underlying trends. There are other factors that could support hold ing rates steady at [the October FOMC meeting]. Financial conditions have started to tighten, though [Financial conditions] remain accommodative on many metrics. Officials might want to see how much FCI tightening occurs for reasons outside of monetary policy before taking further action. One could also argue that if tightening is needed to catch up to a rising neutral rate, it makes sense to take a gradual approach in [tightening] given that r-star is uncertain. Finally, while not a primary consideration, [the author] could imagine that all else equal the Fed might prefer to stay out of the headlines for raising rates just ahead of a midterm election where affordability is the principal concern. All that said, [the author sees] strong fundamental reasons that the Fed should continue to raise rates. Growth momentum remains solid, the labor market is stable with risks of re-tightening, inflation pressures remain broad and elevated, and financial conditions are still not meaningfully restrictive, indeed [Financial conditions] remain accommodative on many metrics. Given [the backdrop that Growth momentum remains solid, the labor market is stable with risks of re-tightening, inflation pressures remain broad and elevated, and financial conditions are still not meaningfully restrictive], the case for skipping October is not terribly compelling. In the wake of the July press conference that triggered concerns about the Fed’s commitment to price stability, Warsh will want to avoid any indications that the Fed is unwilling to take appropriate action against sticky inflation. In terms of framing the tightening cycle, it will be important to see if Warsh continues to lean on the idea of “removing a dose of accommodation.” Indeed, with some tightening in conditions since September, it will be critical to see if [Chair Warsh] maintains the view that broad financial conditions are still accommodative. As [the author] argued after the September meeting, [the idea of “removing a dose of accommodation.”] was the most hawkish framing for the tightening cycle because [the idea of “removing a dose of accommodation.”] did not indicate a natural stopping point for the fed funds rate. The minutes to [the September FOMC meeting] indicated that other officials referenced differen t justifications, including risk management considerations, which have more limited implications for the total number of hikes. Nonetheless, Warsh chose not to highlight [differen t justifications, including risk management considerations] at the last press conference. On recent developments in the data, Warsh is likely to indicate that trends are not consistent with meaningful progress on disinflation. While [Chair Warsh] is not data point - dependent, the latest data must be discouraging, with a broad-based beat for CPI and core PCE set to tick up to 3.1-3.2% year over year in September. The Q3 GDP report should show continued solid growth momentum, and the latest labor market data remain consistent with stability, allowing the Fed to focus on inflation. Warsh will repeat the Fed’s commitment to achieving price stability in a timely fashion. On AI, Warsh is likely to repeat that [AI] is the most transformative technological advancement [Chair Warsh] has seen. While [Chair Warsh] believes there could be positive supply side development that may help to tame inflation at some point in the future, [Chair Warsh] is also likely to ote that in the near term [AI] is supporting demand and putting upward pressure on inflation. [Chair Warsh] could note that [the Fed] are, of course, watching issues related to AI valuations and financial stability risks; however, [the author thinks] [Chair Warsh] will avoid any language that would increase the focus on [issues related to AI valuations and financial stability risks]. Warsh is likely to offer boilerplate language to the effect that the Fed is always monitoring financial conditions and that there has been some tightening in recent months. Warsh will likely want to avoid the type of phrasing [Chair Warsh] used in the July press conference that led to market participants questioning the Fed’s willingness to act. In short, [Chair Warsh] will likely make clear that tightening FCIs are not a substitute for policy rate increases. Lastly, Warsh could provide a brief progress report on the various task forces. Recall that [Chair Warsh] announced last week at [Chair Warsh's] IMF appearance that “Among the external task forces that I initiated about four or five months ago is one on productivity and jobs. We are going to have our first field hearing of a task force on 16 November in the Bay Area, where we are going to wrestle with these issues.” Though [the author doubts] Warsh will provide an in-depth detail ed preview of the conclusions of any of the task forces, [Chair Warsh] may flag other task force meeting dates or add some broad color around the progression of the discussions.
