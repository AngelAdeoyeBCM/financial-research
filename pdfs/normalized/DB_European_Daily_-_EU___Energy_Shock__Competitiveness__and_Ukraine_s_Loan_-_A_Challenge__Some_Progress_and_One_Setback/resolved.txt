# European Daily: EU – Energy Shock, Competitiveness, and Ukraine’s Loan: A Challenge, Some Progress and One Setback

22 March 2026 | 11:11PM GMT At last week’s (19 March) meeting, the EU Council agreed to enhance the policy response to the energy crisis by advancing a set of initiatives with two main policy changes at the EU level. The ETS system is likely to be diluted by increasing the net supply of permits through a revision of Market Stability Reserves and an increase in free allowances. Moreover, member states have been guided to adopt “targeted, temporary and tailored” fiscal measures. But [the policy response to the energy crisis] has not been the first response as a few member states have started implementing broader fiscal measures reducing taxes on petrol and diesel, natural gas and electricity. The EU has also made some progress to address [the EU's] competitiveness gap. First, the timeline of structural reforms to enhance the single market and reduce administrative costs for the business sector has been agreed with the fail-safe instrument of adoption through regulation, not requiring national parliaments’ approvals, in contrast to directives. Moreover, the introduction of the 28th regime (also known as EU Inc.) was agreed for the end of the year and [the 28th regime's] eventual adoption is very likely thanks to enhanced cooperation—i.e. a subgroup of at least 9 member states can adopt [the 28th regime] if no universal agreement can be reached. [Structural reforms to enhance the single market and reduce administrative costs for the business sector], if implemented as planned, could significantly enhance the impact of the approximately EUR 400bn Competitiveness Fund proposed for the 2028-34 EU budget. The EU Council failed to overcome the Hungarian veto and the EUR 90bn loan to Ukraine is delayed further. While a political agreement was reached at the EU Council last December, Hungary later shifted [Hungary's] position and blocked the amendment to the EU Budget needed to issue EU debt and fund [the EUR 90bn loan to Ukraine]. [The author does] not expect [the Hungarian veto] to be resolved before Hungarian parliamentary elections in the middle of April. EU Response to the Energy Shock Between Structural and “Targeted, Temporary, Tailored” The EU Council agreed to set a two-tier strategy to address the elevated energy costs in Europe. The first tier provides long term targets and focuses on streamlining permitting processes to improve the structural bottlenecks in the European energy market: European Grids Package was presented in late 2025 by the European Commission 1. as a set of initiatives prioritizing support to the build-up of major grid projects of European relevance (European Energy Highways) and shift grid connection models to a “first ready, first served” basis to accelerate industrial electrification. Co-legislators are set to finalize [the European Grids Package] in 2026; Industrial Accelerator Act is legislation that was proposed at the beginning of 2. March aiming to tackle permitting bottlenecks for industrial energy access. [The Industrial Accelerator Act] seeks to create “Industrial Acceleration Areas” where clean tech and energy-intensive industries (such as steel and chemicals) can benefit from fast-tracked digital permitting and prioritized access to low-carbon power sources, including renewables and nuclear. The EU Parliament is scheduled to sign off by end of 2026. The second tier, likely more market relevant in the short term, focuses on the policy response to the surge in energy prices. In the press conference following the Council, President von der Leyen stressed that the cost of energy varies between countries and [that the cost of energy varies between countries] has been exacerbated by the recent geopolitical crisis (Exhibit 1). 1. the reliance on natural gas for power generation,
2. the cost of carbon emission permits (EU allowances (EUAs)) and
3. the tax wedge, primarily a responsibility of member states (Exhibit 2). Following the letter that the EU Commission sent to the EU Council ahead of the meeting, the EU Council supported the effort of the Commission to assess options for selectively decoupling electricity prices from natural gas to mitigate high energy costs. [Selectively decoupling electricity prices from natural gas] would be in line with the 2022-23 policy precedent in Iberia – Iberian exception – and this time would be enhanced by the expanded use of long-term Power Purchase Agreements (PPAs) and two-way Contracts for Difference (CfDs) in line with recommendations of the Draghi’s report. The Commission is also exploring options for “subsidizing or capping” gas prices used for power generation to prevent internal market distortions during periods of high volatility. Moreover, the EU Council asked the Commission to present a review of the European ETS by “July at the latest” to reduce the volatility of the carbon price and mitigate [the carbon price's] impact on electricity prices, which currently averages at 11%. Ten Member States, including Italy, Hungary and Greece, moved further calling on the EU to “smooth the phase-out of free allowances (EUAs) from 2028” and extend free carbon permits “beyond 2034”. [The author] pointed out that increasing free allowances is unlikely to reduce the price of EUAs and lower power prices, and [the author sees] that the Commission is likely to increase “flexibility” in the Market Stability Reserve (MSR). [The decision that the Commission is likely to increase “flexibility” in the Market Stability Reserve (MSR)], by increasing net supply of carbon permits (EUAs) in the short term, would be more effective in reducing the carbon price while preserving the price signal for decarbonization (Exhibit 3, left). The Council supported a rebalancing of taxation to offset some of the impact of the energy crisis but called on the Commission to coordinate a fiscal response that needs to be “targeted, temporary, tailored” to the specifics of single member states. As the duration of the conflict extended, national fiscal responses are starting to build, with France, Italy and Spain, first among major European countries, implementing temporary excise duty and VAT reduction on gasoline, power and natural gas. The policies implemented so far are broad in scope and do not align well with the targeted approach advocated by both the EU Commission and the ECB. Although [the author expects] a smaller response than in the 2022 energy crisis, [the author thinks] [national fiscal responses] signal an increasing appetite for broad fiscal support that may conflict with the guidelines agreed in the Council and potentially contribute to a more persistent inflation profile (Exhibit 3, right). Competitiveness With a Clearer Timeline: Progress More Likely Another key item on the Council’s agenda was reaching an agreement on the timeline to address the European competitiveness gap. While the EU has put forward a number of initiatives enclosed in the Competitiveness Compass, [the EU] has failed to secure a list of priorities. The Council addressed [the issue that the EU has failed to secure a list of priorities] by setting a joint roadmap to enhance the Single Market with milestones and targets until March 2027 through coordination among the Commission, Parliament, and Council. Three key initiatives were set up: Regulatory “Deep House Cleaning” and simplification through a systematic 1. review of the EU Commission to eliminate redundant regulations, with the introduction of “sunset clauses” for new laws and a “one-in, one-out” principle to prevent the accumulation of regulatory burdens. Omnibus packages will be the preferred vehicle to advance key reforms for the 2. business sector with the fail-safe instrument of adoption through regulations, that do not require national parliaments’ approval, in contrast to directives. (28th Regime) will allow a new, optional European legal form for companies, 3. allowing for 48-hour digital registration and a single set of corporate rules across all 27 Member States. [The 28th regime] is designed to facilitate innovative firms and startups to scale. While EU Inc. is certainly an improvement on the cost of setting up a business in the EU, [the 28th regime] would not address the fragmentation of national legal systems as firms operating in different member states will still need to navigate different tax, commercial and administrative regulations. The 28th regime is set to be finalized by the end of 2026. The Council also agreed to set the Savings and Investment Union (SIU) to complete [the Savings and Investment Union (SIU)'s] first main achievement with the finalisation of “Phase One” by June 2026, focusing on integrated market supervision, securitization, and harmonized insolvency rules. To prevent delays, and in a key change from the past, the Commission agreed with the Council to use “enhanced cooperation” (i.e. allowing a group of at least nine states to move forward) if a full consensus of 27 states cannot be reached on the key initiatives to foster competitiveness. [The joint roadmap to enhance the Single Market], if delivered as planned, will set a new landscape for the European Competitiveness Fund, a newly proposed central pillar for the 2028–2034 Multiannual Financial Framework (MFF) - the EU budget - currently under negotiation. [The European Competitiveness Fund] provides approximately EUR 400bn in support of strategic investments in biotech, AI, and clean energy that could benefit more effectively from an enhanced single EU market. Financial Support for Ukraine Shifts by Two Months After updates from the President of Ukraine to EU leaders, the Council reiterated [the EU Council's] support for Ukraine but the document was endorsed only by 25 out of 27 member states. Moreover, the EU Council failed to reach an agreement on the 20th package of sanctions against Russia and to overcome the veto put in place by the Hungarian government on the EUR 90bn loan to Ukraine. [The EUR 90bn loan to Ukraine], combined with other macroeconomic assistance programmes, would be enough to cover two years of military and financial assistance from the EU to Ukraine based on the last 4 years. The EU estimates that an additional EUR 30bn would be required to close the funding gap faced by Ukraine over 2026 and 2027 (Exhibit 4). While a political agreement on the loan to Ukraine was reached at the EU Council last December, Hungary later shifted [Hungary's] position and blocked the amendment to the EU Budget necessary to issue EU debt and fund [the EUR 90bn loan to Ukraine]. [The author does] not expect [the Hungarian veto] to be lifted before parliamentary elections are held in Hungary and [the author anticipates] that the issuance of EU debt related to [the EUR 90bn loan to Ukraine] will shift to late April at the earliest.
