# UK Economics The fiscal implications of energy prices – Update

## CITI’S TAKE

A notable increase in energy prices over the weekend has increased the cost of potential policy intervention for the UK government. [The author now sees] the price cap for Q3 2026 at £1,985, with the cap only returning to [the price cap's] Q2 2026 level by Q2 2028. A policy targeted at holding the price cap at [the price cap's] current level would cost £5.9bn to £6.4bn per year, ceteris paribus. Given the moves in energy prices over the weekend, [the author] revisit [the author's] note last week on the fiscal implications of the ongoing energy crisis (see – The fiscal implications of energy prices). In [the author's note last week on the fiscal implications of the ongoing energy crisis] [the author] suggested that there would likely be political pressure for the government to intervene to shield household bills from the spike in energy prices, although there was no urgent pressure to do so. [The author's] view is unchanged: [The author thinks] that the government has options and pressure to act is growing, but, importantly, [the government] have time to make a decision and wait and see if energy prices normalise before there is a need to intervene. However, the rally over the weekend has meaningfully changed the fiscal maths. [The author now sees] the Q3 energy price cap at £1,985 (based on pricing at 12.30pm, 9 March), up from £1,883 when [the author] ran the model on Friday afternoon (6 March). [The Q3 energy price cap at £1,985] is a 20% increase on the current cap. Further, last week [the author] noted that the price cap would return to [the price cap's] Q2 2026 level in Q1 2028, which [the author now sees] as happening in Q2 2028. The fallout is a notable increase in the cost of intervention. Holding the energy price cap steady at Q2 2026 would cost something in the range of £8.8bn to £9.5bn (total, out to Q2 2028), if applied uniformly across households, with an annualised cost of £5.9bn to £6.4bn ([the author's] estimate before the spike was a cost of £3.2bn to £3.5bn). Ofgem price cap forecast, £ Source: Citi Research, Bloomberg There has been some tacit acknowledge that there may be a need to intervene, with the Prime Minister stating a need to manage economic fallout (Bloomberg, 9 March). No further detail has been offered or suggested, however. [The author thinks] the most credible path for intervention would be directly via the policy costs embedded in the household energy bills (much like the Chancellor announced at the Autumn budget). [The most credible path for intervention] carries [the most credible path for intervention's] own problems (which [the author] outline here: and Rates – 2025 Budget Review: On Fiscal Credibility) but may be the easiest to implement. Such a policy could then be targeted both in terms of size of relief and scope of application to mitigate costs as necessary and have the benefit of mechanically reducing CPI as well as supporting households. However, an intervention would raise fiscal questions. [The author thinks] the OBR’s forecast last week—and by extension the framework in which the Treasury makes economic decisions—overestimated the headroom against the fiscal rules given the condition assumptions used. Headroom, while still robust relative to recent levels, is likely lower than the OBR implies. [That Headroom, while still robust relative to recent levels, is likely lower than the OBR implies] generates a risk that the next forecast, due for the Autumn budget, revises down the headroom—a view supported by recent moves in OIS and inflation pricing, both of which add current expenditure and borrowing costs to the treasury balance sheet. That said, the government has space to act. Not only can [the government] eat into the headroom without breaching the rules, but the front-loaded profile of the would-be costs is favourable, in [the author's] view, with the majority falling in Q3 and Q4 2026 and then tapering off through 2027. The current budget balance rule, by contrast, only applies to FY 2029/30, and so such an intervention may not even erode headroom.
