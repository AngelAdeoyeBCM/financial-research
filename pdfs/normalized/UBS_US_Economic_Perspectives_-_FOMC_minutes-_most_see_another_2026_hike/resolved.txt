# US Economic Perspectives FOMC minutes: "most" see another 2026 hike

While the minutes of the September FOMC meeting generally reflected the hawkish shift underway among FOMC participants, the acknowledgement that "most" view another increase in the target range for the federal funds rate as appropriate by year end was perhaps fewer than the "almost all" [the author] had expected. [The acknowledgement that "most" view another increase in the target range for the federal funds rate as appropriate by year end] is still consistent with a majority of participants seeing further tightening in policy this year, but is in line with the recent patient commentary from FOMC participants, suggesting a further hike in December rather than October. Market pricing for October was little changed with 4 bp priced for the meeting now versus 5 bp at yesterday's close. Rationale for hiking differs but general signal of too high inflation In the discussion surrounding the decision to raise rates by 25 bp at the September meeting "all participants" supported the hike, with participants emphasizing "inflation remained elevated while the labor market appeared to be near full employment, with some signs of strengthening, and that economic activity was expanding at a solid pace." Participants also saw the move higher in September as ensuring the "timelier return" of inflation to the Committee's 2 percent goal. However, the minutes revealed slightly different rationales across the Committee on why a higher path for the target range would be required: "many" saw [a higher path for the target range] as prudent risk-management; "a number" saw [a higher path for the target range] as necessary given their modal outlooks rather than risk-management; "some" saw [a higher path for the target range] as diminishing the risk of inflation persistence and de-anchored inflation expectations; and a "couple" thought [a higher path for the target range] would prevent "sector-specific price increases stemming from energy market disruptions and AI-related demand from broadening out". Financial conditions "supportive" The minutes stated that " several" participants viewed the current policy rate as "not restrictive or only mildly restrictive." [That "a couple" of participants raised their appropriate setting for the federal funds rate given upward revisions to their estimates of the neutral federal funds rate] suggests there's a cohort that believes it's not just financial conditions, but the monetary policy setting, that's not restrictive. As [the author] noted in [the author's] post- FOMC reaction, the median FOMC member leaving the nominal policy rate at 3.6% at the end of [the FOMC's] forecast horizon in the September SEP, while inflation returns to 2.0%, indicates policymakers believe a funds rate over 3.5% would be needed to ensure that outcome. In other words, the FOMC is acting like restrictive policy in the near term requires more hikes than the median 3.2% longer-run dot would imply. [The FOMC] have generally rethought the fundamental level of the real funds rate needed to achieve price stability over the next three to four years. Too high inflation and broadening drivers The characterization of inflation dynamics was largely in line with what [the author already knows] and expected: the Committee were concerned about the lack of inflation progress and concerns regarding inflation's persistence had increased. At the July meeting, policymakers largely expected inflation to improve, with " most participants " anticipating that inflation would "step down over the rest of the year" as the effects of tariffs and energy-price increases faded away. By the September meeting, however, participants explicitly stated that they had " not seen sufficient progress on lowering inflation in recent months " and some participants noted that underlying inflation appeared to have " moved sideways." Concern about inflation persistence also intensified by the September meeting, with some participants judging that inflation risks had become "more skewed to the upside " and warning that, after over five years of above-target inflation, elevated inflation could begin to influence expectations and price-setting behavior. The Committee's views on AI also seemingly shifted. In July, "several" participants assessed that the inflation impact of the AI buildout was more limited, affecting only a few categories of consumer prices. By September, "some" participants commented that the AI buildout could cause aggregate demand to outpace aggregate supply, putting upward pressure on inflation. Economy "solid" The description of the economy was also in line with the post-meeting communications, as [the author would] expected, activity is expanding at a " solid pace," business investment is "robust" and consumer spending " resilient." The minutes noted that " several" participants thought that underlying momentum in the economy has increased. "Several" participants had noted that "stock market gains had provided support to consumer spending, particularly among higher-income households " but " low- and moderate-income households faced strains, with higher energy prices weighing disproportionately on their real disposable income."
