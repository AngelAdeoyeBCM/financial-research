[The author's] framework for rate hikes this cycle is informed by the Fed’s late 1990s playbook. In 1998, [the Fed] cut rates three times to pr event downside risks stemming from the collapse of LTCM. With the economy in good shape, and financial conditions loosening with the euphoria over the internet, the Fed took back all three cuts in 1999. 2026 reminds [the author] a lot of 1999 and 2025 reminds [the author] a lot of 1998. Consequently, when the Fed begins hiking, [the author thinks] [the Fed] will do three, likely consecutively. It is also informative how term premiums reacted in the late 1990s. T erm premiums began rising from the second cut in 1998. Even when the Fed hiked rates in 1999 to reverse the insurance cuts, term premiums kept rising through the first three hikes. Put differently, markets didn’t think upside inflation risks had receded just because the Fed began hiking. It wasn’t until the fourth hike that term premiums stabilized, and the fifth hike for [term premiums] to fall. The July FOMC price action suggests rate hikes may be needed to be priced into the market to stabilize or lower term premiums again. The 1999 experience tells [the author] that it may take more than just liftoff to regain inflation credibility. Combing these facts, [the author thinks] the market will need to see at least three hikes priced in if [the author is] to see a stabilization of term premiums and re-pricing of credibility in the long-end of the Treasury curve. In the FX space, the key question is whether recent intervention will change the trend in JPY. [That recent intervention will change the trend in JPY] is not [the author's] view. [The author sees] recent intervention as somewhat of a midway point between a unilateral and a multilateral intervention. In previous multilateral interventions, participating central banks issued press statements on the exact intervention dates to telegraph [participating central banks'] joint support. This time around, the ECB did not do so, and media reports suggest [the ECB] was only alerted to [the recent JPY intervention] after [the recent JPY intervention] happened. Without overt ECB support, [the author thinks] any Treasury interventions are likely to be limited. The Exchange Stabilization Fund (ESF) that the Treasury would draw from to intervene has a limited amount of EUR. And creative ways to get more EUR – including interve ning in the forward market or swapping SDRs for EUR with the NY Fed – would likely come up against roadblocks. Meanwhile, [the author remains] skeptical that the US Treasury would want to outright intervene in the market by selling USDJPY. The fact that [the US Treasury] did not do so in [the recent JPY intervention] is telling, in [the author's] view. The reason is simple: if domestic officials actively engage in policies to weaken the value of the reserve currency of the world, [that domestic officials actively engage in policies to weaken the value of the reserve currency of the world] could have a difficult-to-model and non-linear impact on the market. Such a move could trigger a re-evaluation of the USD’s reserve status, potentially resulting in large outflows from the US Treasury market and push up back-end US yields significantly: exactly what the Treasury is trying to avoid at present, in [the author's] view. Therefore, like prior episodes of JPY interventions, [the author does] not view [the recent JPY intervention] as a sustainable strategy to combat JPY weakness. Rather, [the author interprets] [the recent JPY intervention] as an effort to buy time for fundamentals to shift in favor of a stronger JPY. Slowing the weakening trend in the JPY will require a shrinking of the US D-JPY interest rate differential.
