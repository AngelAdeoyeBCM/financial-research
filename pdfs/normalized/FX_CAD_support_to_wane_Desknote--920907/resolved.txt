[The author] would identify energy industry capex as one such cause – exporter economies should be able to benefit from surging energy prices if capex has improved for related sectors. The mechanism through which [the author thinks] [that exporter economies should be able to benefit from surging energy prices if capex has improved for related sectors] works is that returns from higher commodity prices are recycled into future capex, thus creating a positive growth cycle. [That returns from higher commodity prices are recycled into future capex, thus creating a positive growth cycle] was the case in 2010-2015, when increasing capex across the G10 commodity bloc led to terms of trade being a strong driver of FX. In contrast, as capex declined over 2016-2020, the link between FX and terms of trade weakened. In other words, energy exporters’ currencies could not benefit to the same extent from higher commodity prices because the income was not being put to work domestically. However, capex is now starting to rise again. [That capex is now starting to rise again] could help explain the CAD’s current improved correlation with terms of trade relative to prior supply shocks. That said, the same capex trend has occurred in both Australia and Norway as well. It appears inconsistent that the terms of trade correlation has been stronger for the CAD this time while weaker for the AUD and NOK. Employment trends also a consideration: One pass-through of higher commodity prices could be via hiring in related sectors. Labor growth alongside rising energy prices should suggest some positive pass-through to households and consumption for energy exporters. For Canada and Australia, [the author] compare job changes in the oil and gas industry to total employment. For higher commodity prices to support energy exporters’ economies, [the oil and gas industry] should post stronger job growth. [The author] would caveat that some related industries tend to benefit from indirect effects and hiring might appear with a lag, but as a starting point, labor trends in Canada’s oil and gas industry are broadly comparable to the national total. In contrast, [the oil and gas industry] has seen above-trend job creation in Australia. Thus, there again appears to be a disconnect insofar as the current terms of trade shock has been more positive for Canada than for other DM energy exporters. Geopolitics creates fatter tails for the path of monetary policy, but starting points matter. In the US, inflation has been above target for over five years, which has begun to prompt some concern from policy makers. In contrast, Canada has seen inflation recently within the target range. When considering the labor markets, only the Fed has a dual mandate but the US unemployment rate is fairly stable. In contrast, Canadian job data remains soft, as observed for instance in the February labor force survey, which makes tightening monetary policy difficult. As a result, it is difficult to see the BoC “out hawk” the Fed. [The author considers] two cases going forward. If central banks are required to turn more hawkish, there is more space for Fed cuts to be removed from here but likely little more that can be added to hikes for the BoC. Conversely, if there is a material hit to growth where the Fed is required to cut interest rates, there are likely negative spillovers into Canada that can see [the 33bp of hikes for the BoC in 2026] removed from the curve. Both cases move rate differentials against the CAD and given that USDCAD is the pair most sensitive to rates differentials [that Both cases move rate differentials against the CAD] creates an asymmetry.
