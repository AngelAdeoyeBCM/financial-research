# UK Economics The new Productivity Puzzle and the AI structural shift

## CITI’S TAKE

Since the GFC, UK productivity growth has been poor. Various theories have been put forward to explain [the UK Productivity Puzzle], but none have fully described the problem. It now appears [the author has] the reverse. By some metrics, UK productivity growth is finally on the rise. Building on [the author's] productivity analysis from earlier this year, [the author shows] evidence [the productivity boost] is primarily driven by ICT, although other sectors that benefit from AI advancements also play a role. [The author thinks] [the productivity boost] is both positive and meaningful, although [the author remains] cautious. The UK is experiencing a new productivity puzzle — After 15 years of sluggish productivity growth (let’s call [the 15 years of sluggish productivity growth] the “OG” productivity puzzle), the opposite is finally happening, although the causes of [the productivity boost] are not yet fully clear. [The author thinks] [the productivity boost] is a meaningful shift, but [the productivity boost] is sensitive to the data used — [The productivity boost] is best seen when using HMRC’s RTI (PAYE) data. Annual productivity growth averaged 1.2% per quarter since 2024Q3. The white-collar sector is leading the way, and ICT is the flag bearer — Since 2025, ICT contributed over a third of the annual productivity (PAYE) growth, despite comprising less than 10% of the entire economy. [The author thinks] the productivity boost is correlated with ICT’s pervasive use of AI — Both professional, scientific [and] technical sector, and information [and] communication sectors use AI like no other sectors. Nearly half of firms in the information [and] communication sector report that three-quarters of their staff use AI on a daily basis. While not causation, [ICT’s pervasive use of AI] is highly correlated with increased productivity in that sector. The outcome on inflation is unclear. On the one hand, higher productivity growth would push down on inflation. On the other, labour-market displacement from higher sectoral unemployment would push inflation higher. n | Since the Global Financial Crisis, the UK has been marred by poor productivity growth. Competing theories from academics and policymakers have been put forth to ascribe the causes of the UK Productivity Puzzle (e.g. See Barnett et al, 2014, or Van Reenen and Yang, 2024), but none have fully dominated the narrative. Now, the reverse appears to be happening. By some measures, UK productivity growth is finally picking up, and in a meaningful way too. Building on [the author's] previous productivity analysis from earlier this year, [the author shows] further evidence that, whilst [the productivity boost] is broad-based, [the productivity boost] is primarily driven by the “white collar” sector (excluding finance). [The “white collar” sector] has the clearest productivity enhancing benefits from AI advancements. [The productivity boost] is most obvious in the ICT sector, where cumulative productivity growth since 2022 is 32% (using HMRC’s PAYE measure). However, [the finding that the productivity boost is primarily driven by the “white collar” sector (excluding finance)] is sensitive to the data used and the comparison points. Whilst—by some measures—the pick-up in sectoral productivity has been broad, it really depends on the vantage-point. Namely, first, the time-period being examined and, second, the variables used to calculate measured productivity[, it really depends on the vantage-point]. Productivity has grown inconsistently, so different starting points can yield different outcomes. Nevertheless, using the PAYE productivity measure, ICT is the one sector that has consistently delivered strong annual growth every quarter over the last three years, no matter how you slice the data. On average, YoY productivity growth of that sector has been greater than 7% per quarter since 2023Q3. (Aside from Transport, which averaged 2.6% annual productivity growth during this time, only two other sectors delivered positive productivity growth per quarter during this time period – Public Administration [and] Defence, and Healthcare at 2.6% and 1.4% respectively – but both of [Public Administration and Defence, and Healthcare] are distortionary given the outsized role the public sector plays there). While it is undoubtedly true that HMRC’s PAYE employment measure provide the clearest story of a boost, [HMRC’s PAYE employment measure] also excludes the self-employed—that is a cohort of workers that is growing rapidly. Part-time self-employed workers have formed the lion’s share of the increase in self-employment. [The increase in self-employment] are captured by the Labour Force Survey, but [the Labour Force Survey] has well-publicised statistical issues and is still undergoing refurbishment, so it is difficult to know how robust [the increase in self-employment] is. That said, calculations pooling together self- and PAYE-employment, still show a pick-up in productivity (albeit [the pick-up in productivity] is weaker than with the PAYE data alone). [The author thinks] the fall in PAYE employees (which comes from the reliable PAYE data source) and the concurrent rise in self-employment in the LFS are connected. [The author believes] the catalyst has been the chronically high unit labour costs faced by firms, given strong nominal wage increases and costly labour-market regulation. T ogether, [the author thinks] [strong nominal wage increases and costly labour-market regulation] has put upward pressure on firms’ costs and has been the tipping point in the shedding of employees. [That the catalyst has been the chronically high unit labour costs faced by firms, given strong nominal wage increases and costly labour-market regulation] is not an AI story. One consequence of [the fall in PAYE employees and the concurrent rise in self-employment in the LFS], however, may be that

1. employers have been replacing permanent employees with a more flexible (and cheaper) contract-based workforce, and/or
2. using AI, where it permits. If true, then [costly labour-market regulation] seem to have had the reverse effect on the very employees they were designed to shield (at least in the short-term). The unintended consequence, therefore may have been to catapult UK businesses to swiftly embrace cost-cutting AI technology—a coping mechanism for the rapidly rising labour costs. The new Productivity Puzzle and the AI structural shift The ONS BICS results support [the story that the unintended consequence, therefore may have been to catapult UK businesses to swiftly embrace cost-cutting AI technology]. By far, the biggest reported rise of AI usage amongst firms is in the information [and] communication sector. But [the author also notes] that all sectors, to varying degrees—both at the intensive and extensive margin—are using AI technology primarily to improve their processes, programming and data analysis, and increasingly also for “creativity” use. Absent a market correction, [the author thinks] the long-term impact of the AI shift will be to structurally displace some employees in the labour market, specifically jobs that can be easily automated. While the use of AI is reported to be the highest in the most obvious sectors (information [and] communication, and professional, scientific, and technical), the types of things AI is being used for are “improving business operations” and “providing a product or service to customers”. One could argue that [“improving business operations” and “providing a product or service to customers”] are applicable to all sectors. Indeed, amongst all businesses who use AI regardless of sector, 59% reported using [AI] to improve business operations.1 While [the author remains] optimistic that the productivity improvements are real, [the author is] also cautious and for two reasons. One, much of the productivity “improvement” relies on the administrative PAYE data, which excludes the self-employed, a rapidly expanding cohort (if one believes the LFS). As [the author] don’t have a reliable estimate of how much [the increase in self-employment] is, it is hard to be sure. Two, the productivity improvements – at present – do not appear to be coinciding with a noticeable growth in GDP. Put it differently, [the productivity improvements] are arising due to a slowdown in hiring. Without a market correction, long-term labour-market displacement can lead to structurally higher unemployment, pushing up on U*. The long-term path for interest rates, however, will depend on the positive productivity forces pushing down on inflation on the one hand, against the rising unemployment pressures pushing up on inflation in the opposite direction.
