Introduction One of the more notable exchanges in Kevin Warsh’s nomination hearing (see “Market’s first Warsh-ach test”) was that [Kevin Warsh's] preferred inflation gauges were “trimmed averages” that “take out all of the tail risks, all of the one-off items” to get at the “generalized change in prices”. In this piece, [the author looks] at [trimmed-mean and median inflation measures] and compare [trimmed-mean and median inflation measures] to the more traditional gauges such as headline or core. In particular, [the author focuses] on whether trimmed-mean or median inflation measures are indeed superior measures of trend inflation. The rationale for looking at [trimmed-mean and median inflation measures] is similar to that for core. Excluding food and energy prices is justified, not because [food and energy prices] are unimportant – quite the contrary – but because [food and energy prices] have [food and energy prices'] own idiosyncratic drivers and any large moves up or down are likely to be short-lived or “transitory”. As such, core inflation has often been found to be more informative than headline about trend inflation in certain regimes. Though as [the author] will discuss below, [that core inflation has often been found to be more informative than headline about trend inflation in certain regimes] may not necessarily always be the case.1 Background on trimmed-mean measures Trimmed-mean and median inflation measures make core’s exclusion rationale more product-agnostic. The trimmed-mean measure ranks each granular 1 See for example Matthew Luzzetti, Ph.D. subcomponent of CPI or PCE by their monthly inflation rate and excludes items in the specified top and bottom tails of the monthly distribution – those items with large, potentially idiosyncratic spikes up or down – while the median measure focuses on the item with the monthly price change exactly in the center of the (weighted) distribution, essentially a more extreme trim than the trimmed-mean. Trimmed-mean and median statistics are produced by the Cleveland and Dallas Feds, usually released a couple hours after the BLS [or] BEA official CPI [or] PCE statistics. There are some notable differences in the coverage of the trimmed-mean CPI and PCE. For the CPI, the Cleveland Fed produces the trimmed-mean and removes the top and bottom 8% of the basket with the most extreme monthly moves. The Dallas Fed constructs the trimmed-mean PCE by taking out the 24% with the lowest inflation rates and the 31% with the highest inflation rates.2 Figures 1 and 2 show the monthly prints for core CPI and PCE versus [core CPI and PCE's] trimmed-mean and median alternatives. Generally, [core CPI and PCE versus their trimmed-mean and median alternatives] have similar monthly prints, deviating only when there are particularly large increases in certain core components that get stripped out by trimmed-mean and median. For example, the large run up in used car prices in 2021 boosted core CPI and PCE but was omitted by the alternative inflation measures. Similarly, recent core PCE prints have been elevated by large increases in small goods categories like software and computer accessories that trimmed-mean and median are “looking through”. In year-over-year terms (Figures 3 and 4), median measures seems to run higher than core or trimmed-mean for both CPI and PCE, at least post-GFC and pre - covid. For the CPI, trimmed-mean was relatively in line with core, likel y because the trim amount is relatively small and is more likely to capture the volatile food and energy categories relative to other goods and services. In contrast, trimmed-mean PCE cuts more of the distribution. As such, [trimmed-mean PCE] has been more likely to diverge from core and has tended to run at or slightly above core over [post-GFC and pre - covid]. Are trimmed-mean measures better? Is there necessarily a clear reason to focus on trimmed-mean measures of inflation when setting monetary policy? In this section, [the author] explore reasons why trimmed-mean may not be strictly preferable to headline or core. Trimmed-mean can lag when inflation regimes change One clear benefit to trimmed-mean is that [the trimmed-mean] is easy to communicate why certain products are being excluded on a month-by-month basis. Inflation is measured imprecisely, so excluding some of the “noise” of large moves in smaller categories (which do not necessarily have to be food or energy categories) can provide a clearer picture of the trend. However, fundamental to [that excluding some of the “noise” of large moves in smaller categories (which do not necessarily have to be food or energy categories) can provide a clearer picture of the trend] is the premise that the inflation prints out in the tails are in fact noise and thus not informative about the trend. Previous Fed research has found that trimmed-mean is “more useful in low inflation environments, when the underlying signal is weak relative to the noise in the data”, but may not be able to timely capture changes in the inflation regime that information in the tails may help identify. For example, if large increases in gas prices are indicative of future cost-push energy pressures or spikes in used car prices signal extreme supply-demand imbalances that are likely to persist, then t rimmed-mean and median inflation would systematically strip out that signal. As a result, [trimmed-mean and median inflation measures] would rise much slower than headline or core would [rise]. Indeed, [that trimmed-mean and median inflation measures would rise much slower than headline or core would] occurred post-covid, where the year-over-year rate for both headline and core PCE rose above 3.0% in April 2021, but it was not until roughly six-months later that trimmed-mean (November) and median (October) did so. Were the Fed to have focused solely on trimmed-mean measures of inflation, [the Fed] would have likely responded slower to the inflation data than if [the Fed] also incorporated some signal from headline and core. As such, strictly relying on [trimmed-mean and median inflation measures] could lead to worse monetary policy outcomes. Recent Dallas Fed research pointed out [the potential shortcoming that trimmed-mean and median inflation measures would rise much slower than headline or core would] of trimmed - mean inflation noting that sharp increases in the skewness of the inflation distribution have sometimes preceded or coincided with inflation surges, for example in 1974 and 2021. As a result, trimmed-mean lagged core and headline PCE inflation. However, skewness also spiked in 2012, after which inflation remained subdued. Figure 5 shows the rolling root-mean-squared difference over the past three years of the specified measure from a 37-month centered-moving-average of headline inflation. Of the four measures shown, core and median PCE seem to have the closest distance to t his admittedly simple measure of trend inflation. There are some periods in which trimmed-mean also has a similar distance to trend; however, there also clear periods (e.g. 2021 as described above) in which trimmed-mean presents a less accurate portrayal of trend inflation. As Figure 5 indicates and trimmed-mean’s lagging behavior in 2021 exemplifies, while trimmed-mean may be better in certain regimes (likely those with low [or] stable inflation), [the trimmed-mean] could underestimate (overestimate) inflation during a period when the cross-sectional distribution of inflation is persistently skewed towards the upper (lower) tail. In the current environment where higher tariffs and energy prices have the potential to generate cost-push pressures, trimmed-mean may underperform other metrics. Figure 5: Average distance to a simple measure of trend inflation Forecasting ability Related to the above section, trimmed-mean type inflation measures are often cited as having the ability to improve inflation forecasts. For example, Ball et al (2023) find that across 38 advanced and emerging economies median measures are less volatile than standard core, are more closely related to e conomic slack, and are more closely related to headline inflation. Similarly, Verbrugge and Zaman (2023) find that including trimmed-mean inflation measures can help forecasting inflation over medium- and longer-term horizons. [Verbrugge and Zaman] note, however, that it is not just the “trend information implicit in trimmed-mean estimators” that help to improve forecast accuracy, but by comparing trimmed-mean to headline or core, important information about t he skewness of the cross-sectional inflation distribution can be brought to bear. What is the correct inflation measure? If trimmed-mean inflation measures are not strictly super ior, then what measure should we focus on? Unfortunately, there is not a simple answer. As is the case with all macroeconomic data, extracting the signal from the noise and identifying turning points is not straightforward. Moreover, different inflation measures may be more or less informative about the trend in different regimes. Similar to how [the author looks] at data on the labor market or r-star, [the author prefers] a dashboard approach for monitoring trend inflation, including alternative measures discussed in this note, others not mentioned (e.g. the New York Fed’s multivariate core trend measure which uses a dynamic factor model to isolate the common trend across PCE components), as well as several model-based estimates of trend. Following work by Fed staff, [the author] regularly update [the author's] dashboard of trend inflation measures (a recent example is this piece or within [the author's] monthly inflation chartbook). Currently, almost all of the measures in [the author's] dashboard point to trend inflation being closer to 3% than the Fed’s 2% target. The only exception is trimmed-mean PCE (at 2.33% in February). However, past behavior suggests that trimmed-mean PCE significantly lower than core may not persist. While [trimmed-mean and median inflation measures] may better capture trend inflation in low and stable inflation environments, more traditional headline or core measures may better capture turning points and transitions to more volatile regimes. Warsh’s preference for trimmed-mean aside, it was clear from [Kevin Warsh's] hearing that [Kevin Warsh] wants to change how the Fed thinks and communicates about inflation. Indeed, [Kevin Warsh] expressed [Kevin Warsh's] plan to direct researchers to “use our new understanding and new data sources to see what’s really the inflation rate in the economy.” [Kevin Warsh] alluded to a new data project in mind, a joint public and private sector endeavor including the BLS, to undertake “a survey of a billion prices”. One potential revision to the Fed’s communications could be taking something more akin to [the author's] dashboard approach to inflation by looping a broader set of measures into official communications, in cluding trimmed-mean and median, similar to what some other central banks have done (e.g. the Bank of Canada and the Reserve Bank of Australia). Another pote ntial evolution that Warsh’s comments hinted at could be the eventual adoption of a target range around 2% at some point. In [Kevin Warsh's] comments, [Kevin Warsh] noted that “what we need to do is focus [on the number to] the left of the decimal point, not to the right of the decimal point.” [The eventual adoption of a target range around 2%] would likely be framed as an acknowledgement that inflation is too volatile to micromanage down to the tenth and could be operationalized by presenting a range of different inflation estimates.
