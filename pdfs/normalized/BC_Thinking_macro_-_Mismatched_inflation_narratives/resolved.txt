# Thinking Macro Mismatched inflation narratives

This report builds on the analysis from [the author's] recent note "Energy in the age of fragmentation, " in which [the author discusses] whether an expected secular mismatch between energy supply and demand could generate sustained upside risk to inflation pressure, offsetting medium-term disinflation risks from AI. [The author] recently published The global energy race: Energy in the age of fragmentation, a comprehensive cross-asset report examining the outlook for the global energy sector. This report examines [the global energy race: Energy in the age of fragmentation's] implications for inflation. The inflation dynamic The mismatch between energy supply and demand creates a dynamic that directionally translates into upward pressure on three core factors for inflation risk: baseline expectations, price volatility, and asymmetric risks. As noted in [the global energy race: Energy in the age of fragmentation], energy demand is rising faster than supply at the same time as geopolitical fragmentation is increasing the premium on energy security, creating a complex terrain for global investors to navigate. Key question: does the upside inflation risk from the energy supply [or] demand imbalance overwhelm other disinflationary forces? [The upside inflation risk from the energy supply or demand imbalance] creates a tension in the market narrative. For several months [or] quarters now, the inflation discourse has gravitated toward the idea that AI is near-term inflationary but medium-term disinflationary (for instance, see A Claudy inflation outlook for [the author's] thinking on the topic back in March, and Inflation has a memory problem, where [the author] explore one facet of AI-related inflation). But what if this is wrong, or at a minimum, overly simplistic? While [the author] still strongly sympathize with that framework on a basic level, a more nuanced take would need to acknowledge that the path to any eventual productivity dividend may itself be energy and commodity intensive. The focus of this note is therefore on a growing upside risk that may be underappreciated in inflation pricing: energy. Indeed, one theme from [the global energy race: Energy in the age of fragmentation] is that the energy supply [or] demand imbalance should support commodity prices and implies that inflationary pressure may originate from prices of raw materials, rather than from labor market-related wage increases. [That inflationary pressure may originate from prices of raw materials, rather than from labor market-related wage increases], in turn, suggests that an over-reliance on Phillips-curve-style logic could lead to an understating of upside inflation risks. Source: Barclays Research Baseline expectations Turning to the different channels by which aggregate inflation could be pushed higher by the secular growth in energy and electricity demand, the natural first path is via modal [or] baseline expectations. In essence, excess energy demand should translate into sustained upward pressure on energy commodities (for more details on [that excess energy demand should translate into sustained upward pressure on energy commodities], please see the Commodities in Focus section of [the global energy race: Energy in the age of fragmentation]). [Sustained upward pressure on energy commodities] is an essential factor for assessing inflation risk, as many economists [or] investors rely on commodity futures curves to project market-implied forward raw commodity prices and, by extension, inflation. If [the author's] thesis is correct, and the supply [or] demand imbalance leads to sustained upward pressure on prices of raw materials, [sustained upward pressure on prices of raw materials] should translate into higher commodities futures prices and, thus, higher baseline inflation forecasts. In addition, it is not only the level of the curve, but also the shape that informs inflation risk. For instance, if the entire commodity curve shifts higher, but the curve does not steepen, then in essence the price action implies a one-off price-level shock, rather than a sustained inflation process. With many major commodity curves in backwardation, the first-order implication is actually forward energy deflation, though [the author's] sense is that the signal may be complicated by a combination of futures markets underpricing upside commodity price risk, hedging needs, and risk premia. That is, there is a very real risk that the futures curves are underpricing upside risk. More broadly, a persistent energy deficit would have implications beyond the direct contribution of energy prices to headline inflation. One of the defining features of the post-GFC period was abundant and relatively inexpensive energy, which encouraged investors and policymakers to treat energy shocks as largely transitory. An AI-driven increase in energy demand could challenge that assumption. If markets begin to view energy scarcity as a multi-year structural phenomenon, rather than a cyclical fluctuation, the result may be a higher inflation risk premium embedded in nominal yields, inflation swaps, and breakevens. There is also a convexity dynamic to watch in the inflation indices via the weight in the basket. While [the convexity dynamic] is a bit wonky, [the author] can use the US inflation indices as an example; higher energy prices tend to correlate with a higher weight for energy in the CPI and PCE baskets (Figure 2). If energy prices are rising, not only could there be upward pressure on the energy subcomponents themselves, but the importance of energy for the overall basket would be increasing, leading to a more significant upside impulse, all else equal. [That the importance of energy for the overall basket would be increasing, leading to a more significant upside impulse, all else equal] is especially true for CPI and, thus, inflation market pricing in TIPS breakevens and CPI swaps, but the narrative is equally relevant for other markets (UK RPI, EUR HICPx, etc). Intuitively, [that inflation risk could be influenced by the energy supply or demand imbalance] makes sense and would likely correlate more with upside risks, further underscoring the inherent asymmetry. Higher expected energy volatility should naturally translate into increased inflation uncertainty and upward pressure on inflation risk premium [or] breakevens. One way that [the author] can already see [Higher expected energy volatility] happening is by looking at the CBOE's OVX index (ie, something akin to the VIX for crude oil, Figure 3). Source: CBOE, Macrobond, Barclays Research Asymmetric risks Finally, more elevated volatility by itself does not necessarily introduce upside risk if price shocks are evenly balanced to the upside and downside. However, if risks are asymmetric and skewed to the upside, that would be the case, and indeed [that risks are asymmetric and skewed to the upside] is the world in which [the author generally sees] ourselves. A key focus here is geopolitical fault lines. Two of the biggest price shocks of the past decade have corresponded with a major geopolitical development (Russia invading Ukraine in 2022, the US [or] Iran war in 2026), and in a more fractured geopolitical environment, [a major geopolitical development] may become more frequent. Since [a major geopolitical development] are more correlated with upside risks than downside, [the asymmetry that a major geopolitical development are more correlated with upside risks than downside] should naturally raise perceived inflation risk and, thus, market pricing. Of course, different geographies face different geopolitical risks [or] responses to higher energy prices, but [the author thinks] the high-level underlying logic generally valid. For investors, the relevant point is that a structurally tighter energy system leaves less spare capacity to absorb the next shock. Put together, [the author sees] the above dynamics as supporting the argument that inflation risk premium may be underpriced. To be sure, some risks remain to the downside (AI-related disinflation risks remain real via other channels, and the ever-possible world of a broader economic slowdown), and there are meaningful additional upside risks (fiscal profligacy, de-globalization, etc.), but no discussion is complete without considering what may prove to be a secular mismatch between energy supply and demand.
