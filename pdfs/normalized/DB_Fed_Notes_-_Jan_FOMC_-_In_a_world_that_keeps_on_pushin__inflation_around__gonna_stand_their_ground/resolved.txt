# Fed Notes - Jan FOMC - In a world that keeps on pushin' inflation around, gonna stand their ground

Economics Fed Notes Date 23 January 2026

- [The author expects] the Fed to hold rates steady and present a somewhat more upbeat view about the economy through the policy statement and Chair Powell’s press conference. [The January meeting statement] is likely to upgrade the growth assessment to “solid”, note tentative evidence that unemployment has stabilized, and hint at an improving balance of risks to the outlook.
- Given recent events, Powell’s presser is likely to focus on some non - economic issues – e.g., the recent DoJ subpoena (see “Implications of the latest Fed legal developments”), Governor Cook’s case, who will be the next Fed chair, and whether Powell might remain on the Board (see “Could Jerome Powell follow the Marriner Eccles precedent?”). On the fundamental side, [the author expects] [Chair Powell] will describe policy as “well positioned” as [policy] has entered a plausible range of neutral. [Chair Powell] might also sound somewhat more sanguine on the labor market, while still emphasizing downside risks.
- [The author continues] to anticipate the Fed will remain on hold for the remainder of Chair Powell’s term and will only reduce rates once this year in September. Risks around [the view that the Fed will remain on hold for the remainder of Chair Powell’s term and will only reduce rates once this year in September] are becoming more balanced. Although weaker labor market outcomes could trigger earlier rate cuts than anticipated, recent data have on balance supported [the author's] view that policy is near neutral and therefore may not need to be eased further. FOMC statement Following three consecutive 25bp rate cuts to address downside risks to the labor market, the guidance language in the December FOMC statement signalled a near-term pause. Recall that the Committee did not have the full array of economic indicators that typically form the basis for [the Committee's] economic assessment at the December meeting due to the government shutdown. With Fed officials now better equipped with more complete information on the latest economic trends, the January meeting statement presents an opportunity to reset the table. Starting with the opening stanza, the Committee is likely to upgrade [the Committee's] growth assessment from “a moderate pace” to a “solid pace”. Indeed, [a “solid pace”] was the language that Vice Chair Jefferson used in [Vice Chair Jefferson's] January 16 outlook speech. Regarding the labor market, in light of the recent data – recall that the FOMC only had the September employment report at the time of [the December meeting] – [the author expects] a more sanguine assessment that indicates at minimum no further deterioration since [the December meeting]. Hence, [the author sees] [the January meeting statement] noting “Job gains remain low, and the unemployment rate has stabilized in recent months.” On inflation, as Matthew Luzzetti, Ph.D. Chair Jefferson stated last week, “progress slowed over the past year or so, and inflation remains at a level that is above readings consistent with our inflation target.” With November core PCE inflation up 2.8% from a year ago, unchanged from where the annual rate stood in January, June and September, the Committee may simply state that “Inflation remains somewhat elevated”. Indeed, the risk is that the Committee continues to highlight the lack of inflation progress in [the January meeting statement] similar to Jefferson’s characterization. The most important change to [the January meeting statement] should come in the second paragraph. Recall that the Committee had been justifying rate cuts over the past three meetings by stating that “The Committee is attentive to the risks to both sides of its dual mandate and judges that downside risks to employment rose in recent months.” Given the more sanguine labor market assessment alongside virtually no discernible progress on inflation, [the author expects] the Committee to drop [the Committee's] prior reference to rising labor market risk and simply state “The Committee is attentive to the risks to both sides of its dual mandate.” However, [the author expects] [the January meeting statement] will stop short of the “roughly in balance” language used to describe the risk distribution last January. In short, the January meeting statement should send a strong signal that with the policy rate now within the range of Fed officials ’ estimates of neutral, the Committee is well positioned to respond to risks to either side of [the Committee's] dual mandate should incoming data warrant an adjustment. Though [the author expects] almost all FOMC voters to be on board with such messaging, Governor Miran will likely continue to dissent in favor of additional easing. Press conference [The author expects] considerations around Fed independence and recent geopolitical developments – and the market response – to take precedence over the economic outlook in Chair Powell’s press conference. Most prominently, Powell is likely to be asked about the Department of Justice’s subpoena in relation to [Chair Powell's] Congressional testimony about renovations to the Federal Reserve buildings. [The author] discussed [the author's] key takeaways from [the Department of Justice’s subpoena] here (see “Implications of the latest Fed legal developments”). As a reminder, Powell responded forcefully in a statement, arguing that [the Department of Justice’s subpoena] were not about the testimony or renovations but instead “is a consequence of the Federal Reserve setting interest rates based on our best assessment of what will serve the public, rather than following the preferences of the President. This is about whether the Fed will be able to continue to set interest rates based on evidence and economic conditions —or whether instead monetary policy will be directed by political pressure or intimidation.” [The author expects] Powell will reiterate [the point that the Department of Justice’s subpoena were not about the testimony or renovations] but otherwise refrain from commenting on the open legal question. [Chair Powell] will likely take a similar approach if asked about Governor Cook’s case. This will naturally lead to questions about who will be the next chair of the Federal Reserve and whether Powell has considered remaining on the Board of Governors after [Chair Powell's] chair seat expires in May (see “Could Jerome Powell follow the Marriner Eccles precedent?”). On [who will be the next chair of the Federal Reserve], [the author expects] Powell will not comment. Regarding [whether Powell has considered remaining on the Board of Governors after his chair seat expires in May], Powell has not entertained [whether Powell has considered remaining on the Board of Governors after his chair seat expires in May] in the past when [whether Powell has considered remaining on the Board of Governors after his chair seat expires in May] was raised. [The author expects] [Chair Powell] will follow a similar path and remain noncommittal on [Chair Powell's] future. Most importantly, [Chair Powell] will continue to emphasize that [Chair Powell] remains focused on successfully completing [Chair Powell's] Congressionally-mandated job of achieving price stability and maximum employment. As [Chair Powell] noted in response to a question about [Chair Powell's] legacy to close [Chair Powell's] last press conference, “I really want to turn this job over to whoever replaces me...with the economy in really good shape. That’s what I want to do. I want inflation to be under control, coming back down to 2 percent, and I want the labor market to be strong. That’s what I want. And all of my efforts are to get to that place.” That said, while not a base case, [the author does] see recent legal actions as raising the potential for Powell to stay on as a governor. Indeed, if the administration insists on following through with a criminal prosecution of Chair Powell, and Senate Republicans stand firm in not advancing nominees to the Federal Reserve Board, it is likely the FOMC would choose Powell to remain on as chair. Questions around the implications of geopolitical developments for the economic outlook, and the market response to [recent geopolitical developments], could also feature prominently in [Chair Powell’s press conference]. [The author suspects] that Powell will frame [recent geopolitical developments] as risks to the outlook at this point, rather than impacting officials’ base case for the economy. [Chair Powell] could note that [Fed officials] will be closely watching how financial markets continue to evolve as [Fed officials] gauge the potential impact of [recent geopolitical developments] on the economic outlook. With respect to economic fundamentals, although Powell will likely continue to emphasize that downside risks remain prominent for the labor market, [Chair Powell's] tone may sound somewhat more sanguine. The case for downside risks remains. Employment gains are still subdued, and the breadth of [Employment gains] is still particularly narrow, with only a few sectors (mostly education and health care services) adding labor. In [the sense that the breadth of Employment gains is still particularly narrow], the low hiring dynamic has not changed and the labor market remains susceptible to shocks. At the same time, there are some reasons to be more optimistic at the margin. First and foremost, growth momentum remains robust. After a strong Q3 (4.3%), real GDP growth looks to have remained firm in Q4, against most expectations for a sharp deceleration. [The author expects] [growth momentum] to continue, and it appears Fed officials have generally become more optimistic about the growth outlook as well. For example, NY Fed President Williams recently cited a 2.5 - 2.75% expectation for growth this year, which would have put [NY Fed President Williams] near or above the highest growth forecast from the December SEP. Specific to the labor market, after a burst of layoff announcements in prior months, the latest data (e.g., JOLTS, jobless claims, WARN, permanent layoffs) confirm that the low layoff environment persists. More importantly, there is some tentative evidence that the rise in labor market slack was not as severe as feared. The unemployment rate fell back to 4.375% in December from 4.5% in November (the latter was initially reported at 4.6%). In addition, the quits rate has risen and now looks stable for most o f the past 18 months. [The latest data] have not uniformly improved; job openings to unemployed has fallen and sentiment is substantially weaker. Nonetheless, [the author sees] reasons for some degree of greater optimism around the risk distribution for the labor market. While the labor market backdrop may have brightened somewhat, t he inflation picture has become messier since the December FOMC meeting. Fed officials have generally echoed [Fed officials'] prior comments around an expectation that a disinflationary trend remains in place. The underpinnings of that story were mostly that shelter inflation should decline over time with the potential to undershoot pre-covid run rates and tariffs will likely manifest as a one-time price level shock that will begin to unwind in the second half of the year. Fundamentally, [the stories that shelter inflation should decline over time with the potential to undershoot pre-covid run rates and tariffs will likely manifest as a one-time price level shock that will begin to unwind in the second half of the year] remain in place. However, the disinflationary path is not as clear-cut as believed just a month ago. The government shutdown is likely to move a portion of measured inflation from 2025 to 2026 due to how the BLS dealt with price data. The skew of data collection towards the second half of Nov ember pushed some inflation into December. The carry forward assumption for some of the regional indices is also likely to push some inflation into January. Finally, the carry forward assumption on shelter will push inflation for primar y rents and OER from October 2025 to April 2026. If [that the government shutdown is likely to move a portion of measured inflation from 2025 to 2026 due to how the BLS dealt with price data] were the only stories, the Fed could likely look through [the distortions that the government shutdown is likely to move a portion of measured inflation from 2025 to 2026 due to how the BLS dealt with price data] and continue to emphasize a disinflationary trend. Beyond [the considerations that the government shutdown is likely to move a portion of measured inflation from 2025 to 2026 due to how the BLS dealt with price data] there are some reasons to remain cautious about the inflation outlook. [The author expects] December core PCE to print 0.4% month-over-month and 3.0% year-over-year. Super core inflation remains sticky at elevated levels. Within PCE, health care inflation is likely to rise to 3% year-over-year based on PPI, more than 1 percentage point above pre-Covid rates, and hospital services inflation is near 4%, a several-decade high. In addition, measures of pipeline inflation from the PPI continue t o point to upside risks for core goods inflation over the coming months. With price data showing seasonal strength in January and February in recent years, it likely remains too early to declare victory on inflation. Amongst other topics, Powell could be asked about [Chair Powell's] assessment of the level of restriction. [Updates to LW and HLW] support the view from some hawks that the policy rate may not have m uch further to fall, if at all. Finally, [the author does] not anticipate any news related to the Fed’s balance sheet, with reserve management purchases (RMPs) underway.
