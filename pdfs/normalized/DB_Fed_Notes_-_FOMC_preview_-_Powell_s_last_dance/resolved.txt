Meeting statement [The author's] base case is th at the FOMC statement remains largely unchanged from March. The intermeeting data continue to support the Fed’s description of growth as “solid”, job gains “low” but unemployment “little changed” and inflation “somewhat elevated”. Most importantly, [the author does] not expect [the FOMC statement] to explicitly note that risks to the dual mandate are “roughly” balanced, as [the FOMC statement] did [explicitly note that risks to the dual mandate are “roughly” balanced] in January 2025, and expect the forward guidance language to remain unchanged “In considering the extent and timing of additional adjustments to the target range...” Risks to [the author's] view are skewed toward more hawkish language. Hawkish versions of [the FOMC statement] could drop “additional”, which indicates a dovish bias by Matthew Luzzetti, Ph.D. Peter Hooper, Ph.D. implicitly signal ing the continuation of a series of rate cuts, or insert the aforementioned language around balanced risks. There are good arguments supporting [more hawkish language]. First, core PCE inflation is on track to rise 4.1% annualized in Q1, the largest quarterly increase since Q2 2023. In year-over-year terms core PCE inflation should average just over 3% in Q1, the highest 3-month average since Q1 2024. Recall that in May 2024 FOMC statement, core PCE inflation progress had stalled in similar fashion, and the Committee stated that “Inflation has eased over the past year but remains elevated. In recent months, there has been a lack of further progress toward the Committee’s 2 percent inflation objective.” To be sure, the fed funds rate was at [the fed funds rate's] peak at the time and the Committee was shifting towards a balanced risk assessment. That said, it is possible that the Committee notes the lack of recent inflation progress or simply reverts to describing inflation as “elevated”, scrapping the “somewhat” modifier. Second, as the minutes from the March meeting indicat ed, there has been a growing chorus of participants – in size and conviction – supporting more balanced language. In March, [the growing chorus of participants] grew to “some” from “several” in January who “judged that there was a strong case for a two-sided description of the Committee’s future interest rate decisions in the post-meeting statement, reflecting the possibility that upward adjustments to the target range for the federal funds rate could be appropriate if inflation were to remain at above-target levels.” It’s possible th at [the FOMC statement] could make two-sided risks clear er by removing “additional” from the guidance language. However, with the market pricing a relatively flat path for the fed funds rate, there might be little benefit of raising the potential for rate hikes given the still elevated uncertainties with respect to developments in the Middle East – Brent crude is down a few dollars per barrel from the time of the last FOMC meeting on March 18. Officials might not want to make a revision to [the FOMC statement] that needs to be reversed in relatively short order. As such, [the author sees] June as better placed for such a change, when officials should have more clarity on [the ongoing conflict in the Middle East], labor market stability, and any passthrough to core inflation. Press conference The ongoing conflict in the Middle East will no doubt remain in focus at Chair Powell’s press conference. With uncertainty still pervasive, [the author expects] Powell to once again emphasize that officials are unsure of the precise impact of [the ongoing conflict in the Middle East] on the US econo my and monetary policy. However, Powell could highlight, as many of [Powell's] colleagues have also done, that the longer oil prices remain elevated the more likely are adverse scenarios for both growth [or] labor and inflation. Powell might echo comments from Governor Waller that the Fed could perhaps “look through” a short-lived oil price rise, but a more persistent increase would heighten worries about passthrough to core inflation and could not necessarily be ignored. As important will be how Powell describes the balance of risks to the economy and monetary policy. As noted earlier, in recent meetings support has grown for adopting two-sided language about the policy outlook. Most likely, th e group supporting [adopting two-sided language about the policy outlook] overlapped with the seven officials who expect rates on hold this year and view neutral above the median. Key from Powell will be how [Powell] frames the evolution of the size and influence of that group. In terms of the implications for interest rates, Powell will note that policy remains “well positioned” to respond to the consequences of [the ongoing conflict in the Middle East]. Incoming data and [the ongoing conflict in the Middle East] have reinforced the case for a “wait-and-see” posture from the Fed, but with markets pricing low probability of a change in policy at the next few meetings – pricing officials would largely agree with at present – [the author does] not expect any firm guidance from Powell on how long of a pause is appropriate. On rate hikes, [Powell] could re peat that higher rates are not part of the current baseline outlook, thereby suggesting at least a slight bias towards rate cuts given policy is generally viewed above neutral (more on [that policy is generally viewed above neutral] below), but that officials are not taking any options off the table. [Powell] is unlikely to lay out conditions that could bring about [rate hikes]. Beyond [the ongoing conflict in the Middle East], the timing and direction of the next rate move will depend on a few topics. First, officials should feel better about the labor market. Although payrolls have been extraordinarily volatile – Waller highlighted the fact that [payrolls] have oscillated between positive and negative over the past 10 months – job gains have picked up after smoothing through these swings. [The signal that job gains have picked up after smoothing through these swings] is supported by the weekly ADP series which has accelerated sharply over the past month. The last reading from [the weekly ADP series] was consistent with monthly private sector job gains above 200k. Against a backdrop in wh ich officials have recently adopted a view that the breakeven monthly payrolls reading is close to zero, an acceleration of [monthly private sector job gains above 200k] is notable for policy. It will likely take another month or two of solid labor market data, but if that materializes, officials’ concern about downside risks to employment should diminish further and the case for cutting would rely more heavily – if not entirely – on inflation progress. Second, Powell is likely to repeat [Powell's] sentiment on inflation from the March meeting. At that press conference, inflation was mentioned 67 times, the labor market [or] employment/unemployment was only referenced 40 times. Focus is likely to remain on core goods and in particular the passthrough from tariffs and potential for the oil price shock to add to [the passthrough from tariffs] – the persistence of elevated oil prices will be critical here. Powell specifically highlighted [the Fed] wanted to see progress on [core goods] before contemplating cuts. While officials still expect [the passthrough from tariffs] to wane later this year, evidence from the PPI suggest core goods inflation is likely to stay elevated at least into Q3. There could also be greater focus this time on super core inflati on. When pressed on how the Fed is thinking about [super core], Powell attributed the stickiness to a “bunch of idiosyncratic things” – [the author] will look for more insights into the evolution of [super core]. Ultimately, [Powell] will likely emphasize the need to remain vigilant on inflation and ensure that inflation expectations remain well anchored at 2% in the presence of another oil price shock. Third, and related to the first two, is the Committee’s assessment of the degree of restrictiveness of policy. The median longer-run dot rose to 3.1% in March, and [the author continues] to see evidence in the behavior of growth, inflation and financial conditions that neutral is likely higher than officials have penciled in (see “US Economic Perspectives: Is the Fed r(*)estrictive? “). We will be keen to see whether Powell adjusts his language on this point. Most recently he has described policy as near the border of the top end of neutral and mildly [or] modestly restrictive. Does the recent stabilization of the labor market, stickiness of inflation, and easing of financial conditions warrant a change in this assessment? Powell could also be quizzed on the potential effects of AI on the economy – a topic that dominated market attention prior to the events in the Middle East. While we expect him to emphasize the uncertainty about the impact at this point, he could also echo some of the views from his colleagues in recent speeches, including: AI-driven investment should boost real GDP growth this year, the potential for AI to lift the neutral rate, possible downside risks to the labor market from AI over time, and the potential for AI to impact inflationary pressures further out. Given news reports about a joint meeting with Powell, Treasury Secretary Bessent, and several bank CEOs about potential cyber security risks from Anthropic’s Mythos, Powell will also likely have to co mment on how he views financial stability risks from AI. A few other topics could come up. We don’t expect Powell to break new ground on the balance sheet, where reserve management purchases (RMPs) have recently been lowered from $40bn to $25bn per month. Going forward, the pace of RMPs will continue to be determined by the NY Fed’s assessment of market conditions and what is needed to keep reserves ample. We also don’t expect Powell to comment on Kevin Warsh’s testimony or provide an update on his future (see “Market’s first Warsh-ach test”). On [that Powell would stay on as Chair pro tempore until Powell's successor is confirmed] [Powell] could be asked about recent news reports that the Trump Administration disagrees with the Fed interpretation of the law that Powell should remain chair of the Board of Governors. (Chair of the FOMC seems to have been established through a vote of the Committee earlier this year.) However, Powell did not commit to whether [Powell] would remain on the Board after the DoJ investigation is resolved. At present, the DoJ has signaled [the DoJ's] intention to appeal a recent ruling that quashed the Fed subpoenas, which suggests that a resolution is less likely to occur before Powell’s Chairmanship ends on May 15. As such, the April meeting may not be Powell’s last dance as Fed chair.
