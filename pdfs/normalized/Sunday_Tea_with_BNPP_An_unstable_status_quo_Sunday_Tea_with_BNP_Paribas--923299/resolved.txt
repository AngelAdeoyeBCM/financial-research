## KEY MESSAGES

- The Middle East conflict is extending into [the Middle East conflict's] third month with scant sign of a durable resolution on the horizon. Each additional day of disruption takes the global economy closer to a potential tipping point after which more significant damage will be inflicted.
- At the same time, the ongoing – even if elusive – prospect of a resolution, a strong US earnings season and momentum in recent economic data has helped to support risk assets despite the disruption.
- Should [the Middle East conflict] continue, the path of least resistance right now remains for higher energy prices. But provided incoming data remains solid – and [the author thinks] this week’s US payrolls data will be just that – this bifurcated, somewhat unstable status quo can persist. More of the same: In Global Outlook Spring 2026: Looking beyond the war, dated 24 April, [the author] argued that the conflict in the Middle East was already sufficiently extensive to inflict some damage to the global economy and deliver a sizeable inflationary impulse. At the same time, [the author] suggested that a resilient starting point and tailwinds from AI and defence spending would prevent a recession and, in turn, keep risk assets supported and the dollar under pressure while pushing yields higher. Incoming data and market moves over the past week have been broadly consistent with [the author's] core views, with markets appearing to give the benefit of the doubt to the idea that [disruption in the Middle East] will not lead to a significant hiking cycle or a global recession. Indeed, the S&P500 has continued to climb despite oil prices briefly touch ing a four-year high of more than USD125/bbl. From flows to fundamentals: In equity markets, [the author thinks] the baton was passed last week from flows to fundamentals. The massive inflows that drove the rapid move higher from the lows are begin ning to be replaced by a steady, but slower, rate of incremental buying. The best performing equity sectors YTD are those with the strongest earnings revisions. Price action from the Mag -7 names reporting last week wa s mixed; META, NVDA, MSFT fell, but GOOGL, AAPL and AMZN moved higher post earnings. The read for the capex beneficiaries was bullish. The net effect was volatility dampening at an index level, but still bullish. [The author's] view is that whil e flows and positioning no longer suggest the addition of risk, the earnings momentum picture is so strong [the earnings momentum picture] will likely continue to support a bullish outlook for US equities. An unstable status quo: The challenge, however, is that disruption in the Middle East is entering [disruption in the Middle East's] third month and, at least at the time of writing, there was little to suggest a resolution is imminent. The fact that Qatar Energy extended the force majeure on [Qatar Energy's] LNG supplies by another month into mid-June may signal pessimism in the region about the possibility of a speedy de-escalation. The path of least resistance for oil prices is higher, should [the unstable status quo] continue. After all, each day of reduced flow through the Strait of Hormuz implies a reduction in accumulated oil inventories that cannot be recouped. It is a matter of accounting that, absent a change in the supply dynamic, prices will have to be high enough to create the demand destruction that will bring the system into balance. While the UAE’s surprise exit from OPEC suggests a potential expansion in production that may help to lower prices in the medium term, [the author] don’t expect any impact in the near term. Although the Strait remains closed and the Fujairah pipeline is at or near capacity – [the author expects] more supply from the UAE from 2027 onwards (see UAE leaves OPEC – limited short-term impact, more supply in 2027, dated 28 April). This uneasy tension between the relative optimism of markets and the frozen geopolitical situation, cannot persist forever. At some point there will be a reconciliation. Either a diplomatic solution that puts the global economy on a pathway to gradual normalisation will materialise, or an undeniable and significant economic impact will arise that is harder for markets to look through. [The author's] base case assumes an eventual resolution, but not yet. The unstable status quo can continue for a while. Central bank waiting game: Against [the unstable status quo], last week’s central bank meetings largely confirmed [the author's] expectations that the shock has delivered a sufficiently extensive inflationary impulse to warrant some form of response. The Fed retained [the Fed's] easing bias in form only, but in practice [the Fed] will likely follow a symmetric bias and [the author sees] a growing risk of a hike this year if the unemployment rate declines and the Strait remains closed (see April FOMC: The end of an era, dated 29 April).. 03/05/2026 2 [The author expects] this week’s US jobs market data to be solid – [the author looks] for a 65k rise in non-farm payrolls, a steady unemployment rate and firmer wage pressures. [The US jobs market data] should support a wait-and-see stance from the Fed. But it would only take a few more months of above - trend job growth to push the unemployment rate to 4% or below, a level at which [the author thinks] wage-price dynamics could creep back into the Fed’s thinking. With markets pricing a symmetric bias and then some, [the author thinks] the market has caught up to [the author's] views. At current levels, [the author sees] risk –reward favouring a small long bias in the front end – which [the author maintains] via 2s30s steepeners. The ECB signalled a hike in June, [the author's] base case, as the default option, while the BoE took cuts off the table, which opens the way to rate hikes (see ECB: When, not if and Bank of England: Hikes on the horizon, dated 30 April). In both cases, [the author expects] 50bp of hikes to be delivered this year. Meanwhile, the Bank of Japan delivered a ‘hawkish hold’ and [the author expects] [the Bank of Japan] to deliver a hike in June as well. The BoJ is already behind the curve, in [the author's] view. Additional pressure on USDJPY in recent days has, according to press reports, resulted in finance ministry intervention. [Finance ministry intervention] merely buys time, in [the author's] view, and should the policy mix of ‘rates on hold, yen-buying intervention and expansionary fiscal policy’ persist, the underlying pressure on the currency will likely continue to build, raising the risk that eventually a more rapid policy tightening becomes unavoidable. (see Japan: What does the MoF’s FX intervention imply for BoJ policy, dated 1 May). Given poor fundamentals [the author] would expect the impact of intervention to be short lived and thus [the author likes] using [finance ministry intervention] to buy the dip in [USDJPY] (see FX: Buying the dip in USDJPY, dated 30 April). Policy rate meetings in Poland and the Czech Republic this week (both seen on hold) will be watched closely for any evidence of central bankers becoming less comfortable with the ‘wait-and-see’ approach communicated up to now. The large upside surprise in Polish inflation last week, despite government support measures, has likely lowered the bar to more hawkish communication this week, while the risk of a rate hike in July or September (versus [the author's] central case of rates on hold) has also increased. [The author thinks] the CNB’s quarterly forecast update this week could show some disconnect between the generally more hawkish research department (that has flagged upside risks to inflation) and monetary policy council members who have continued to pour cold water on the prospect of rate hikes later this year and downplayed the need to respond to an ECB that looks likely to raise rates from June. UK local elections with national implications: Finally, after an extended period of political volatility, UK voters will head to the polls on 7 May for England’s local elections and national parliamentary elections in Scotland and Wales. While Thursday’s polling test will not directly impact the composition of the House of Commons or the national of the government of the UK, that doesn’t mean [the polling test] comes without national implications. Polling institutes project widespread losses for the ruling Labour Party, even in constituencies that have traditionally been strongholds, as pressure builds from both sides of the political spectrum. If such losses materialise, there is likely to be a resurgence in political uncertainty as concerns about a change in leadership return to the forefront. Media reports have suggested that key Labour figures, including Angela Rayner and Wes Streeting, are still deciding whether to launch a challenge in the weeks after [the polling test]. Whether through a resignation or a challenge, a change in leadership risks reigniting concerns about the UK’s fiscal position. As UK political risk premium is embedded, [that a change in leadership risks reigniting concerns about the UK’s fiscal position] could add to pressure on gilts and GBP.
