# UK Weekly Digest - What do households want from the Budget

Economics UK Weekly Digest Date 28 August 2026

[The author is] two months away from Chancellor’s Healey’s inaugural budget. Domestic and international attention on the UK’s fiscal outlook will slowly increase as all eyes turn to how the new Burnham government handles [the new Burnham government's] first budget. What do we know so far? Not very much [we know so far]. Questions around whether the Government will try and tackle the medium-term spending review will be front and centre for investors - though expectations are shifting on whether the Government will broach [the medium-term spending review] in the autumn. Talk of a National Social Care service also looks likely to be put on the back burner for now (though [the author] could see firmer plans, including a roadmap to a national social care service being announced later this year). Additional defence spending has also likely been pushed back to the next parliament. With a handful of discretionary measures already announced - mainly to alleviate some of the cost-of-living pressures - the coming few weeks will be crucial in shaping expectations ahead of Budget Day (28 October). For now, it looks like the Government may push for a more modest budget, with a bigger budget coming in the next year or so. Last week, [the author] highlighted some signs of a Burnham bounce, with households turning (marginally) more optimistic on the economy and [households'] personal finances. In [the UK Weekly Digest], [the author turns] to the remaining questions on what households want from the forthcoming Budget.

1. Britons back Burnham’s devolution. In a convincing backing of Burnham’s devolution drive, 41% of households surveyed agreed that more decisions about how England is governed should be made outside of London (net balance: +21%). [That more decisions about how England is governed should be made outside of London] tend ed to resonat e more strongly with the young (where support for devolved economic policy rose to 45%). Breaking [the author's] responses down by income group, it was the low- and high-income groups (< GBP 20k and >50k) that reported relatively high support for devolution. [That Britons back Burnham’s devolution] will be good news for PM Burnham who has championed the acceleration of devolution policy across England. [The author] will likely hear a lot more about the Government’s devolution policy in the Budget, with plans to increase regional fiscal autonomy in the making. 2. Households expect Burnham to deliver lower interest rates. Perhaps one reason why households are more optimistic on the economy and [households'] personal finances is because more households than not, think that PM Burnham will lower mortgage rates over the next six months. Across [the author's] survey, 10% of households think that PM Burnham will oversee a reduction in mortgage rates relative to rais ing [mortgage rates]. And again, it’s the young who are most optimistic about an improvement in mortgage rates (+31%, net balance). Interestingly, high-income earners also feel the same way (+32% net balance). Figure 2: Small majority think PM Burnham will deliver lower mortgage rates in the next 6m Source: dbDIG Primary Research
3. How should the government improve the public finances? [The author] asked households how the Burnham government should go about improving the public finances. Across [the author's] entire sample, only 14% favoured tax rises. 26% of respondents saw spending cuts as the more optimal solution to improve the public finances. 15% saw “neither” tax rises nor spending cuts as an optimal solution. And 10% didn’t have a view. Support for higher taxes was concentrated among the young – but only barely at +15%. In fact, more young people thought spending cuts would be better. Where was support for spending cuts the highest? Among the older-aged population (55+) [support for spending cuts the highest], whilst those on higher incomes (> GBP 50k) also reported strong support for public spending reform (28%). Figure 3: Households think improvement to public finances should come from spending cuts as opposed to tax rises Source: dbDIG Primary Research
4. What taxes do households think Government should increase (if it had to)? If the Government had to increase taxes, which one should it focus on? Households overwhelmingly favoured corporation tax rises (32%) as the main form of tax revenue raiser at the coming budget. Second on the list was a capital gains tax, with 16% opting for a rise to generate additional tax revenues. Third on the ranking for households would be property taxes. Just under 10% of households surveyed signalled an appetite for property tax reform (with the young most in favour of such tax changes). Income tax rank ed next on the list of potential tax hikes favoured by households (8%). Figure 4: Which taxes should the Government prioritise? Source: dbDIG Primary Research
5. Which areas of public spending should be reduced? Where should Government look to reduce its spending? [The author's] dbDIG survey highlighted strong convictions across multiple channels. 52% of survey respondents noted that overseas aid should be cut (note, news reports suggest that the Government is eyeing an increase in the overseas aid budget to 0.7% of GNI). 36% of respondents said [36% of respondents] would prefer to see the size of the civil service cut. 33% of respondents noted that welfare spending was also too high and could be shaved. 23% saw culture, media, sport as another area ripe for spending cuts. And very interestingly, 13% of households saw defence spending as another potential source f or public spending cuts – despite heightened geopolitical uncertainty.
