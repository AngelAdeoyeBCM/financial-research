# Fed Notes - Implications of the latest Fed legal developments

Economics Fed Notes Date 12 January 2026

This weekend offered extraordinary news that the Department of Justice issued a subpoena to Federal Reserve Chair Powell in relation to [Federal Reserve Chair Powell's] Congressional testimony about renovations to the Federal Reserve buildings in Washington, D.C. (see here). In response, Powell issued a statement (see here), arguing that [the subpoena to Federal Reserve Chair Powell] were not about [the Congressional testimony about renovations to the Federal Reserve buildings] or renovations but instead “is a consequence of the Federal Reserve setting interest rates based on our best assessment of what will serve the public, rather than following the preferences of the President. This is about whether the Fed will be able to continue to set interest rates based on evidence and economic conditions—or whether instead monetary policy will be directed by political pressure or intimidation.” [The author offers] a few thoughts on [the subpoena to Federal Reserve Chair Powell]. First, [the subpoena to Federal Reserve Chair Powell] offer an unprecedented challenge to Fed independence. There are various examples of presidents pushing Fed chairs towards particular actions historically. Perhaps most famously, LBJ mounted a pressure campaign against the Fed’s interest rate policy under William McChesney Martin in the mid- 1960s that included a physical confrontation between the two. President Trump has also pushed for easier Fed policy on countless occasions, has threate ned to remove Chair Powell, and has attempted to remove Fed Governor Lisa Cook. That said, [the subpoena to Federal Reserve Chair Powell] is the first time that the Department of Justice has threatened legal action against a sitting Fed chair, a unprecedented escalation in the administration’s pressure campaign for lower rates. Second, Congress and markets remain important constraints on the administration’s ability to reshape the Fed. Regarding [Congress], Republican Senator Tillis noted that [Republican Senator Tillis] would block any additional nominees for the Fed Board of Governors until the administration’s actions are resolved. Without [Republican Senator Tillis'] vote, the Senate Banking Committee would be split 12 -12 on Fed nominees (assuming all other Republicans voted yes), which would stall the nomination in Committee absent 60 votes in the Senate. Tillis’s pledge has received support from additional Republicans, including Senator Cramer, another Republican on the Senate Banking Committee, and Senator Murkowski from Alaska, among others. Regarding markets, the market reaction – namely the significant sell-off at the long end of the curve and sharp fall in stock markets -- was an important driver of the Trump administration’s reversal of [the Trump administration's] Liberation Day tariff announcements. A similar reaction could constrain the administration on [the subpoena to Federal Reserve Chair Powell] as well. The moves today directionally support [the view that a similar reaction could constrain the administration on the subpoena to Federal Reserve Chair Powell as well], with long-end rates initially rising by a few basis points, approaching the highest levels in four months, and stock prices initially opening down 0.7%. However, the reversal in risk assets and partial unwind of the bond market moves implies that markets are unlikely t o cause any Matthew Luzzetti, Ph.D. Peter Hooper, Ph.D. reassessment by the administration at this point. The mild market move so far is likely in part a consequence of evidence the Senate will be a constraining force. Third, [the author has] argued that it will be difficult for the next Chair to come from outside of the Fed and convince [the next Chair's] [or] [the next Chair's] colleagues to cut rates if the data are inconsistent with that action. Yes, the Fed chair can have outsized influence over the FOMC. The recent rate cut at the December meeting likely does not occur if Powell does not push [the recent rate cut at the December meeting] through. But that influence is earned. Powell built considerable political capital over time by forging a consensus on the Committee that accounted for a variety of views. At times [Federal Reserve Chair Powell] was able to utilize that capital to move a contentious action through the Committee, like in December. The administration’s legal threats over the weekend raise greater concerns around Fed independence and reinforce [the author's] view that a noticeable policy pivot in a dovish direction is unlikely if [the author gets] a new Fed chair, unless the data argue for that outcome. Consistent with [that a noticeable policy pivot in a dovish direction is unlikely if the author gets a new Fed chair, unless the data argue for that outcome], front-end yields have actually risen this morning, as the market has priced out a small amount of rate cuts this ye ar. In short, t he market is s ignaling [the subpoena to Federal Reserve Chair Powell] may prove counterproductive to the administration’s stated aim of lower interest rates. Fourth, [the author] recently wrote a piece discussing the history of Fed chairs staying on after their terms expired and arguing that there were plausible paths to Powell choosing that outcome (see “Could Jerome Powell follow the Marriner Eccles precedent?”). While [that Powell could choose to remain at the Fed] was never a base case, and indeed [that Powell could choose to remain at the Fed] seemed like a relatively remote possibility, this weekend’s events likely increase the probability that Powell could choose to remain at the Fed. Indeed, if the administration insists on following through with a criminal prosecution of Chair Powell, and Senate Republicans stand firm in not advancing nominees to the Federal Reserve Board, it is likely the FOMC would choose Powell to remain on as chair.
