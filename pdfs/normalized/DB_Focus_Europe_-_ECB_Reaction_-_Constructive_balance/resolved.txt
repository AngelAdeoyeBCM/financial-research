As expected also, President Lagarde repeated that monetary policy is “in a good place”. There was no sense of imminent change in policy, in either direction. But the ECB is taking nothing for granted. [The ECB] continues to keep the options open going forward with the data-dependent, meeting-by-meeting, no-precommitment approach to policy. Domestic resilience vs. external vulnerabilities The external environment remains “challenging” because of tariffs [or] global trade tensions and “a stronger euro over the last year”. The exchange rate was mentioned in the longer press conference statement, not the shorter policy decision. [That the exchange rate was mentioned in the longer press conference statement, not the shorter policy decision] implies the euro was not considered a significant issue. In the press conference President Lagarde played down the exchange rate. The ECB monitors the euro, but the big appreciation was a year ago. [The big appreciation] is already incorporated in the ECB’s inflation forecast. Since last summer, the euro has been in a normal trading range. As [the author] mentioned in a recent note, it would need further and clear appreciation for the currency to be monetary policy relevant. The domestic resilience story felt richer than before. In addition to the references to a low unemployment rate, solid private sector balance sheets, the pass - through of earlier rate cuts, and rising spending on information and communication technology (A I), the February statement includes references to the gradual roll out of public spending on defence and infrastructure (in the description of current conditions, not just as an upside risk) and rising spending on construction. Inflation baseline unchanged HICP inflation fell to 1.7% yoy in January, in line with [the author's] expectations. [HICP inflation of 1.7% yoy in January] is below the ECB staff forecast of 1.9% for Q1 published in December. President Lagarde played down the significance of [HICP inflation of 1.7% yoy in January], saying [HICP inflation of 1.7% yoy in January] is more like the inflation profile the ECB was anticipating in September (that forecast did not lead to policy easing). As far as the Governing Co uncil is concerned, [the Governing Council] remains confident that inflation will be on target in the medium-term. While negotiated wages and the forward wage tracker point to moderating pressures, there was a new statement referring to uncertainty about payments above negotiated settlements. In 2025, wage inflation came in above the level implied by settlements, consistent with positive wage drift. [Positive wage drift] may signal labour market tightness. Lagarde did not talk up AI productivity. [President Lagarde] said [AI productivity] would take a while to unleash. Broadly balanced risks There was also a sense of greater richness and depth to the upsides in the risk assessment on growth. Relative to December, [the risk assessment on growth] now references the adoption of new technologies (AI), the EU’s new trade agreements (Mercosur, India) and the potential for deeper integration of the EU Single Market. EU leaders will discuss policies to improve the Single Market on 12 February. The ECB is planning to share [the ECB's] views on what needs to change with EU leaders. To the extent the new growth upsides are supply factors, [the new growth upsides] are not necessarily additive to the inflation outlook. Higher energy prices were added as a new upside risk to inflation. There is still a downside risk to inflation from countries with overcapacity (China). The wording is subtly different, however. Previously there was a downside risk to inflation if [countries with overcapacity] “increase” [countries with overcapacity's] exports to the euro area. Now the assessment says the risk would come from exports increasing “further”. [That the risk would come from exports increasing “further”] could imply any rise from current levels of imports would be considered a threat to inflation. No directional signal, comfortable at 2% Overall, President Lagarde described the risks as broadly balanced. [Broadly balanced risks] was Lagarde’s reading of the debate on the Governing Council. The official statement just lists the upside and downside risks. Despite the constructive [or] positive/hawkish elements, the ECB is not sending a directional signal for monetary policy. The ECB is comfortable with rates at 2% for the time being. DB Outlook The latest ECB press conference was consistent with [the author's] baseline that ECB policy rates will remain at 2% through 2026. In [the author's] baseline, the next move is a hike in 2027. [The author sees] fiscal easing and a tight labour market eventually leading to a rotation of inflation risks from near-term undershooting to medium-term overshooting. In 2026, the risks for now remain skewed towards further policy easing given the inflation undershoot and the external vulnerabilities: trade frictions, China competitiveness, geopolitical uncertainty, FX appreciation, etc. [The author's] baseline assumes the domestic resilience will dominate the external vulnerabilities and the ECB be able to stay on hold. But it’s fair to say that uncertainty around the path of monetary policy is high.
