# Fixed Income Blog - When positioning becomes a policy problem

Rates Fixed Income Blog Date 13 October 2026

Hedge fund leverage has clearly captured the attention of policymakers, and for good reason. The BIS refers to [hedge fund leverage] as the new fiscal-financial stability nexus. Over the past week alone, there have been multiple speeches and reports from the likes of the BoE, IMF, BIS, Federal Reserve on the topic. As the IMF notes, the substantial growth in h edge fund assets over the past decade has been largely financed through leverage, either through repo for fixed income or prime brokerage for equity exposure. Sovereign bond exposure has more than doubled since 2022. There is a real consensus that hedge funds now play a critical role in sovereign bond markets, and that high leverage, crowded trades and bank to non-bank financial institutions (NBFI) interconnections can amplify shocks. [That high leverage, crowded trades and bank to non-bank financial institutions (NBFI) interconnections can amplify shocks] is compounded by a lack of visibility around exposures and linkages. Positioning played a big role in the widening in front-end OAT spreads over the past week and has also been a factor in driving the front end in GBP and EUR throughout the year. Two other well-known examples include the ‘dash for cash’ in 2020 and LDI stress in 2022. There are a number of solutions being discussed. [The solutions being discussed] broadly include:

1. better data and surveillance,
2. central clearing for cash and repo,
3. minimum haircuts, and
4. credible central bank backstops. The first three measures are intended to improve the resilience of the system in the event of a shock, rather than directly targeting a specific counterparty or level of leverage. Clearing will reduce counterparty risk and introduce netting efficiencies. Margin and haircut practices conversely will dampen the excessive build-up of leverage. In the US, the market is working towards the 2027 deadline for mandatory clearing of eligible UST transactions. In the UK, the BoE has been consulting on central clearing and haircuts with potential policy proposals due in early 2027. [Central clearing and haircuts] are complex, given the need to assess the trade-offs between calibration and market impact. As a result, [central clearing and haircuts] will take time to implement. Ultimately, t his still leaves markets reliant on central bank backstops during periods of severe stress. [Central bank backstops during periods of severe stress] raises questions on the type of intervention, as well as the criteria to determine whether [intervention] is necessary. De Cos of the BIS (mooted as a contender for the next ECB Presidency) makes the point that “to address stress centred on NBFIs, central banks cannot rely on lending operations but instead need to rely on asset purchases, which as discussed before may be hard to unwind and price”. But that “separating price movements caused by fundamentals from those driven by market dysfunction can be very challenging. This is particularly true during stress episodes, as dysfunction is often triggered by news about fundamentals, including fiscal shocks”[, De Cos of the BIS (mooted as a contender for the next ECB Presidency) makes the point]. Arguably [that separating price movements caused by fundamentals from those driven by market dysfunction can be very challenging] raises the bar for intervention, meaning [the author] may need to get used to greater volatility, especially as changes in market structure make such episodes more likely.
