# US Economic Perspectives - Reaping the productivity gains from prior labor market tightness

Economics US Economic Perspectives Date 8 January 2026

Four years ago, [the author] highlighted how the US economy was likely entering the early stages of a sustained productivity boom (see “Is a productivity boom in the offing?”). [The sustained productivity boom] is confirmed by this morning’s data, which showed productivity advanced at 4.1% and 4.9% annualized in Q 2 and Q 3 2025, respectively. Over the past three years, real output per hour in the nonfarm business sector has grown 2.5% annualized. That ranks near the 65th percentile since WWII. Since 1985, that reading ranks at the 68th percentile. Indeed, the only non-recessionary period over the past 40 years where productivity growth outperformed the recent episode is the late 1990s. [Productivity at 4.1% and 4.9% annualized in Q 2 and Q 3 2025] are also likely to be revised higher once payrolls are downgraded with the benchmark revisions. Figure 1: Productivity trend has been strong in recent years Figure 2: Percentile rank of current productivity growth Source: BLS, Haver Analytics, Deutsche Bank Rather than optimism that AI would deliver a very early boost to productivity growth, we underpinned this assessment on the stable relationship between wage growth and productivity in recent decades. In particular, wage growth tends to reliably lead productivity growth by 2-3 years, as the former creates conditions – e.g., a tight labor market makes labor scarce and expensive, which incentivizes capital deepening and innovation (and vice versa) – that perpetuate the latter. 1 The historically tight labor market that prevailed in 2021/2022 thus sent a strong signal that productivity growth was likely to pick up in the near term. 1 Research from the St. Louis Fed has demonstrated [the relationship between wage growth and productivity] also holds at the micro level. In the near-term at least, the most important consideration is whether productivity gains, perhaps driven by AI, allow firms to continue to substitute away from labor, leaving the labor market in a precarious state. [The author's] baseline remains that the labor market will stabilize this year, supported by a stronger growth outlook as headwinds fade (e.g., trade policy uncertainty) and tailwinds emerge (e.g., greater fiscal support from tax cuts). But weaker labor market outcomes remain a clear risk. More generally, [the author expects] continued strong productivity growth to intensify comparisons of [the sustained productivity boom] to the late -1990s, an episode where Fed Chair Greenspan initially argued for holding back on tightening given [Fed Chair Greenspan's] belief that productivity gains would al low for inflation-less growth (though the Fed eventually raised rates from 4.75% to 6.5% during the late -1999/early-2000 period). In a recent report, [the author] detailed the similarities and differences with that episode (see “Why a repeat productivity surge may not be the same panacea for price pressures”). With respect to the latter, [the author sees] other factors, namely a reversal of disinflationary pressures from global and demographic forces, a s limiting the read-through from the Fed’s response in the 1990s to today.
