Beyond the dots, [the author] will be most interested in revisions to 2027 inflation forecasts, which will signal any shift in views about the persistence of inflation pressures -- a critical determinant of rate hikes.

With the Committee moving increasingly in a hawkish direction, [the author expects] Warsh to tack towards the center, not argue for near-term rate cuts, and not take rate increases off the table. That said, [Chair Warsh] could look to counterbalance [the signals of Warsh to tack towards the center, not argue for near-term rate cuts, and not take rate increases off the table] with a broader narrative that envisions medium-term disinflationary pressures from AI and productivity. Summary of Economic Projections (SEP) [The author expects] several revisions to the [Summary of Economic Projections] – the most important being the median policy rate forecast. In the March [Summary of Economic Projections], seven officials had pencilled in one rate cut for 2026 while seven others expected to keep the policy rate steady this year. In light of recent developments and Fedspeak (see “US Economic Perspectives: DB AI hawk-dove update: More hawks eyes on inflation “and “Fed Watcher: Officials re-assess inflation risks, re-open hike debate “), we expect a general upward shift in the dots with 4-5 officials projecting a hike in 2026, thereby pushing the median up to 3.625%. We would not be surprised to see more officials projecting a hike than a cut in 2026 given that the most dovish forecaster, Stephen Miran, has now left the Board. We see the 25bps increase likely carrying over into 2027 and 2028 medians as well. In addition, we expect the median long-run rate to edge up further to 3.25%. Recall that several officials have mentioned the potential of a higher neutral rate given AI investment and building evidence that the Fed may not be restrictive (see “Overinsured?”). To be sure, it is entirely possible that Chair Warsh do es not submit dots at all for this [Summary of Economic Projections]. It is also possible that Governor Bowman joins [Chair Warsh]. However, [that Chair Warsh do es not submit dots at all for the June Summary of Economic Projections] could also potentially signal an imminent end to the [Summary of Economic Projections] – a step which the majority of the Committee may not feel comfortable with taking at this point. Recall that Chair Powell had initiated a review of communications policy during last year’s policy framework review with a focus on how [the Committee] could improve the [Summary of Economic Projections]. The Committee was unable to reach a consensus and it was therefore left to Warsh to pick up the debate where Powell left off (should [Chair Warsh] choose to do so). The rise in the policy rate should be partly motivated by upward revisions to the median headline and core inflation forecasts. For headline PCE, [the author expects] the median estimate to rise by 70bps to 3.4% for 2026 and 10bps (to 2.3%) for 2027, leaving 2028 unchanged at 2.0%. [The author expects] 2026 core PCE to rise by 30bps to 3.0% and 2027 to edge up a tenth to 2.3% -- the latter is most important to watch as a more significant upward revision would be a stronger argument for higher interest rates. Similar to the headline PCE forecasts, policymakers are likely to still signal a return to 2.0% by 2028. Regarding the growth and labor market forecasts, [the author expects] minimal revisions of the mark to market variety. Finally, [the author expects] some shift in the distribution of risks around officials’ forecasts that would reinforce the hawkish developments in the median dots. In particular, risks to the labor market are likely to be more balanced. In short, the [Summary of Economic Projections] should affirm that the Fed is firmly on hold in the near term with the focus being squarely on inflation at this point given a largely stable growth and employment outlook. Overall, [the author expects] Chair Warsh will stick to a few of the key themes [Chair Warsh] has outlined about the Fed’s communications, including most recently at [Chair Warsh's] nomination hearing in front of the Senate Banking Committee (see Fed Notes: Market’s first Warsh-ach test). Specifically, [the author expects] Warsh to refrain from any form of forward guidance, as [Chair Warsh] has been consistently critical of that practice in recent years. As [the author] noted, [Chair Warsh] may opt to not submit “dots” in the [Summary of Economic Projections], and [the author] would expect [Chair Warsh] to downplay any signal from the dot plot even if [Chair Warsh] chooses to provide [Chair Warsh's] views on the appropriate path for policy. So what will we hear from Warsh? [The author thinks] [Chair Warsh] will take the opportunity to frame the discussions at the meeting. [That Chair Warsh will take the opportunity to frame the discussions at the meeting] could include that the Committee believes policy is well positioned at the moment and refrain from providing guidance about future policy actions. If pressed, [the author thinks] [Chair Warsh] is unlikely to either suggest that the Committee is actively contemplating rate hikes or take that action off the table. While [the author suspects] [Chair Warsh] will reflect a discomfort with inflation – an intensifying sentiment across the Committee best reflected by the recent hawkish pivot by Governor Waller – [the author also expects] Warsh to balance [the discomfort with inflation] against a broader framing of the economic outlook. Recall that Warsh has criticized forward guidance, data dependence, and the Fed’s models, leaving a narrative-based approach as potentially most relevant. Within [the narrative-based approach], [Chair Warsh] could emphasize the importance of productivity growth and AI for providing a strong backdrop for supply-side growth that – over time – could prove to be disinflationary. Warsh is also likely to highlight some aspects of the inflation outlook that are less concerning, including softer measures of trend inflation, moderating wage growth, stable labor market slack, shelter disinflation, and the potential for tariff price pressures to abate over time. That said, [the author does] not anticipate an overtly dovish message from Warsh – e.g., [the author does] not expect [Chair Warsh] to lean excessively on measures like trimmed mean inflation – as doing so would risk a more significant rise in inflation expectations and bond yields in the belly and longer end of the curve. Notably absent from [Chair Warsh's] nomination hearing was a focus on the labor market. While in previous years that side of the dual mandate ha s provided scope for the Fed to lower rates even in the face of elevated inflation, recent data have provided Fed officials with greater confidence that the labor market is at worst stabilizing. Indeed, recent evidence suggests that job gains have picked up noticeably, running well above common estimates of the breakeven rate. If [that job gains have picked up noticeably, running well above common estimates of the breakeven rate] persists, upside risks to the labor market could well emerge – something [the author] will be looking for in the risk distribution charts at the back of the [Summary of Economic Projections]. [The author] will be keen to see how much focus Warsh gives to the labor market and [Chair Warsh's] assessment of current and prospective trends.
