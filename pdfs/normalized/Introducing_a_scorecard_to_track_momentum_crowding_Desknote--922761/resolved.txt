At its core, crowding in financial markets occurs when too many investors hold similar positions for similar reasons. In the context of equity factors, [crowding in financial markets] manifests as an excess accumulation of capital in stocks that score highly on a particular characteristic. The mechanism is self-reinforcing: as a factor attracts inflows, its constituent stocks are bid upward, performance improves, further inflows follow and the pattern resumes. The trade becomes consensus, and consensus trades are fragile. The risk is not merely theoretical. When positioning gets sufficiently crowded, even a modest shift in sentiment can trigger a disorderly unwind. The August 2007 “quant crisis” remains the canonical episode, but the momentum crashes of early 2009 and late 2018 share the same fingerprints, as does 2021’s post-Covid reflation trade [share the same fingerprints]. Momentum is structurally prone to crowding. [The US momentum factor] is a purely price-based and self-referential signal. [That the US momentum factor is a purely price-based and self-referential signal] makes momentum structurally negatively skewed (Figure 2). Gains are steady and gradual, but [the US momentum factor's] crash episodes are disproportionately severe. This paper attempts to develop and test a set of crowding indicators drawing on cross-sectional price dynamics, options market information, fundamental valuation signals and trend analysis. The goal is to provide a structured framework for assessing when the risk [or] reward of holding momentum tilts unfavourably and, conversely, when a less crowded momentum factor presents a compelling entry point. Shared ownership creates shared price dynamics – and that co-movement, once stripped of common risk factors, is precisely what [the author] seek to capture. Construction Methodology The construction proceeds in two steps. For each stock in [the author's] US momentum basket (BNPFMOM1 Index), [the author] run a time-series regression of daily returns against the S&P 500 and four Dow Jones equity style factors: momentum, value, quality and low beta. [The S&P 500 and four Dow Jones equity style factors] absorb the bulk of systematic return variation, namely market beta and factor exposures. What remains – the residual – is in theory idiosyncratic to each stock. [The author] then compute the average pairwise correlation of [the residual] across all holdings in the basket. [The average pairwise correlation of these residuals across all holdings in the basket] becomes [the author's] crowding score, percentile-ranked against [the average pairwise correlation of these residuals across all holdings in the basket's] own history to produce a 0–100 reading (Figure 3). The link to crowding is intuitive. Under normal conditions, idiosyncratic residuals should be largely uncorrelated – by construction, there is no common factor driving [the residual]. But when a large and homogeneous investor base owns the same stocks, selling pressure or momentum-chasing affects all holdings simultaneously and in the same direction. The result is a rise in residual co-movement that cannot be explained by market or factor dynamics alone. [The rise in residual co-movement that cannot be explained by market or factor dynamics alone] is the crowding signal. In spirit, [the price-based proxy for crowding] is close to Baltas (2019), who similarly exploited residual return correlations to detect positioning congestion in futures markets. The intuition is simple: when a trade is crowded, investors express growing conviction in its continuation through the options market. In turn, that directional consensus leaves a detectable imprint on the implied volatility surface. Construction Methodology For each stock in [the author's] US long [or] short momentum basket (BNPFMOM1 Index), [the author] decompose the implied skewness of the return distribution into two distinct components. The upside skew is constructed as the spread between the three-month implied volatility at a 120% strike and the at-the-money (ATM) implied volatility, computed separately for the long and short legs of the factor and then weighted accordingly. A high reading signals pricing of strong continuation on the long leg in options, i.e. investors are expressing growing conviction that momentum winners will keep winning. The downside skew mirrors [the logic that the upside skew is constructed as the spread between the three-month implied volatility at a 120% strike and the at-the-money (ATM) implied volatility]: [the downside skew] compares the three-month implied volatility at an 80% strike to ATM, again across both legs. Elevated downside skew on the short leg indicates market pricing of continued deterioration of momentum losers, reinforcing the directional consensus on that side of the book. The two components are then summed into a single composite implied skewness score, which is percentile-ranked over [the single composite implied skewness score's] history. The options market is in many ways a more forward-looking crowding gauge than price-based measures. Residual correlations reflect what has already happened in positioning while implied skewness signals what options traders expect to happen. When crowding is building, that expectation tends to manifest in the volatility surface before it shows up in realised returns. The two indicators are therefore complementary. The premise is straightforward: if crowding creates excess capital in winners at losers’ expense, it should leave a detectable imprint not only in prices and volatility surfaces but also in the relative valuation multiples of the long and short legs. Construction Methodology [The author] construct [the valuation spread between momentum winners and losers] as follows. For each stock, [the author] retrieve three valuation multiples – price-to-earnings (PE), price- to-sales (PS), and price-to-book (PB). [The author] compute a weighted aggregate valuation for the long leg using positive exposures, and a similar measure for the short leg using absolute negative exposures. [The valuation spread between momentum winners and losers] is the log-ratio of short-to-long valuation, capturing how expensive the long leg trades relative to the short leg. [The author] z-score [the valuation spread between momentum winners and losers] against [the valuation spread between momentum winners and losers'] expanding history, interpreting the indicator relative to [the valuation spread between momentum winners and losers'] own past distribution and avoiding look-ahead bias. The final composite valuation spread is the simple average of the three z-scores – PE, PS and PB – giving a more robust reading than any single multiple. A high z-score indicates the long leg trades at a historically elevated premium relative to the short leg, beyond normal cross-sectional valuation dispersion. The link to crowding is intuitive and grounded in literature. Momentum baskets are long recent winners and short recent losers. Normally, winners trade at a moderate premium – a valuation gap reflecting genuine earnings momentum and compensated by forward returns. When the trade becomes crowded, persistent inflows push winners’ valuations higher, detached from fundamental improvement. The spread widens not because the longs are better businesses, but because too much capital chases the same stocks. [The dynamic that persistent inflows push winners’ valuations higher, detached from fundamental improvement] precedes momentum crashes: the long leg becomes expensive, the short leg cheap, and potential energy for a sharp mean-reversion builds. [The valuation spread between momentum winners and losers] is the only one anchored in fundamentals, not market microstructure or options pricing.
