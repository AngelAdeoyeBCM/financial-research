# US Economics Notes - What you need to know for the week ahead

Economics US Economic Notes Date 23 October 2026

Commentary for Monday: This week’s economic calendar heats up with the main event being Wednesday’s FOMC meeting. As [the author] noted previously (see “Fed Notes: October FOMC preview: A tricky meeting amidst no inflation treats “), we expect the Fed to hold rates steady this week. Chair Warsh’s press conference will be focused on several topics: Why the Committee decided to not raise rates? How has recent inflation data impacted the monetary policy outlook? What is the Committee’s assessment of financial conditions after recent AI-related wobbles? Note that we will host our usual webinar ahead of the FOMC meeting on Tuesday at 10AM; to register please see Webinar: USD rates outlook ahead of the October FOMC meeting. Regarding the meeting statement, we expect minimal changes. Other than the policy action language reflecting the hold decision, the only other revision we envision is the removal of the second to last sentence explaining the rationale behind last month’s hike: “Today’s policy action will support a timelier return to the Committee’s 2 percent goal.” To be sure, the Committee could provide a new basis for the decision to hold, possibly referencing recent tightening of financial conditions or the desire to see more data to judge underlying inflation trends. However, [the author doubts] Warsh will want to add language that could be misinterpreted as a form of forward guidance or a backtracking on the Fed’s commitment to achieve price stability in a timely manner. The easiest course of action is to remove [the second to last sentence explaining the rationale behind last month’s hike] and add [the second to last sentence explaining the rationale behind last month’s hike] back when (if) [the Committee] hike again at the December meeting. Dissents to a hold are likely. [The author] would not be surprised for Dallas Fed President Logan and Cleveland Fed President Hammack to both dissent, as [Dallas Fed President Logan and Cleveland Fed President Hammack] did [dissent] to the hold in July. As [the author's] recent AI Fedspeak monitor revealed, communications from [Dallas Fed President Logan and Cleveland Fed President Hammack] re mained hawkish, even prior to the hotter September inflation data (see “AI Fedspeak monitor: Hawkish consensus, measured tightening”). With respect to [Chair Warsh’s press conference], Warsh is likely to signal that recent inflation data reinforce expectations from the September meeting that further tightening will likely be needed over time. However, skipping October allows officials more time to assess trends in the data. There are other factors that could support holding rates steady at [the October FOMC meeting]. Financial conditions have started to tighten, though [financial conditions] remain accommodative on many metrics. Officials might want to see how much FCI tightening occurs for reasons outside of monetary policy before taking further action. One could also argue that if tightening is needed to catch up to a rising neutral rate, it makes sense to take a gradual approach in [tightening to catch up to a rising neutral rate] given that r-star is uncertain. Matthew Luzzetti, Ph.D. Finally, while not a primary consideration, [the author] could imagine that all else equal the Fed might prefer to stay out of the headlines for raising rates just ahead of a midterm election where affordability is the principal concern (see “What you need to know for the Mid-Terms chartbook [and] Webinar registration “). All that said, we see strong fundamental reasons that the Fed should continue to raise rates. Growth momentum remains solid, the labor market is stable with risks of re-tightening, inflation pressures remain broad and elevated, and financial conditions are still not meaningfully restrictive, indeed they remain accommodative on many metrics. Given this backdrop, the case for skipping October is not terribly compelling. In the wake of the July press conference that triggered concerns about the Fed’s commitmen t to price stability, Warsh will want to avoid any indications that the Fed is unwilling to take appropriate action against sticky inflation. He could note that they are, of course, watching issues related to AI valuations and financial stability risks; however, we think he will avoid any language that would increase the focus on this issue. Warsh is likely to offer boilerplate language to the effect that the Fed is always monitoring financial conditions and that there has been some tightening in recent months. He will likely want to avoid the type of phrasing he used in the July press conference that led to market participants questioning the Fed’s willingness to act. In short, he will likely make clear that tightening FCIs are not a substitute for policy rate increases. Lastly, Warsh could provide a brief progress report on the various task forces. Recall that he announced last week at his IMF appearance that “Among the external task forces that I initiated about four or five months ago is one on productivity and jobs. We are going to have our first field hearing of a task force on 16 November in the Bay Area, where we are going to wrestle with these issues.” Though [the author doubts] Warsh will provide an in-depth detailed preview of the conclusions of any of the task forces, [Chair Warsh] may flag other task force meeting dates or add some broad color around the progression of the discussions. This week’s data docket should reinforce the hawkish tone coming out of Wednesday’s FOMC meeting, Tuesday’s headline and core durable goods orders should remain sturdy on the back of aircraft orders as well as AI-driven capex. More importantly for monetary policymakers will be Thursday’s personal income and spending report, which will include the latest reading on the core PCE deflator, the Fed’s preferred inflation metric. Recall that the stronger-than-expected CPI and PPI prints from a couple of weeks ago resulted in [the author's] tracking for September’s core PCE print rising to +0.36%. Absent any revisions, [September’s core PCE print] would correspond to a year-over-year rate of 3.14%. However, there did seem to be some downward revisions evident in August’s PPI readings for healthcare and airfares, which should help to prevent the year-over-year rate from rounding up to 3.2%. Note, also, that the PCE inflation data will be released alongside the advance Q3 real GDP report which should show inflation-adjusted output growth of 3.3% annualized. Lastly, Fed officials will no doubt monitor Friday’s Q3 employment cost index (ECI) for any signs of increasing wage pressures in the labor market. As a reminder, the [employment cost index] is a more comprehensive measure of labor costs that captures non-wage compensation such as benefits. The fixed sector weights in the [employment cost index] also allow for a cleaner read on underlying wage trends than average hourly earnings in the monthly employment report. A meaningful upside surprise in the [employment cost index] could cause the Fed to question [the Fed's] long-held view that the labor market is not currently a source of inflationary pressures. In summary, the Fed is likely to maintain a hawkish outlook despite not raising rates this week. This week’s slate of data releases should reinforce [the messaging that the Fed is likely to maintain a hawkish outlook despite not raising rates this week].
