[The author discusses] Warsh’s recent views on the economic outlook, interest rate policy, the Fed’s balance sheet, and financial regulation below. Economic outlook Warsh has made a few comments on the evolving balance of risks facing the US economy over the last two years. In the fall of 2024, [Kevin Warsh] opposed interest rate cuts because [Kevin Warsh] worried that the FOMC risked declaring victory over inflation prematurely. In 2025, [Kevin Warsh] argued that the Trump administration’s deregulatory policies and potential spending cuts would be disinflationary enough to outweigh any one-time effect of tariffs on prices, which the Fed could look through anyway. More recently, [Kevin Warsh] said that the US economy “could be at the front end of a productivity boom” driven by artificial intelligence (AI). As a result, [Kevin Warsh] said, “We are probably in the early innings of a structural decline in prices. [artificial intelligence] is going to make almost everything cost less.” Because [Kevin Warsh] thinks that “[artificial intelligence] will be a significant disinflationary force,” [Kevin Warsh] has argued that the Fed should not keep interest rates high solely in response to strong GDP growth. Instead, [Kevin Warsh] said, the FOMC should imitate the approach taken under the leadership of former Chairman Alan Greenspan in the 1990s, when strong GDP growth was powered by strong productivity growth that was “structurally disinflationary.” Interest rate policy Warsh’s less concerned take on the inflation picture might position [Kevin Warsh] on the dovish side of the FOMC’s current policy debate. Warsh has not made many comments recently on the state of the labor market and whether the recent softening provided a strong case for the Fed’s latest risk management cuts. Many of Warsh’s recent comments on interest rate policy have been tied to [Kevin Warsh's] views on balance sheet policy, discussed below. [Kevin Warsh] argues that the Fed should lower interest rates while also shrinking [the Federal Reserve's] balance sheet so that the two policies have offsetting effects on financial conditions, limiting the impact on inflation. [Kevin Warsh] argues that under the current policy mix, “Wall Street is doing great, but Main Street is not doing as well,” and that cutting interest rates will “support households and small and medium-size businesses.” The Fed’s balance sheet Warsh’s views likely differ the most from those of current Fed officials on the topic of balance sheet policy. [Kevin Warsh] opposed the Fed’s QE2 program and resigned from the Fed after [the Fed’s QE2 program] was announced. Since then, [Kevin Warsh] has been a frequent critic of the Fed’s balance sheet, noting that [the Fed’s balance sheet] remains far larger than [the Fed’s balance sheet] was before the 2008 financial crisis, and [Kevin Warsh] continued to call for shrinking [the Fed’s balance sheet] over the last year. Warsh has offered several arguments for reducing [the Fed’s balance sheet]. First, that the Fed has too large of a role in financial markets and should aim to stay out of asset markets outside of crises. Second, that past asset purchase programs caused a “misallocation of capital” by “redirecting capital from the real domestic economy to financial assets” and have “been a material part of the inequality story.” Third, that the larger balance sheet contributes to higher inflation. And fourth, that the Fed’s asset holdings have “subsidized” the government’s financing costs, enabling fiscal policymakers to run larger deficits. Most Fed policymakers and members of the Fed staff would likely take a different view on [Warsh's arguments for reducing the balance sheet]. [Most Fed policymakers and members of the Fed staff] see the growth of [the Fed’s balance sheet] relative to the size of the economy as a necessary consequence of faster growth in demand for the Fed’s liabilities and the switch to an ample reserves framework for implementing monetary policy. [The current ample reserves framework] has broad support within the Federal Reserve, and Chair Powell gave a speech just a few months ago to explain [the current ample reserves framework's] advantages. As a result, [the author] would not expect a major reduction in the size of the Fed’s balance sheet that would require reverting to the old framework for implementing monetary policy if Warsh is confirmed as Chair. However, steps to reduce regulatory requirements on banks could reduce [banks'] demand for reserves at the Fed, which could allow the Fed to maintain a somewhat smaller balance sheet under [the current ample reserves framework]. Most such changes, such as changes to the Liquidity Coverage Ratio or the Net Stable Funding Ratio, would require support from the majority of the Board of Governors and in some cases from other federal banking agencies. But changes to the softer and more informal guidance that bank supervisors have sometimes provided about how banks satisfy high-quality liquid assets (HQLA) requirements, with reserves or other assets, could occur without a formal vote. [Kevin Warsh] argued recently that [the Fed’s approach to financial regulation] imposes excessive compliance costs on banks and has “systematically disadvantaged small and medium-sized banks.” In [Kevin Warsh's] view, small and medium-sized banks face too much regulatory uncertainty about whether [small and medium-sized banks'] mergers will be approved, and regulators should instead “say we’re open for business, for consolidation among small and medium-sized banks.” negotiations on bank regulatory and supervisory standards with [the Federal Reserve's] global counterparts within the Basel framework. [Kevin Warsh] recently said that “Fed leaders have tried to bind US banks to a complicated, vaunted set of rules in the name of global regulatory convergence,” but argued that “the Basel endgame isn’t America’s endgame.” [Kevin Warsh] called for “a new, reformed American regulatory regime” that would “make the US the best place for the world’s banks to do business.” Late last year, Warsh praised the “good early work” undertaken recently by Vice Chair for Supervision Michelle Bowman in “crafting a new regulatory framework.” [and] Co.
