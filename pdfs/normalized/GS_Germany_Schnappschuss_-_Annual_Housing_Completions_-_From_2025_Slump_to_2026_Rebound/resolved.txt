# Germany Schnappschuss - Annual Housing Completions - From 2025 Slump to 2026 Rebound

22 June 2026 Deutsche Bank Research Europe Germany Economics Germany Schnappschuss Date

Residential Construction: Strongly Supply-Side Constrained In 2024, residential completions exceeded even [the author's] already optimistic forecast (forecast: 240,000; official data: 252,000). However, 2025 marked a significant setback, with the housing market experiencing a sharp downturn. Only 207,000 dwellings were completed, down 18% year on year and the lowest level since 2012. [The 207,000 dwellings completed] was clearly below [the author's] expectation of 250,000. 2025: Construction Backlog Stagnated Against Expectations The biggest surprise was that the construction backlog—defined as approved but not yet completed dwellings—remained broadly unchanged at around 760,000 units at the end of 2025, similar to the level recorded in 2024. Relative to Germany’s existing housing stock, [the construction backlog] stood at 1.8% in 2025, whereas a “natural” backlog, measured by the historical median, typically lies slightly below 1%. [The author] had expected [the construction backlog] to decline by 23,000 dwellings in 2025. [The assessment of the construction backlog to decline by 23,000 dwellings in 2025] was based on relatively low capacity utilization, which fell from above 80% before the interest rate shock in 2022 to well below 70% in 2025 and remained around that level in 2026. In principle, [relatively low capacity utilization] should have freed up additional capacity to absorb new demand. On the supply side, labour shortages and bureaucratic constraints may help explain why [the construction backlog] remained broadly unchanged. On the demand side, some projects may still be in the pipeline, with developers waiting for a more supportive investment environment. Interestingly, construction backlogs vary substantially across the German states. Brandenburg, Berlin, and Bavaria have particularly high ratios—at around 2.5% relative to the total housing stock. [Brandenburg, Berlin, and Bavaria] are home to strong economic centres, including Berlin [or] Potsdam and Munich. By contrast, the only states with comparatively low backlogs, close to the natural level (below 1%), are Saarland, Saxony-Anhalt, and Thuringia. [Saarland, Saxony-Anhalt, and Thuringia] share several common characteristics: [Saarland, Saxony-Anhalt, and Thuringia] are small, lack a major urban centre, and, above all, have shrinking populations. As a result, weak demand appears to matter more than a more elastic supply side due to an efficient bureaucracy and many workers. In 2025, the number of buildings already under construction fell to 307,000, down from 330,000 in 2024. At the same time, a record 36,000 building permits expired, the highest level since 2002 (2024: 29,000; before 2024, the long-term annual average was around 20,000). In addition, the average time from permit approval to completion for new residential buildings lengthened further, reaching 27 months in 2025. Moreover, recently higher short-term and long-term interest rates are also an important factor. Despite these challenges, there are also some positive developments. The number of building permits are increasing. In 2025, a year-on-year increase of 10% was recorded. Data for the first quarter of 2026 points to further improvement, and [the author expects] another 14% increase, bringing the total to 260,000 in 2026. Subsidies for social housing and the Growth Opportunities Act—which is highly attractive but still not widely used—are providing important impetus for the residential sector. [The author also sees] a sector that is markedly more innovative than in the past, particularly relative to the complacency that prevailed during the ultra-low interest rate environment up to Q1 2022. In [the author's] assessment, modular construction represents a key lever to materially improve productivity. In [modular construction], not only individual components are manufactured industrially, but entire rooms—including fittings, furniture, and electronics—are produced off-site. [Modular construction] can significantly increase the degree of prefabrication and, in [the author's] view, could prove critical to boosting productivity and reducing construction costs. [The author's] core forecasting models remain based on building permits and the construction backlog. [The author forecasts] suggest that completions will total 240,000 in 2026 and 260,000 in 2027, down from [the author's] previous numbers of 270,000 and 290,000, respectively. [The author assumes] that the construction backlog will decline to 750,000 in 2026 and 740,000 in 2027. Modular construction is not separately identified in official statistics, but is captured under prefabricated buildings. The number of prefabricated new dwellings has been rising, with [prefabricated new dwellings'] share increasing from 9.1% in 2015 to 13.8% in 2025. Following [the interest rate shock in 2022], [the share of prefabricated new dwellings] has risen by around 0.7pp per year. [The author expects] [the trend that the share of prefabricated new dwellings has risen by around 0.7pp per year] to continue, reaching 14.5% in 2026 and more than 15% in 2027. [That this trend to continue, reaching 14.5% in 2026 and more than 15% in 2027] would imply 31,000 prefabricated new units completed in 2026 and 35,000 in 2027—a new record high. The data also point to clear regional differences, suggesting that [the sharp downturn] has not been uniform across Germany. In eastern Germany, the number of completed new residential buildings fell by 34% in 2025, roughly twice the 17% decline recorded in the West. Both developments are fully consistent with [the author's] analysis and forecasts until 2040. The demographic change will render real estate investments in many areas riskier than in the past.
