# US Economic Perspectives Fed officials signal patience

The prevailing message from today's Fed commentary was patience. Federal funds futures implied odds of an October rate hike pared back to 28% as of this writing. Officials broadly reiterated that inflation remains too high and that upside inflation risks persist but [Fed officials] did not show urgency to tighten policy further in the near term. Rather, policymakers emphasized the need to assess incoming data. Vice Chair Jefferson said the FOMC may need more time before determining [the FOMC's] next move, Vice Chair for Supervision Bowman said [Vice Chair for Supervision Bowman] does not currently see an urgent need for further action. Governor Cook did not comment directly on the near-term path for rates but noted that supply shocks have become more " salient" and [supply shocks'] effects " surprisingly persistent," complicating the conventional wisdom that central banks should look through supply shocks. Other speakers, such as regional presidents Kashkari, Collins, Schmid and Barkin, emphasized that inflation remains too high and stressed the importance of returning to the FOMC target, but stopped short of signaling urgency to hike imminently. Jefferson suggests FOMC may need more time to consider next move In remarks this afternoon at the University of Virginia, Vice Chair Jefferson suggested that it may take more time to determine "any future adjustments in policy" (link here). "My colleagues and I will need to come to our own judgment, which may take more time," [Vice Chair Jefferson] said. " I will continue to assess whether underlying trends suggest that inflation will return to target with sufficient speed." Jefferson noted that [Vice Chair Jefferson] supported the Fed's September hike and saw [the Fed's September hike] as " appropriate policy " to ensure long-term inflation expectations remain well anchored. "S ince our September meeting, yields across the term structure have increased further, a sign that investors are reassessing the evolving macroeconomic landscape," [Vice Chair Jefferson] noted. Vice Chair Jefferson acknowledged that inflation has moderated from [inflation's] pandemic highs but emphasized that headline PCE inflation remains too high at 3.4% and has exceeded the Fed's 2 percent target for more than five years. "As my base case, I expect inflation to be elevated in the short run before resuming its decline toward our 2 percent goal as the effects of energy and other price shocks fade," [Vice Chair Jefferson] noted. "I view risks to my inflation forecast as tilted to the upside due to recent geopolitical developments and stronger-than-anticipated aggregate demand." [Vice Chair Jefferson] described economic activity and labor market conditions as " broadly solid " with "roughly balanced" risks, though [Vice Chair Jefferson] noted " I continue, however, to hear anecdotal evidence of elevated price sensitivity and softening consumer demand, especially for low-income consumers." Bowman doesn't "currently see an urgent need for further action" In the Q&A of an event on modernizing financial regulation, Vice Chair for Supervision Bowman stated that [Vice Chair for Supervision Bowman] didn't currently see an urgent need for future action (link here). "Inflation remains above our target. But after the Committee's action in September, what we need to have is a better understanding of how the underlying trends in the economy and how financial conditions are evolving going forward. So I don't currently see an urgent need for further action. And I think we need to better understand the totality of the data, but remain attentive to the risks," [Vice Chair for Supervision Bowman] said. In prepared remarks (link here), Bowman commented on the impact of relaxing large bank capital requirements under the enhanced supplementary leverage ratio (eSLR) since November 2025. Bowman argued, "Leverage ratio reforms have improved Treasury market functioning and strengthened its resilience to stress by relaxing regulatory balance sheet constraints." Another piece of broader capital overhaul includes the Basel III proposal introduced in March, and Bowman expects " to have it finalized before the end of the year" (link here). Cook says supply shocks have become more frequent and more persistent FRB of New York President Williams moderated a central banking panel this afternoon with Governor Cook. Cook did not offer a specific near-term path for rates, but acknowledged that "inflation has been too high for too long" and warned that supply shocks have become both more frequent and more persistent. [Governor Cook] noted that in [Governor Cook's] view, the appropriate policy response to a shock depends on the shock's source and broader effects. "Our conventional view used to be that we would look through these supply shocks because tighter monetary policy is not going to have an effect on oil prices, not going to have an effect on a war ... Now it is possibly the case that the optimal policy response could have different predictions depending on many different factors," [Governor Cook] said. [Governor Cook] also stressed the importance of well-anchored inflation expectations. "We just want to make sure that inflation expectations don't become unanchored," [Governor Cook] said. " And that's central, I think — a guiding principle in our decision-making." Williams was primarily the interviewer, but [FRB of New York President Williams] agreed with that framing, noting, " There isn't one answer to how to respond to a supply shock." [FRB of New York President Williams] also described inflation expectations as "absolutely foundational to how we think about this." Cook also noted that policymakers must account for the delays in the information [policymakers] receive and the effects of [policymakers'] decisions. " Our actions also peter out through the economy with a lag. So you have to weigh this when you're considering a change in interest rates," [Governor Cook] said. Williams also reinforced that judgment-based approach, noting that the FOMC must determine "how do we make sense of the data" and that policy is "not going to follow the Taylor rule mechanically no matter what." Cook's inflation outlook was relatively cautious, and [Governor Cook] warned that the AI infrastructure build-out is already producing pockets of price pressures. Although [Governor Cook] noted that [Governor Cook] expects AI eventually to deliver productivity gains and disinflationary effects, [Governor Cook] also said that the timing is uncertain: "The AI build-out is potentially creating inflationary pressures that may not resolve very quickly. So I think this is one of the main things that concerns me right now for 2027." [Governor Cook] also noted, "I worry about when the productivity gains that would produce disinflation will come, and where the supply bottlenecks are going to be next." Kashkari "jotted down" two 2026 hikes in [Summary of Economic Projections] In a Council of Foreign Relations event last night, FRB of Minneapolis President Kashkari restated [FRB of Minneapolis President Kashkari's] view that "inflation is still too high" after yesterday's PCE prices release (link here). "There are many different measures of inflation, but it's running at around a 3% rate," Kashkari said. "It's been elevated now for more than five years. I didn't think the inflation data today really changed that story for me very much." Kashkari also revealed his that [FRB of Minneapolis President Kashkari's] September Summary of Economic Projections (SEP) federal funds rate "dot" assumed one more rate hike " later on this year.""I jotted down two hikes this year, and these are jotting down in pencil," [FRB of Minneapolis President Kashkari] said. "This is as of September, I had jotted down... two hikes for this year, one in September and then one later on this year." On Bloomberg this morning (link here), Kashkari noted, "We will do what we need to do to get inflation back down to target. The question is how high rates have to go. I don't know the answer to that. You were both talking about the massive demand for investment capital. I think about that in the context of the neutral rate. It is set by the balance of savings and investment in the economy. Investment capital has to come from savings, and if demand for capital is high, the clearing price rises and interest rates go higher." [FRB of Minneapolis President Kashkari] continued, "If AI proves to be as productive as people hope it will be, then this investment cycle could go on for a long time." Collins, Schmid, and Barkin emphasize inflation risks FRB of Boston President Collins, FRB of Kansas City President Schmid, and FRB of Richmond President Barkin participated in a panel today at the FRB of Richmond's Investing in Rural America conference in Asheville, NC. All three said that [Collins, Schmid, and Barkin] supported the FOMC's September hike and emphasized that inflation has been too high for too long. Collins noted, "The data that we see — there are some promising pieces, but there are some parts that aren't as promising, and I see more risks on the inflation side ... And so with the labor market on relatively solid footing, monetary policy can focus on ensuring that we restore in a timely way, sustainable, durable, 2 percent inflation." Schmid argued that policymakers need to have a " laser focus" on the implications of elevated inflation, noting, "We just haven't fulfilled our promise on the inflation side [of the mandate.]" All three officials did not specify how [the views on inflation that inflation has been too high for too long] might impact the cadence or extent of additional policy tightening. Barkin noted that doing so would amount to forward guidance, from which the FOMC has " made clear " [the FOMC] is moving away. Waller speaks on economic data in the age of AI Governor Waller's speech today did not touch on the economic outlook or monetary policy, but rather focused on the future of economic data in the age of artificial intelligence (link here). [Governor Waller] highlighted the evolution of Federal Reserve Economic Data (FRED) into a global public resource with more than 850K data series and 18 million users, arguing that [Federal Reserve Economic Data (FRED)'s] value rests on being a transparent and reliable source of information. Waller emphasized that AI creates opportunities by making data easier to access, analyze, and explain, but also introduces risks such as hallucinations, bias, misattribution, and erroneous interpretations. To address [risks such as hallucinations, bias, misattribution, and erroneous interpretations], [Governor Waller] announced new initiatives including the [Federal Reserve Economic Data] Model Context Protocol (MCP) Connector, which allows AI systems to access [Federal Reserve Economic Data] data more accurately and reliably.
