# US Money Markets [Treasury General Account] into repo

At the QRA this week, [Treasury Borrowing Advisory Committee] discussed the feasibility of parking extra cash in the repo market to earn a return. While direct returns might be low, extra cash in repo could smooth funding markets. [The author has] been recommending 1y1yf SOFR [or] FF wideners and continue to do so.

- At this week’s refunding meeting, Treasury asked [Treasury Borrowing Advisory Committee] to explore investing excess [Treasury General Account] cash in the repo market. The idea of running such a program has received increased attention as the [Treasury General Account] has risen to ever-higher levels, and as a possible way to allow the Fed to run a smaller balance sheet.
- Treasury’s objective looks to be earning excess return by investing that cash in repo, but the direct returns from running such a program are likely marginal, and dependent on the level of repo rates relative to the interest rate the Fed pays on reserve balances.
- One indirect consequence, however, would be suppressing repo pressures, as the Treasury may end up only deploying that cash when repo moves above IORB, smoothing out funding markets. [Suppressing repo pressures] would support SOFR [or] FF wideners, which [the author has] been recommending at the 1y1yf point (current -4.0bp, entry -5.5 bp).
- Smoother funding would indirectly support swap spreads, which helps with Treasury’s issuance costs further out the curve. [That Smoother funding would indirectly support swap spreads] is another cost benefit to Treasury.

[Treasury Borrowing Advisory Committee] exploring placing excess cash into repo markets At this week’s quarterly refunding meeting, Treasury asked the Treasury Borrowing Advisory Committee (TBAC) to explore the feasibility of investing excess cash held in the Treasury General Account (TGA) into the Treasury repo market when balances exceed Treasury’s minimum cash requirement. The issue has gained prominence as Treasury’s target [Treasury General Account] balance has moved steadily higher in recent years and as the [Treasury General Account]—now one of the Federal Reserve’s largest liabilities—has increasingly featured in discussions about potential ways to reduce the size of the Fed’s balance sheet. Treasury’s cash management policy requires holding enough cash on hand to meet roughly one week of obligations in the event of a loss of market access. In practice, [Treasury’s cash management policy] means holding sufficient balances to cover all gross maturing debt as well as net fiscal outflows. Over the past year, that policy has at times implied [Treasury General Account] balances in excess of $1tn. Treasury primarily builds the [Treasury General Account] through bill issuance, but because bill auction sizes are adjusted only gradually, issuance must often occur well in advance of anticipated cash needs. [That issuance must often occur well in advance of anticipated cash needs] can result in periods when [Treasury General Account] balances temporarily exceed the minimum implied by the cash management policy, leaving Treasury with excess cash. [Treasury Borrowing Advisory Committee] was therefore asked to explore whether [excess Treasury General Account cash] could be placed into the repo market, reflecting Treasury’s broader effort to manage the [Treasury General Account] more efficiently given [the Treasury General Account's] larger structural size. The growing focus on [Treasury General Account] management also reflects [the Treasury General Account's] role on the Federal Reserve’s balance sheet. Alongside currency in circulation and reserve balances, the [Treasury General Account] is one of the Fed’s largest liabilities. As policymakers and market participants continue to debate the appropriate size of the Fed’s balance sheet, improved [Treasury General Account] management is increasingly viewed as a potential avenue through which the Fed could operate with a smaller balance sheet, all else equal. Large and rapid swings in the [Treasury General Account]—particularly around tax payment dates—also introduce volatility into reserve balances, since increases in the [Treasury General Account] drain reserves from the banking system. [That Large and rapid swings in the Treasury General Account—particularly around tax payment dates—also introduce volatility into reserve balances] complicates reserve management for the Fed under the ample reserves framework. For example, this past April the [Treasury General Account] increased from roughly $700bn to over $1tn in the span of about a week. While no funding pressures emerged as reserves were drained, [the dynamic that Large and rapid swings in the Treasury General Account—particularly around tax payment dates—also introduce volatility into reserve balances] was a concern for the Fed last December and one factor behind [the Fed] initiating [the Fed's] RMP program at an elevated $40bn per month pace. Against that backdrop, several themes emerged from the [Treasury Borrowing Advisory Committee] discussion. The first is that the amount of excess [Treasury General Account] cash available to invest is subject to large swings. Some days Treasury might have hundreds of billions of excess cash, other days [Treasury] might have none. Moving that excess cash in and out of repo means that repo markets would need to absorb potentially large and abrupt swings in Treasury cash flows. For [the reason that repo markets would need to absorb potentially large and abrupt swings in Treasury cash flows], [Treasury Borrowing Advisory Committee] noted that such an excess cash repo investment program would likely need to be capped. But that return is likely to be modest at best, and more so if [the excess cash repo investment program] is capped. Moving cash from the [Treasury General Account] into repo increases reserve balances one-for-one, and those reserves earn interest from the Fed (IORB). From a consolidated balance sheet perspective—Treasury plus the Fed— Treasury would earn the tri-party repo rate (TGCR) while the Fed pays IORB. Over the past several years, TGCR has generally traded below IORB, with only brief periods—most notably in Q4 2025—when repo rates exceeded IORB. Moreover, under the ample reserves framework, the Fed generally aims to keep money market rates close to IORB, implying that any sustained spread between TGCR and IORB is likely to be thin or negative. In other words, the returns would not only be small but only exist in certain repo environments, such as when reserves conditions tighten (for instance as the [Treasury General Account] rises). But [that the returns would not only be small but only exist in certain repo environments, such as when reserves conditions tighten] presents an opportunity for Treasury to deploy that excess cash into repo whenever repo rates move over IORB, counteracting the effects of a rising [Treasury General Account]. [That the Treasury may end up only deploying that cash when repo moves above IORB] would make the Treasury a notable marginal cash investor in potentially large size, likely helping suppress funding market pressures. Smooth funding markets likely benefit Treasury in other ways, such as supporting swap spreads, and in turn reducing the cost of issuance further out the curve. Such a program would support SOFR [or] FF wideners, which [the author has] been recommending at the 1y1yf point (current -4.0bp, entry -5.5 bp) While not a stated goal in the Treasury discussions, [the author thinks] another attraction of such a framework is that [the excess cash repo investment program] could reduce the minimum required [Treasury General Account] balances if structured correctly, potentially allowing the Fed to operate with a somewhat smaller balance sheet. But if Treasury structures [the excess cash repo investment program] as only investing excess cash, [the excess cash repo investment program] is unlikely to reduce the peak [Treasury General Account] levels driven by the cash management policy. For instance, in the QRA Treasury noted [Treasury’s cash management policy] indicates that [Treasury] needs to have $1tn on hand at the end of July to meet [Treasury's] outflow needs, and when the [Treasury General Account] reaches that level their would be no excess to invest. As a result, simply investing “excess” cash above the minimum requirement would do little to support a structurally smaller Fed balance sheet. [Treasury Borrowing Advisory Committee] discussed an alternative framework in which Treasury would hold only three days of projected outflows in the [Treasury General Account], with days four and five placed into the repo market. Outflows associated with days four and five can be sizable as well, particularly around coupon settlements, suggesting that [the alternative framework in which Treasury would hold only three days of projected outflows in the Treasury General Account] would also need to be capped to avoid large and abrupt swings in the amount the Treasury is investing. But importantly, designing [the excess cash repo investment program] [the way that Treasury would hold only three days of projected outflows in the Treasury General Account, with days four and five placed into the repo market] would would give Treasury an available cash balance to deploy even when the required minimum is very high, meaning that Treasury can move into repo as the balance peaks and the drain on reserves is extreme. [That Treasury can move into repo as the balance peaks and the drain on reserves is extreme] would give [Treasury] meaningful capacity to counteract the effects of a rising [Treasury General Account] on repo markets directly. In short, if the objective is to earn a return on [excess Treasury General Account cash], that direct return is likely marginal and dependent on the repo environment. But structuring [the excess cash repo investment program] [the way that Treasury would hold only three days of projected outflows in the Treasury General Account, with days four and five placed into the repo market] could could have indirect benefits by supporting swap spreads and lead to lower issuance costs for the Treasury. And while not a stated goal of [the excess cash repo investment program] as outlined, [the excess cash repo investment program] would be effective at reducing peak [Treasury General Account] balances and, in turn, alleviating some need for the Fed to run a larger balance sheet—the extent of which would depend on how the program is structured and the level of any caps.
