# US Economic Notes - What you need to know for the week ahead

Economics US Economic Notes Date 7 June 2026

Post CPI Conference Call: Wednesday Jun 10, 9:00 am EST PROD/PROD0000000000629104.link Commentary for Monday: With more positive news on the labor market last Friday, both Fed officials and market participants will focus on the other side of the dual mandate, inflation. As [the author] discussed in [the author's] recap (see “May employment: Mayday moment for rate hikes?”) the May employment report marked a trifecta outperformance on headline (+172k vs. +179k) and private (+120k vs. +177k) payroll gains relative to consensus expectations. The 93k in net upward revisions also contributed to the strength of the latest establishment survey data. Though outsized increases in leisure [or] hospitality (+70k vs. +30k) and local government (+55k vs. +6k) hiring comprised most of the May payroll outperformance, it is important to note that job gains continued to broaden across sectors w ith the three-month diffusion index (53.8 vs. 53.2) now at [the three-month diffusion index's] highest level since March 2024 (56.2). The household survey reinforced the narrative of labor market stability with the unemployment rate (4.296% vs. 4.337%) essentially unchanged over the last three months. In addition, the U -6 rate declined by a tenth to 8.1%. Importantly, household employmen t (+129k) rebounded as unemployment declined (-66k) thus keeping the labor force participation rate steady. In short, there was very little to disparage in the latest labor market data and at minimum Fed officials are likely to feel increasingly confident that employment has stabilized. To be sure, rising confidence in the labor market outlook does not extend to the inflation side of the mandate where pressures are mounting. Against [the backdrop that rising confidence in the labor market outlook does not extend to the inflation side of the mandate where pressures are mounting], the focus quickly shifts to Wednesday’s CPI and Thursday’s PPI releases, which will provide an update on the fallout from the latest energy supply shock. Regarding [Wednesday’s CPI], another large increase in gas prices (+6.8% seasonally adjusted) should boost headline CPI (+0.51% forecast vs. +0.64% previous) relative to core (+0.22% vs. +0.38%). Should [the author's] forecasts hit the mark, year-over-year headline CPI would rise to 4.29% from 3.81%, while core would edge up to 2.87% from 2.75%. (see “Webinar: May CPI preview [and] webinar registration). At the subcomponent level, we are looking for continued tariff-related price pressures in apparel as well as strength in information technology commodities, while lagged wholesale prices could boost used car prices. On the services side, rents should normalize after payback in April from last October’s government shutdown. We will also be looking for the extent of any Matthew Luzzetti, Ph.D. bleed-through from higher gasoline prices into core, particularly from airline fares and delivery services. Given our projections for CPI and categories that the BEA takes from the PPI, we are ex ante tracking +0.33% for May core PCE, which would increase year-over-year a tenth to 3.39%. Thursday’s PPI will help to refine [+0.33% for May core PCE] – particularly, the health care services, domestic airfares and portfolio management. More broadly, [Wednesday’s CPI] will be interpreted through the lens of underlying inflation momentum, which, as we noted in “US Economic Perspectives: Sideways trend for inflation, uptrend in rates?”, continues to show limited progress toward target with median and mean estimates still clustered around the low 3% range. [The limited progress toward target] is consistent with [the author's] broader thematic work highlighting that inflation pressures remain unusually broad-based across goods and services and are not confined to a narrow set of categories, limiting the extent to which the Fed can rely on passive disinflation (see “What’s keeping inflation above 2%? Almost everything”). In short, while [the author's] baseline remains that the Fed is on hold near neutral indefinitely (see “World Outlook: 1999 meets 1990 “), hawkish-leaning officials such as Dallas’s Logan and Cleveland’s Hammack have been highlighting the possibility that monetary tightening may be required if current trends persist. Indeed, as inflation risks rise and labor market risks recede, Fed policy may have become overinsured against downside risks to the labor market and prospects for future rate hikes might best be interpreted as a prudent reversal of this insurance as the balance of risks tilts towards inflation (see “Fed Notes: Overinsured?”). [The sentiment that monetary tightening may be required if current trends persist] seems to be shared by consumers already (see “Consumers think the Fed might hike rates”).
