German sovereign rating risk - agencies and federal states likely to outperform Bunds According to German state TV news (link), and other media outlets, referring to Ifo president Clemens Fuest, there are concerns regarding the German sovereign rating due to strongly increasing interest payments. For example, interest payments will more than double from EUR 30.2bn in 2026 and EUR 41.8bn in 2027 to EUR 81bn in 2030. At this stage, all German sovereign ratings are at triple A stable, with rating reports are available under this link. Given the explicit government guarantees, the ratings of KfW and Rentenbank are fully linked to the rating of Germany. Moreover, at least in the case of Fitch and Scope, the ratings of federal states are also fully linked to the rating of Germany. Moreover, also in the case of S&P and Moody's, the rating of Germany is a crucial driver of the ratings of federal states. On 17 March 2026, Moody's highlighted that "Germany's Aaa rating would come under downward pressure in a scenario of a significant and prolonged deterioration in [Germany's] economic strength. Such an erosion could result from a much slower implementation of the infrastructure and defense package, a substantial diversion of the fiscal stimulus toward government consumption, a lack of visible political commitment and progress in addressing Germany's main structural challenges that are holding back private investment, or from failure to begin tackling rising demographic challenges. A sharper-than-expected increase in the government debt burden that materially weakens debt affordability metrics, combined with weaker growth, would also have a negative impact on [Germany's Aaa rating]. While [the author's] baseline scenario does not assume a military confront ation between NATO and Russia, an escalation of the war in Ukraine into a conflict involving NATO members would exert downward pressure on [Germany's Aaa rating]. Despite the increasing debt-to-GDP ratio, the broadly doubled federal budget deficit, increasing interest payments and the increasing use of special funds seem clearly negative for the rating of Germany. Moreover, contingent liabilities, mainly due to the strong use of federal and federal state promotional banks were the highest in Germany in 2024 already (link and more details below). [The author] cannot help thinking that a downgrade of Germany is still unlikely, with a negative outlook or negative watch /review for downgrade probably the worst that could happen this year. However, in the e vent of negative rating actions regarding Germany, l ooking at 0% risk-weighted French agencies and French regions, German agencies and federal states are likely to outperform Bunds. While [the author believes] Bunds are likely to widen versus swaps in such a scenario, [the author expects] German agencies and federal states to widen also but to widen less. Germany has highest contingent liabilities among EU countries Based on Eurostat data, the level of liabilities of public corporations classified outside general government varied widely among the EU countries. Significant amounts of liabilities were recorded in Germany (84.4%), ahead of the Netherlands (73.1%), Luxem bourg (65%) and France (61.9%). By contrast, small amounts of public corporation liabilities were recorded in Slovakia (3.4%), Spain (4.3%), Cyprus (7.3%) and Romania (8.7%). The high level of liabilities in certain EU countries is primarily due to [the Eurostat data] including liabilities of public banks controlled by governments. Generally, financial institutions have significantly higher debt liabilities compared with non-financial corporations. At the same time, [financial institutions] have also significant amounts of financial assets, which are not captured in [the Eurostat data]. In [the author's] view, [that financial institutions have also significant amounts of financial assets, which are not captured in the Eurostat data] applies particularly to German promotional banks, which typically have strong balance sheets, high capital levels and strong track records regarding NPL, for example KfW, Rentenbank, NRW Bank and L-Bank. Canadian pension funds - [public sector entities] has a 20% risk weight LCR level 2A eligible In [the author's] view, Canadian pension funds are 20% risk weighted, LCR level 2A eligible and not 0% weighted (i.e. not preferred) under EU Solvency II. The same should apply to other non-EEA pension funds rated at least AA-on a second-best rating basis (Credit Quality Step 1 or CQS1) and having a link to the public sector. Assuming Canadian pension funds are viewed as public sector entities (PSE) in accordance with Art. 116 CRR, the question is whether [Canadian pension funds'] debt can be treated as an exposure to the sovereign (Article 116 (4) CRR, link). For [public sector entities] outside the EEA, [that Canadian pension funds' debt can be treated as an exposure to the sovereign] is only possible if the competent authority of a third country with equivalent supervisory confirms the equivalence of risk positions with sovereign exposure. Canadian Capital Adequacy Requirements (CAR) seem sufficient in [the respect that the competent authority of a third country with equivalent supervisory confirms the equivalence of risk positions with sovereign exposure], i.e. Section 4.1.2. allows for equal treatment of provinces and crown agents with the Canadian sovereign. However, on [the author's] understanding, Canadian pension funds are not crown agents. Hence, for Canadian pension funds, Article 116 (2) CRR seems to apply, referring Article 115(-1) CRR (link), in turn referring to the rating of the Canadian pension funds. As Canadian pension funds are rated CQS1 (i.e. at least AA-on the second-best-rating basis), a risk weight of 20% applies. There sems a risk that investors could argue that Canadian pension funds are not [public sector entities] but are corporates. However, also in [the case that Canadian pension funds are not public sector entities but are corporates], a 20% risk weight seems applicable based on Article 122

1. CRR (link), again due to being rated at CQS1. Overall, based on Article 116
2. CRR (PSE) or Article 122 CRR (corporates), a 20% seems to apply for Canadian pension funds. Bloomberg shows a 20% risk categorizing Canadian pension funds as [public sector entities]. Under the EU LCR Delegated Act (link), bonds issued by Canadian pension funds are Level 2A eligible based on Article 11 (1) (b) or Article 11 (1) (e) respectively – again depending on whether Canadian pension funds are categorized as [public sector entities] or corporates. In the case of the latter (which is not [the author's] view), additional criteria apply (e.g., the maximum time to maturity of the securities at the time of issuance is 10 years). Under EU Solvency II, bonds issued by Canadian pension funds are non-preferred (i.e. are not 0% capital weighted).
