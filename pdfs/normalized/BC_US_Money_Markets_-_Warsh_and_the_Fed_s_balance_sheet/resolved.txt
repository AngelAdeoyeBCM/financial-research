# US Money Markets Warsh and the Fed's balance sheet

A Warsh Fed is unlikely to move away from the Fed's "ample" reserves framework. But within [the Fed's "ample" reserves framework] [a Warsh Fed] does tip risks somewhat toward lower reserve balances and higher repo volatility. [A Warsh Fed] also likely means the Fed might lean toward a shorter portfolio WAM.. With Kevin Warsh looking likely to be the next chair of the Federal Reserve, one major question for judging front-end rates is how [Kevin Warsh's] views are likely to shape balance sheet policy. [The author] don't think [a Warsh Fed] represents a major shift at the Fed (see here) but Warsh is generally seen as quite hawkish on the balance sheet. For instance, he has argued that reducing the Fed's footprint would allow the Fed to cut interest rates further: "Run the printing press a little bit less. Let the balance sheet come down. Let Secretary Bessent handle the fiscal accounts, and in doing so, you can have materially lower interest rates. " This raises an important question: how can the Fed reduce the balance sheet beyond its current size, and what type of framework would that entail? [The author thinks] there would be very little support on the committee to move away from ample and back to scarce, but within [the Fed's "ample" reserves framework] there is a bit of wiggle room. Balance sheet background: where are we now? [The Fed's balance sheet] has been a major focus for Fed policy makers over the past few months as [the Fed] had been reducing [the Fed's] security holdings for several years as [the Fed] aimed to reach an "ample" level – enough reserves in the system so that swings in [the Fed's] nonreserve liabilities still keep enough reserves in the system so that [reserves] do not move into scarcity. In December, the Fed determined that reserve levels had reached "ample, " as funding pressures emerged in the fourth quarter and started a program to increase [the Fed's] holdings of T-bills to maintain [the "ample" level] over time. Why does the Fed hold so many assets? It's all about the liabilities. The Fed has several major categories of liabilities beyond reserves on [the Fed's] balance sheet, which are backed by assets such as UST coupons and increasingly T-bills. The Fed has limited control over many of [the major categories of liabilities beyond reserves], two of which are the TGA and currency in circulation. The TGA, which is Treasury's cash balance at the Fed, is set such that Treasury holds enough cash to fund itself for seven calendar days, assuming a loss of market access. That currently has [Treasury] targeting a quarter-end balance of $850bn. Currency in circulation has historically grown faster than the broader nominal economy. As banks and households demand for currency rises, the Fed issues more cash, and currently that total is about $2.4tn. That means the Fed needs over $3tn in securities alone before [the Fed] even creates $1 of bank reserves. FIGURE 1. Overdrafts were substantial pre-GFC... Banks use reserves to settle payment flows as well as to maintain appropriate liquidity buffers such as holding enough liquid assets to fund outflows under liquidity requirements such as the liquidity coverage ratio (LCR) as well as lowering net stable funding requirements. Fedwire funds service settles $4.5tn in payments on average each day, but there is no netting of [the $4.5tn in payments]. [That there is no netting of the $4.5tn in payments] means if banks have large payment needs and lower reserve balances, [banks] need to overdraw [banks'] reserve account to potentially settle [large payment needs]. The Fed issues banks credit to do [that banks need to overdraw banks' reserve account to potentially settle large payment needs] and to keep the payment system flowing, otherwise the gross settlements over Fedwire might seize up in a system with lower reserves. Since the Fed has operated with a large balance sheet, however, overdrafts have been negligible. For instance, prior to the GFC, the Fed's balance sheet would increase up to twenty-fold intraday, as [the Fed] extended credit to banks even on an unsecured basis. [That the Fed's balance sheet would increase up to twenty-fold intraday, as the Fed extended credit to banks even on an unsecured basis] created credit risks to the Fed, and in a sense the end-of-day snapshot of a small Fed balance sheet was largely a mirage. In contrast, daylight overdrafts are quite low now as a share of reserve balances, meaning banks have enough liquidity to settle payments flow without borrowing from the Fed. [The Fed's "ample" reserves framework] strikes [the author] as much more logical: banks hold more liquidity, intraday credit from the Fed is lower. It is no wonder that the Fed landed on [the Fed's "ample" reserves framework] rather than going back to the era of scarce reserves. [The author thinks] there would be very little support on the committee to move away from ample and back to scarce. So what might change on balance sheet policy? There is a bit of wiggle room within what the Fed defines as ample. What [what the Fed defines as ample] means in practice is likely on the order of a few hundred billion in reserves. [The author] talked about two camps at the Fed already, those that want enough reserves in the system all the time to accommodate even the largest swings in the TGA, and those that prefer a smaller balance sheet most of the time, with the Fed's repo backstop facility (SRP) providing marginal reserves when [the largest swings in the TGA] occur. A Fed chair that is aiming for a smaller balance sheet might find support for doing fewer reserve management purchases (RMPs) over time, and running repo pressures a touch higher at times, to achieve a lower balance sheet, which would be well within the bounds of the Fed's existing framework. [That a Fed chair that is aiming for a smaller balance sheet might find support for doing fewer reserve management purchases (RMPs) over time, and running repo pressures a touch higher at times, to achieve a lower balance sheet] would also give the Fed a better market-based read on where [the Fed] is on the demand curve. [The author has] already highlighted the risk that the pace of RMPs is lowered more post Q1 (see here), and [the author thinks] Warsh as Fed chair raises that prospect on the margins for the second half. The other lever that is still in play is the composition of the Fed's balance sheet. The prevailing thinking is either holding a "neutral portfolio" to match outstanding liabilities or a shorter duration portfolio. In either case, the Fed's portfolio, at about 9+ years, is much longer than outstanding liabilities, at 6 years. Waller has been a big proponent of matching the Fed's assets to [the Fed's] liabilities, which would reduce the risk of the Fed generating losses, as was the case in the past few years. Essentially [matching the Fed's assets to its liabilities] would mean backing reserves with T-bills, meaning the Fed is providing a liquidity transformation to banks rather than a maturity transformation. [The author suspects] that Warsh would lend more support to [matching the Fed's assets to its liabilities], meaning a shorter portfolio is more likely the landing spot for the long-run composition. There is no question the Fed needs to shorten [the Fed's] WAM in either framework; the question is the extent and how quickly [the Fed] chooses to get there. [The author] intend to watch closely how comments around both of these issues evolve in the coming months.
