# Fixed Income Blog - Examining the impact of central clearing on SOFR and repo

Rates Fixed Income Blog Date 30 March 2026

With the deadline for the SEC central clearing mandate of eligible UST repo trades a little over a year out (June 2027), regulators have been working to facilitate development of requisite infrastructure to ensure that the costs associated with the transition are minimized. Central clearing promises to reduce systemic risks and enhance dealer balance sheet efficiency, though not without raising specific industry concerns, including higher costs associated with clearing trades to a central counterparty (CCP), imposed on both the margin and capital fronts. The details around how [specific industry concerns] are addressed will determine the full impact on funding markets. In this note, [the author focuses] specifically on how [the shift to central clearing] might impact SOFR fixings within the Fed’s target range. Adding a new segment to SOFR The prevailing repo market structure continues to favor the non-centrally cleared bilateral repo (NCCBR) segment despite the benefits that central clearing offers, such as mitigated counterparty risks and balance sheet netting for dealers. [The preference that the prevailing repo market structure continues to favor the non-centrally cleared bilateral repo (NCCBR) segment] stems from the additional legal and operational costs associated with central clearing, alongside the required margins calculated via strict Value-at- Risk (VaR) models imposed by the Fixed Income Clearing Corporation (FICC). In contrast, non-cleared repo, particularly in the NCCBR segment, offers greater flexibility in terms, with the majority of transactions featuring no haircut and more discretionary margins. The NCCBR segment is relatively opaque and not captured by SOFR’s calculation, though [the NCCBR segment] currently makes up a significant proportion of dealers’ lending to hedge funds, most of which occurs at higher rates than the current SOFR distribution. The central clearing mandate is poised to integrate more activity in [the NCCBR segment] into the SOFR calculation, which will have the technical impact of biasing SOFR higher toward dealer-hedge fund client trades. The two key effects will be a wider SOFR-fed funds spread (SERFF more negative), and a compression of the GC - SOFR spread. To quantify the potential impact on SOFR, [the author uses] data from Office of Financial Research’s (OFR) 2022 NCCBR pilot collection (which captured data for three days in the span of two weeks in June 2022), acknowledging that funding market conditions are vastly different from back then. A research note published by the [Office of Financial Research’s] last year found that central clearing the NCCBR segment would not have shifted SOFR at all in 2022 due to exemptions for repo that would either not need to be cleared, such as those with non-Treasury collateral, or that would not be included in the SOFR calculation, such as term repo (SOFR is overnight only). To accurately assess the additional volume that would be incorporated into SOFR under a central clearing mandate in the current environment, [the author needs] to account for a higher percentage of eligible transactions out of the total NCCBR volume, which was about 9% in 2022, in addition to overall growth in the NCCBR segment. One way to dimension the additional volumes that will be captured by SOFR would be to exclude the inter-affiliate and central bank [or] government counterparties from the total volume when calculating the percentage of eligible volume, as [the inter-affiliate and central bank or government counterparties] are likely unaffected by the basis-trade-driven growth in leverage. [The author finds] that 17% of volume in N CCBR excluding inter-affiliate and central bank [or] government counterparties would be eligible to be added to SOFR, nearly doubling the share from 2022. [The 17%] can be thought of as an upper-bound estimate given that inter-affiliate and central bank [or] government counterparties are fully excluded in the calculation, but [the inter-affiliate and central bank or government counterparties] still represent a small proportion of total repos which if included, would slightly reduce the SOFR-eligible percentage. Applying [the 17%] to the latest estimate of NCCBR volumes ($5tn) yields roughly $850bn in additional volume to be included into SOFR. [The roughly $850bn in additional volume to be included into SOFR] is more than a four-fold increase from [Office of Financial Research’s]’s estimates from 2022 and exceeds the three-fold increase in overall SOFR volumes over this period. [The author notes] that [the roughly $850bn in additional volume to be included into SOFR] is consistent with both the growth of hedge fund repo borrowing and the growth of cash-futures basis trades as measured by leveraged funds’ net interest rate futures positions. Balance sheet benefits and dealer intermediation While the technical implementation of central clearing is likely to bias SOFR fixings higher, there are important offsetting factors to consider. One of [important offsetting factors to consider] is the impact on dealer intermediation capacity, with the [Office of Financial Research’s] estimating that central clearing would have netted over $200bn in repo positions in August last year. During periods of heightened demand for balance sheet netting, such as month - and quarter-ends, dealers push clients into centrally cleared repo activity. However, [that dealers push clients into centrally cleared repo activity] introduces RWA capital constraints for some banks, as the dominant sponsored central clearing service (in which dealers sponsor clients into FICC) requires dealers to guarantee the performance of the sponsored member. [RWA capital constraints for some banks] contribute to p ersistent intermediation challenges in sensitive periods within the current repo market structure. Figure 4: SOFR has tended to rise in days leading up to and coming out of month-ends The chart shows that in the days leading up to and coming out of reporting days, repo rates are pressured higher as foreign dealers step away from triparty borrowing. The GSIB surcharge proposal released this month could smooth some of the constraints introduced by [the behavior that foreign dealers step away from triparty borrowing] due to the STWF adjustments and removal of cliff effects improving willingness to expand balance sheets. The switch to averaging will also reduce the acute year-end pressures. Additionally, the Collateral-in-Lieu (CIL) service, approved in December 2025 by the SEC, has potential to generate more capacity to alleviate borrowing constraints. [The Collateral-in-Lieu (CIL) service] enables the CCP (FICC) to take a lien on the repo collateral instead of requiring a separate margin, thereby reducing overall margin costs. Furthermore, [the lien on the repo collateral] would obviate the need for [Fixed Income Clearing Corporation] to require dealers to guarantee the performance of the cash lender [dealers] are sponsoring, leading to a reduction in capital costs and freein g up RWA-constrained dealers to intermediate more repo. As more dealers onboard to [the Collateral-in-Lieu (CIL) service] over the next year and trading volumes in sponsored GC grow, [the author anticipates] gradual abatement of month - and quarter-end pressures. Should the aforementioned month-end intermediation challenges be resolved fully (SOFR-RRP spreads staying at the average levels between days 7 and 17), [the author estimates] a decline of approximately 1bp in monthly SOFR fixing averages, all else equal. Another potential effect of enhanced balance sheet capacity on SOFR is a possible shift of more activity into term repo as opposed to overnight, which would remove some of the new higher-rate lending that would otherwise be added to the SOFR calculation. While difficult to quantify, [the possible shift of more activity into term repo as opposed to overnight] represents another possible offset to the technical impact of including NCCBR transactions. Conclusion Based on available information, [the author anticipates] only a modest increase in SOFR, approximately 1 -2bps, as a result of the shift to central clearing given the offsetting effects of the mechanical rise from incorporating higher-rate repos and the reduction in i ntermediation and balance sheet constraints. There are a number of other relevant factors that could swing [the SEC central clearing mandate of eligible UST repo trades]’s impact on SOFR in either direction. For instance, whether the combination of cross - margining, ACS access model development, an d “done-away” development can effectively reduce the higher costs (both margin and operational) associated with central clearing[, could swing the SEC central clearing mandate of eligible UST repo trades’s impact on SOFR in either direction]. If costs are brought down, relative value trades would be more attractive, increasing leverage demand and consequently, funding rates. Conversely, ongoing liquidity and capital deregulation for large banks could stimulate increased bank lending into repo, which would keep a tighter lid on SOFR despite higher leverage demand. Finally, industry representatives have requested additional exemptions from central clearing for foreign financial institutions, and given that many hedge funds engaging in fixed-income relative value trades are domiciled in the Cayman Islands, such exemptions could materially reduce the volume of NCCBR activity subject to [the SEC central clearing mandate of eligible UST repo trades].
