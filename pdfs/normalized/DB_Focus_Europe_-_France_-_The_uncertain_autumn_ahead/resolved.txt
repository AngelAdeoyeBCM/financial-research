# Focus Europe - France - The uncertain autumn ahead

Economics Focus Europe Date 8 September 2026

As investors return from the summer, France faces a busy and potentially consequential autumn. The 10-year OAT-Bund spread, at around 85bp, reflects legitimate concerns about the fiscal trajectory and political uncertainty ahead of the 2027 presidential ra ce. Yet the picture is more nuanced than the focus on France’s vulnerabilities alone might suggest. The economy proved more resilient in H1 than headline GDP implies, although H2 looks harder. Fiscal execution has shown no major signs of slippage, but the arithmetic becomes considerably more challenging in 2027. And while the autumn budget carries genuine censure risk, [the author sees] a plausible route to adoption, not least because the incentives facing the RN may be changing as [the RN] gets closer to power. The first c atalyst comes on 27 September with an under-the-radar Senate election which, as [the author discusses] here and here, could constrain a future RN presidency. France also retains underappreciated strengths, including demographics and potential gains from defence and AI investment.

- **Real e conomy** – Better underneath than headlines indicate, for now. France underperformed the euro area in H1, with GDP falling 0.2% q/q in Q1 and stagnating in Q2. The composition was somewhat more encouraging in Q2: private consumption and net trade supported activity (real exports, while on a saw-toothed path, stood 3% higher than in Q2 2025), two notable developments in the middle of a significant global energy shock (see Figure 2). On the supply side, manufacturing and market services posted advances, although modest, while agriculture was heavily affected by this summer’s exceptional weather. The detail therefore suggests that surveys, particularly the PMIs, overstated the weakness in the core economy. For starters, the heatwaves became even more intense over the summer months, potentially dampening agricultural activity further – a shock that will never be captured by business surveys, focused on manufacturing and services. Gas prices have increased, with a potentially more adverse effect on production than oil. Besides, the indirect effect of higher energy prices should soon start to feed through to inflation, while tighter financing and credit conditions weigh on already-weak investment, especially residential. France and Europe are also benefiting relatively less from the immediate demand impulse associated with the global AI investment boom than the world average, as [the author] discussed here. - **Deficit risks** – 2027 remains the bigger concern. Budget execution through July remains broadly similar to last year and provides little evidence of major renewed fiscal slippage so far, following a 2025 deficit (5.1% of GDP) that undershot the initial target (5.4%), helped in part by proactive expenditure management. The government has communicated that the 2026 target (5% of GDP) will be difficult to reach, but [the author expects] any moderate overshoot to be predominantly cyclical, ie driven by lower activity:

The concern is 2027 rather than 2026: higher 2026 inflation will feed into indexed social expenditure next year, previous budget measures (for instance, the suspension of the 2023 pension reform) will continue to affect the accounts, and debt service, defence and healthcare generate persistent upward pressure. The environment for debt stabilisation has also become considerably less favourable: the gap between nominal growth and marginal sovereign funding costs is now the most adverse since the euro-area sovereign debt crisis, making debt stabilisation materially more demanding than [debt stabilisation] has been for much of the past decade (Figure 3, left). The comparison with Italy is increasingly uncomfortable: Italy still has a materially higher debt stock, but [Italy's] deficit and primary-balance dynamics have improved much more rapidly (Figure 3, right). 2027 budget: a narrow but viable path to adoption. Passing the 2027 budget will be difficult. The government lacks a majority, the presidential election is approaching and opposition parties have little incentive to accommodate the executive. With another agreement with the PS looking less likely this time around, attention turns towards the RN, whose MPs voted for censure at each stage when 49.3 was used on the 2026 budget. Censure is therefore a genuine risk. Yet, in [the author's] view, the outcome is less binary than the parliamentary arithmetic (no majority) suggests. First, a potential deficit target of 4.9% of GDP in 2027 points to limited consolidation rather than a sweeping fiscal tightening, reducing the political cost for an opposition party of allowing a budget to pass. Even [the potential deficit target of 4.9% of GDP in 2027] faces significant implementation risks; the fierce resistance triggered by early leaked proposals (such as the under-indexation of high pensions) suggesting the government will struggle to hold the line. Second, the RN has incentives of [the RN's] own to avoid a prolonged fiscal impasse. Another special law regime would leave whoever takes power in 2027 with little ability to redirect fiscal policy before the autumn. A full-blown budget – required if a special law is in place at the time of the election – would take months to assemble and pass, not to mention the fact that the deficit is expected to slip in [another special law regime] (Figure 4, left). Proximity to power may itself reinforce the incentive to reassure investors. The recently announced intention to anchor the programme around a 3% deficit – possibly even lower – “golden rule” arguably points in that direction. For a detailed analysis of what RN may or may not do should [the RN] reach power, see [the author's] recent series of notes (here, here, here and here). [That the RN has incentives of the RN's own to avoid a prolonged fiscal impasse] does not imply RN “support” for [the 2027 budget], but depending on the final package, the party could remain critical while ultimately declining to bring down the government – for example by abstaining on a motion of censure following Article 49.3. Officially, RN is waiting to see [the 2027 budget] to take a stance and will oppose tax increases. But two independent press reports (here and here) suggest [the possibility that the party could remain critical while ultimately declining to bring down the government] is being contemplated within RN ranks. Before the budget battle gets fully under way comes an election that has attracted remarkably little investor attention. On 27 September, 178 of the Senate’s 348 seats will be renewed. Senators are elected indirectly, predominantly by local representatives, meaning this year’s electoral college reflects the balance produced by the March municipal elections. As [the author] explained in a note published earlier this year, the relevant question is not whether the RN can take control of the upper Chamber – [the RN] cannot [take control of the upper Chamber]. Even a strong result would leave [the RN] in a small minority. The importance lies instead in the composition of a chamber that could become an institutional constraint under an RN presidency: constitutional changes under Article 89 require both chambers to adopt the same text. The Senate election is limited in immediate political impact, but potentially important for the policy set available after 2027. The political calendar then accelerates. PS and Place Publique hold [PS and Place Publique's] presidential primary on 9 -10 October, with a second round on 16 -17 October if needed. On the election, polls continue to show a large RN first round lead, while the identity of [the RN's] eventual challenger remains much more uncertain (Figure 4, right). Bottom line. France therefore enters the autumn with genuine economic, fiscal and political risks, but not an uniformly negative outlook, in particular on the political front. The immediate test is whether economic resilience (relative) and a workable path through [the 2027 budget] can hold as the harder 2027 fiscal and political choices come into view.
