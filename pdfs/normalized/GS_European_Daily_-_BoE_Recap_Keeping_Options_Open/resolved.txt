BoE Recap—Keeping Options Open The MPC voted 8-1 to hold Bank Rate at the April meeting, with Pill dissenting in favour of a 25bp hike. The vote split was in line with consensus expectations, although [the author] had expected that Greene would also support raising Bank Rate at [the April meeting]. The Monetary Policy Summary noted a “risk of material second-round effects” that policy “would need to lean against”. At the same time, [the Monetary Policy Summary] also acknowledged that a weakening economy and tightening financial conditions could help to moderate inflationary pressures. The Summary noted that the MPC judged a hold to be appropriate “at this meeting” while reiterating that the Committee “stands ready to act as necessary” to keep inflation on course to meet the target. The Bank showed three scenarios for the economic outlook in the MPR, varying the assumptions on energy prices as well as the extent of second-round effects. Economics Research In scenario A, oil and gas prices followed the futures curves, and no second-round effects emerged. Under [scenario A], the projections showed headline inflation peaking at 3.6% in 2026Q4 before falling below target later in the forecast horizon, ending the projection at 1.7% in 2029Q2. Scenario B assumed that energy prices peaked at similar levels but the increase was more persistent than in scenario A, while modest second-round effects developed. [Scenario B] led to inflation peaking at 3.7% but falling back more gradually and stabilising at 2% in 2028 and 2029. Scenario C assumed a larger and more persistent increase in energy prices together with larger second-round effects, with inflation rising to 6.2% in 2027Q1 and ending the forecast at 2.6%. Bailey indicated that [Governor Bailey] placed most weight on scenario B “albeit with slightly reduced second-round effects” while also placing some weight on scenario C. Other Committee members expressed a range of views for the likelihood of the different scenarios playing out. Breeden and Lombardelli appeared to place most weight on scenario B, Greene suggested that B represented a lower bound on second-round effects, while Ramsden indicated that scenario A and B were equally likely. Most of the Bank’s policy simulations showed some increase in Bank Rate compared to [Bank Rate's] current level even in scenarios A and B, with the forward-looking Taylor rule being the exception. Looking one year ahead to 2027Q2, the implied level of Bank Rate ranged from 3.4-4.4% in scenario A (with an average of 4.0%), 3.7-4.4% in scenario B (average 4.1%), and 4.2-5.5% in scenario C (average 4.9%). In the press conference, however, Bailey chose to focus on the changes in the policy simulations since the February forecast round rather than the implied level of Bank Rate. [Governor Bailey] noted the rules showed a higher path for Bank Rate than at that point in time. But [Governor Bailey] argued that, given that in February markets expected rates to fall by around 40bp this year, the changes in the interest rate responses in scenarios A and B would “largely be accommodated by reversing out these assumed cuts without further increasing rates”. In [Governor Bailey's] individual paragraph, however, [Governor Bailey] did also place some weight on scenario C, which [Governor Bailey] suggested “would require a stronger monetary policy response”. Bailey suggested that the analysis overall indicated that “there are paths through this where we don’t have to increase rates, and there are scenarios where we do [have to increase rates]”, keeping options open for the upcoming meetings. [Governor Bailey] described today’s decision as an “active hold” that represented the best judgement based on the assessment of range of possible outcomes, rather than a “passive wait-and-see hold”. Nonetheless, [Governor Bailey] indicated that [Governor Bailey] does not think the market curve is in the wrong place “because there are risks around this”. The Path Ahead Looking ahead, [the author maintains] [the author's] forecast for unchanged Bank Rate this year (followed by cuts back to 3% in 2027). First, [the author's] commodity strategists look for a decline in energy prices as energy flows gradually pick up into the summer. As a result, [the author sees] a lower peak inflation rate than the Bank’s Scenario B. Second, [the author expects] further labour market weakening as restrictive policy and higher energy prices weigh on growth. Third, Bailey’s interpretation of the policy simulations suggests that [Governor Bailey] would probably need to see further energy price pressures before supporting higher rates. That said, [the author sees] a low hurdle for the BoE to deliver a couple of hikes during the summer if energy prices remain elevated for longer or the inflation data point to stronger passthrough. The incoming price surveys show material indirect inflation effects from the energy shock, pointing to upside risks to [the author's] core inflation forecast. Raising Bank Rate would also be consistent with the policy simulations presented in the MPR, which imply one to two hikes on average in Scenario B. The ECB’s likely tightening could also play a role in tipping the BoE into action. As a result, [the author sees] risks as clearly skewed towards hikes in [the author's] scenario analysis. [The author] attach a 40% subjective probability to [the author's] on-hold baseline (unchanged from before), 30% to two hikes (vs 25%), 15% to four hikes (unchanged) and 15% to no hikes and earlier cuts (vs 20%). The resulting probability-weighted path is slightly below near-term market pricing and more meaningfully below for 2027.
