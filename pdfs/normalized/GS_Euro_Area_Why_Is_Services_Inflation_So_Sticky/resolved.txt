# Euro Area—Why Is Services Inflation So Sticky

Services inflation in the Euro area has been stickier than expected, with the year-over-year rate standing at 3.2% in January—nearly unchanged from last May. [Services inflation in the Euro area] remains above the 2-3% range historically consistent with 2% core inflation, given historical core goods inflation of 0-1%. The three most plausible explanations for [services inflation in the Euro area] are

1. a robust labour market,
2. services prices still catching up to relatively higher goods prices, and
3. wage growth running hotter than usual. [The three plausible explanations for why services inflation has stayed high] are challenging to disentangle, as [the three plausible explanations for why services inflation has stayed high] operate simultaneously and interact with one another. However, the Euro area offers the unique advantage of cross-country comparisons, enabling better identification of each driver. [The author therefore uses] a quarterly panel of the 10 largest economies since 2000 to regress services inflation on [the three plausible explanations for why services inflation has stayed high] and other control variables. [The author's] results suggest that all factors—labour market tightness, the relative price of services to goods, and wage growth—have contributed to boosting services inflation in recent years. The recent moderation was largely attributable to diminishing catch-up effects as the services-to-goods price ratio normalised. In contrast, a weak labour market and relatively lower goods prices acted as headwinds during the 2010s. [The author's] model suggests that services inflation remains more elevated than normal due to remaining services price catchup (worth 0.4pp), resilient labour markets (0.5pp) and firm wage growth (0.3pp). [The author's] model points to gradual services disinflation ahead. Further normalisation in the services-to-goods price ratio and moderating wage growth should subtract 0.5pp from services inflation over the coming year, allowing [services inflation in the Euro area] to fall back within the 2-3% target-consistent range. A robust labour market should prevent an undershoot. In an upside scenario where energy prices spike 70% (e.g., under a Strait of Hormuz closure), services inflation would move sideways, remaining above 3%. [The author sees] three plausible explanations for why services inflation has stayed high. First, the labour market remains robust. The unemployment rate stands close to estimates of the structural rate, and [the author's] broader measure of labour market tightness—which incorporates job openings and other indicators—suggests conditions are even firmer (Exhibit 2, left). Labour market tightness matters for services inflation because the services sector is highly labour-intensive. [That Labour market tightness matters for services inflation because the services sector is highly labour-intensive] is the core mechanism of the Phillips curve: when labour markets tighten, workers bargain for higher wages, and firms pass [higher wages] through to prices. Second, services prices have been catching up to goods prices. The services-to-goods price ratio typically trends upwards—a pattern that is particularly stable when excluding volatile energy and food prices (Exhibit 2, right). But the surge in goods inflation in 2021-22 caused large deviations from [the trend that the services-to-goods price ratio typically trends upwards], which services prices have since had to recoup. The intuition is that absolute price levels have little economic meaning; what matters is the ratio of prices to one another. There is no reason, for instance, for a toaster to become more expensive than a haircut unless their relative demand or input costs have changed.
