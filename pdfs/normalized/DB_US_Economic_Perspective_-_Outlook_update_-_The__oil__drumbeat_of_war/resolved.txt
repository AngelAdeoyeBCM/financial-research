# US Economic Perspective - Outlook update - The (oil) drumbeat of war

Economics US Economic Perspectives Date 16 April 2026

Introduction [The author] entered 2026 with a constructive view on the economy. [The author's] narrative was that headwinds from the historic trade policy and tariff shock from 2025 were likely to fade over the course of this year as tariff rates fell and that growth tailwinds were set to strengthen from monetary and fiscal policy, financial conditions, robust productivity and AI-related investment. While these pillars of [the author's] outlook have largely materialized as expected, the economy has been dealt another adverse shock from a spike in oil prices precipitated by the war in Iran. [The author's] baseline is that as long as oil prices have peaked and are on a slow downward trajectory over the coming months, the economy is likely to weather another substantial shock relatively well. Indeed, the adjustments to [the author's] forecast are relatively modest: slightly weaker growth, marginally lower unemployment, and modestly highe r inflation. Against [the backdrop that the adjustments to the author's forecast are relatively modest], [the author has] removed [the author's] lone Fed rate cut from this year and now see the Fed on an indefinite hold near neutral. Growth: Oil drag versus fiscal and FCI boost [The author has] modestly lowered [the author's] growth forecast this year, with Q4/Q4 real GDP growth now projected at 2. 3% (down from 2.4% previously). [The downgrade that the author has modestly lowered the author's growth forecast this year] reflects the drag from higher oil prices (roughly -0.2pp) as well as weaker consumer spending to start the year counterbalanced by continued tailwinds for growth (see Impact of oil price shocks a “frack”-tion of what they once were, Into the danger zone? Nonlinearities between oil prices and growth and How big of an oil shock can the Big Beautiful Bill handle?). [The continued tailwinds for growth] include: supportive financial conditions, fiscal policy (larger tax refunds), robust productivity, and a continued strong pace of AI investments. [The author's] growth forecast assumes oil prices broadly follow DB’s commodity team’s expectations that Brent will remain elevated in the near-term before moderating towards $75-80 per barrel by year end. The most notable risk to [the author's growth forecast] is a further escalation in Iran which leads to a more severe and protracted oil price shock. Although recent monthly payrolls prints have been very volatile, the labor market has shown increased evidence of stabilizing. Most measures of labor market slack have been stable over the past 12 -18 months. Together with the recent decline in the unemployment rate to 4.3% confirms that the monthly breakeven payroll number has fallen to [approximately] 0 -50k jobs [or] month, consistent with recent Fed staff research (see here and here). [The author's] baseline is that the unemployment rate will remain near or perhaps slightly below current levels (Q4 lowered by 0. 1pp to 4.3%). Despite greater confidence in stabilization, the labor market remains in an uncomfortable equilibrium of low hiring and low firing along with a relatively narrow base of sectors producing growth. Downside risks are therefore present, including from AI beyond the near-term, even though we do not believe the latter has had a material adverse effect on the labor market thus far (see latest Tracking AI’s impact on the labor market (Apr. 2026) and Will AI drive unemployment higher this year? AI thinks not). Shelter disinflation should continue modestly further, and core goods may reverse some tariff effects later this year, but “super core” inflation has proven sticky. [The author has] incorporated some upside into core inflation this year from the oil price shock and now see core CPI and PCE at 2.7% and 2.9% (Q4/Q4), respectively. [Core PCE] is about 0.1pp higher than [the author's] prior forecast and half a percentage point higher than [the author's] outlook projections last November. Headline inflation h as undergone the more substantial upward revision in recent months, with CPI and PCE now anticipated to end the year around 3.25%. Following the upward revisions, risks are now more balanced for this year. A more sustained oil price shock would impart upside risks, while further shelter disinflation or a quicker reversal in tariff effects represent downsides. Further out, [the author continues] to project core inflation will re main modestly ([approximately] 0.25pp) above the Fed’s target over the forecast horizon. First, tariff revenues have likely peaked and look set to significantly undershoot the CBO’s projections as [the Trump administration] does not fully replace IEEPA rates. Second, it has been confirmed that refunds of the ~$175bn of tariffs paid under IEEPA powers will proceed with the process beginning imminently. [The author's] baseline is that roughly $50bn of refunds will be dispersed this year, with the majority in 2027. Finally, the Iran war has added an unexpected additional expense for the defense budget. To be sure, there remain two-sided risks to [the author's] fiscal outlook. First, [the author has] not made any assumptions on potential revenues from several ongoing Section 301 investigations. While [the Trump administration] has signaled [the Trump administration's] intent to ramp up [the ongoing Section 301 investigations] in the wake of losing [the Trump administration's] IEEPA case, it remains unclear if [the ongoing Section 301 investigations] would be used as a negotiating tool or as a revenue source. In addition, the Trump administration might want to avoid pursuing further unpopular inflationary policies ahead of the midterm elections. Regarding downside risks, Congress is likely to pursue another budget reconciliation bill this year which could include some type of mild stimulus. [The author's] base case is that another reconciliation bill this year will be narrow in scope and unlikely to provide meaningful further support for the economy (e.g., [the author does] not expect substantial stimulus checks for households). However, should gasoline prices remain elevated, Congressional Republicans could pass a temporary federal gasoline tax holiday in [Congressional Republicans'] upcoming reconciliation bill. On an annual basis, a gasoline tax holiday could be worth around $45bn to consumers. Absent those policies, [the author projects] a federal budget deficit as a share of GDP in the 6. 25- 6.50% range this year. [The author's] baseline is now that the Fed will remain on hold indefinitely. Unchanged rates are consistent with policy rules with [the author's] economic forecast, which prescribe rates at or above current levels. The timing of Warsh’s nomination remains uncertain, as [Warsh’s nomination] is tied to the DoJ’s investigation of Chair Powell. Ultimately, however, [the author does] not see a turnover in leadership as producing a clearly more dovish reaction function for the Committee. There are two-sided risks to [the author's] view. Rate cuts this year most likely require some weakening in labor market conditions along with softer inflation. A rate hike this year is no longer a trivial possibility, but [the author does] not expect such conditions to manifest in 2026. Getting there likely requires: a re-tightening of the labor market such that [the labor market] returns as a source of inflationary pressures as well as an acceleration in core PCE above 3%. Upward movement in inflation expectations would also contribute to [a rate hike this year].
