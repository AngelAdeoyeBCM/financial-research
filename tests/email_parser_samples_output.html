Oil Markets Weekly: Dry powder

Subject: Oil Markets Weekly: Dry powder Sender: natasha.kaneva@jpmresearchmail.com Received: 2026-09-17T12:03:59 Oil Markets Weekly Dry powder For the first time since the start of the Iran conflict, we don’t have a baseline view. We simply don’t know how to model the endgame. At the onset, we thought we did. We assumed there were economic red lines the US administration would be unwilling to cross: $100 oil, gasoline near $5 a gallon, a 4% headline inflation or a 5-handle on the 10-year Treasury yield. Those constraints gave us an implicit timeline, and we expected that by June there would be some form of agreement to reopen the Strait. Six months later, many of those lines have been crossed, yet the exit strategy is less clear, not more. Oil is above $100 and the 10-year yield has a 5-handle. Gasoline, at $4.37, remains at record seasonally adjusted levels even though peak driving season is behind us. More concerning, diesel is at an all-time high of $6.31 a gallon heading into winter—the period of peak seasonal demand—while inventories sit at all-time lows. The market is on edge, in our view. Based on current supply and demand conditions, we estimate September fair value at $90, yet Brent is trading at $106. Using our rule of thumb that every 1 mbd of supply disruption adds roughly $4 to the price, that $16 premium implies the market is pricing in the risk of an additional 4 mbd of losses on top of the 10 mbd already disrupted—rather than a confirmed, enduring reduction in supply. And there is no shortage of risk for the market to price. Over the past week, the conflict has opened new pressure points across the region. Houthi advances along Yemen’s Red Sea coast and into the Bab el-Mandeb have put another critical shipping route at risk, while an attack on Saudi Arabia’s East-West pipeline temporarily shut a key alternative route for crude exports. Nor is the volatility contained to the Gulf. Despite President Trump’s claim of an energy truce, Ukrainian drones struck Russia’s Slavyansk refinery on Saturday and, in a more significant display of reach, hit the Taneco refinery in Tatarstan—more than 1,200 km from the Ukraine-Russia border—on Sunday, followed by the Syzran refinery in Russia’s Samara region on Monday. Meanwhile, Russia launched attacks on the Ukrainian capital and other cities. With no clear signals from either the US or Iran that they are prepared to de-escalate—and absent a diplomatic breakthrough on September 24, when President Trump and President Xi are set to meet in DC—the assumption that the disruption is temporary is becoming increasingly difficult to sustain. So what happens if it doesn’t? The answer depends less on how long the conflict lasts than on how the physical oil market continues to clear while it does. Commodities are physical markets. They have to clear, meaning supply and demand must balance almost in real time. Put simply, when supply exceeds demand, the surplus flows into inventories, signaling an oversupplied market and pushing prices lower. The opposite is also true. When demand exceeds supply, the shortfall is met by drawing down inventories. Stock draws signal a market in deficit, which tends to push prices higher. In other words, inventories are the key clearing mechanism between supply and demand. It is also why changes in OECD commercial inventories carry the largest weight in our oil pricing model. That logic sat at the center of our Illusion of Plenty thesis in April. Given the scale of the supply disruption, we expected inventories to draw rapidly and cautioned that, while the headline number looks considerable, the volume actually available to draw is far smaller. At the pace we anticipated—around 1.6 billion barrels—the drawdown would have pushed available stocks from 8.4 billion barrels at the start of the conflict toward operational floor levels by September. As inventories fell, we expected prices to rise toward $100 a barrel and remain there even after the Strait reopened. That was the theory. In practice, the market cleared differently. Faced with uncertainty over how long the conflict might last, governments and consumers largely chose to preserve inventories, keeping barrels in reserve for tougher days ahead. Stocks fell sharply through May, but then leveled off in June and July, before resuming more modest draws in August and so far in September. In total, draws of crude oil and refined products amounted to 555 million barrels, only about one-third of what we had expected (Figure 1). In other words, even the limited drawable inventory was largely preserved. Figure 1: Global oil inventories forecasted in April vs actuals mb [Inline figure 1]

Source: JODI, IEA, EIA, Kpler, J.P. Morgan Commodities Research Where those barrels came from is just as telling as the size of the draw. Of the 555 million barrels released, the US accounted for 219 mb (excluding natural gas liquids), followed by China at 147 mb, Europe at 77 mb, and Japan at 69 mb. South Korea moved in the opposite direction, building inventories by 12 mb, likely as insurance against an unusually uncertain supply backdrop, while several other countries were also able to add modestly to stocks. Instead, policymakers shifted far more of the adjustment burden onto demand (Figure 2). Since March, demand is running around 4.4 mbd below last year's level, about one and a half times larger than the adjustment implied by inventory draws or 2.9 mbd. Figure 2: Global oil demand forecasted in April vs actuals mbd [Inline figure 2]

Source: EIA, IEA, JLC, Bloomberg Finance L.P., Wood Mackenzie, J.P. Morgan Commodities Research This distinction is critical for price formation. Falling inventories tend to lift prices, while falling demand tends to depress them. By leaning much more on demand destruction and much less on stock draws, the market has been able to absorb an extraordinary supply disruption without a sustained rise in crude prices. Since the conflict began, Brent has averaged just $94. It also suggests that fears of inventories being close to exhausted as a balancing mechanism are premature, at least for now (Figure 3). Morgan Commodities Research Remaining capacity, however, is uneven. US commercial stocks are already near the bottom of their recent range, while the SPR can provide only another 30 mb. The next round of draws would therefore have to come from the countries that have conserved inventories through the first six months of the conflict. Japan and South Korea, both of which have added to their inventories in recent months, could release around 15 mb and 20 mb, respectively. Europe could contribute another 50 mb across crude and products without significant strain. Together with the US, that implies about 120 million barrels of potential supply from the OECD sources. China remains the largest swing factor. At a comfortable draw rate of around 1 mbd, it could release another 120 mb by year-end, with perhaps another 10 mb coming from other non-OECD countries. In aggregate, these draws would be sufficient to pull global inventories toward our 7.65 billion barrels ‘stress’ threshold. Beyond that point, however, the system becomes materially more fragile and demand adjustment would likely need to intensify, potentially through more explicit government intervention. For now, there is still ample inventory to cushion a prolonged disruption before operational stress levels are reached, which limits the need for crude prices to move materially higher. If Middle Eastern flows were to remain at current levels, however, our framework would imply 4Q26 and December 2026 prices around $7 and $8 above our current forecasts, respectively. UBS: Brazil 2026 Elections: What to watch in the first round

Subject: UBS: Brazil 2026 Elections: What to watch in the first round Sender: roque.montero@ubs.com Received: 2026-09-28T10:47:11 EM FI/FX Strategy Brazil 2026 Elections: What to watch in the first round Polls have tightened materially ahead of Sunday's vote, raising the odds of a turning point in fiscal expectations. We recommend long 9m USDBRL 4.5 digital puts Brazil elects a new president, but fiscal credibility is on the ballot On Sunday, Brazilians will head to the polls to elect the country's next president, Congress (full lower house and two-thirds of the Senate) and state governors, with a runoff between President Lula and Senator Flávio Bolsonaro widely expected on Oct 25. Beyond election-related uncertainties, the key question for markets is whether the next administration can restore fiscal credibility. Public debt has risen by 10pp of GDP since the 2022 election despite the introduction of a new fiscal framework, raising questions about the sustainability of current fiscal dynamics. The Finance Minister appointment in December and the March bi-monthly fiscal report are likely to provide the first meaningful tests of post-election fiscal delivery. With recent polling shifts increasing the odds of a turning point in fiscal expectations, we recommend positioning for lower USDBRL via long 9m digital puts struck at 4.5. At roughly 8% premium, the structure offers inexpensive exposure to a more constructive post-election fiscal outcome. In such an outcome, long-end yields could compress by 200-250bp over the next three to four months and gradually move toward 10% by end-2027E (vs. 14% today). Conversely, persistent fiscal uncertainty could push USDBRL toward 5.4-5.5 and long-end yields roughly 100bp higher, although high carry, a supportive external backdrop and lighter positioning should help contain downside. Polling momentum has shifted toward Flávio ahead of Sunday's vote The race tightened following the Supreme Court controversy in early September, with UBS' poll aggregator now showing Lula and Flávio at 44% and 41% of first-round voting intentions, compared with 42% and 37% in late August. With no other left-wing candidates on the ballot, Lula could face a high bar to expand support, while Flávio appears better positioned to consolidate votes from the remaining, predominantly center-right candidates (8-10% of voting intentions in recent polls). While this dynamic is partly reflected in runoff expectations, with Flávio now narrowly ahead in our runoff poll aggregator, betting markets have moved more decisively, shifting from a roughly 25pp advantage for Lula in late August to an 11pp lead for Flávio today. That said, major pollsters continue to depict a highly competitive second round with no clear front-runner. Election night: What to watch and the key known unknowns thereafter Markets head into the vote looking for validation of the recent shift in runoff expectations. A narrow Lula lead (3pp or less), particularly if accompanied by a strong showing by Flávio in Minas Gerais (key swing state) and São Paulo (20% of the electorate), or an outright Flávio lead, could reinforce recent runoff repricing. Beyond election night, four known unknowns could reshape the race ahead of the runoff. First, the Banco Master investigation. Developments remain difficult to predict and could still impact the election. Recent reports involving Flávio illustrate such risks. Second, the recent hike to Bolsa Família. The increase will be disbursed one week before the runoff on Oct 19. With 20% of eligible voters typically abstaining and non-voters more likely to lean left, the measure could support turnout among Lula's base and influence runoff dynamics. Third, additional policy initiatives. Recent government measures, including the ban on online betting and a new debt-relief program, could also prove supportive of Lula's standing among voters. Fourth, Flávio's ability to consolidate support from the remaining candidates after the first round.
