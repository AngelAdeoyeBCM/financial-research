BoJ hike expectations, technical factors and FX supply-demand behind the recent sharp JPY appreciation The recent sharp JPY appreciation was likely driven by BoJ rate hike expectations, technical factors and FX supply-demand, including anticipation of pension repatriation. USDJPY fell from around 164 to nearly 155 following coordinated US-Japan intervention at end-July, then rebounded to around 160 in early September before reversing again and briefly plunging into the 152s on 8 September. We believe the renewed JPY appreciation reflected stronger BoJ rate hike expectations, technical factors and anticipation of pension funds shifting back toward domestic assets. First, hawkish remarks by BoJ officials including Board Member Takata pushed market pricing for rate hikes by year-end from around 40bp in August to 45-50bp in September. Second, from a technical perspective, the break below the 155 support, which held through both the Golden Week and July interventions, opened the way for further downside. Third, JPY's FX supply- demand has improved with rounds of interventions. Furthermore, the GPIF’s unusual August Board of Governors meeting triggered a sharp rise in expectations that the fund would shift back toward domestic assets. With the strong 155 support now broken, USDJPY is technically vulnerable to further downside tests. Given that 155 held even through two rounds of intervention, it is now more likely to act as resistance for the time being, while the 158s (200dma) is likely to be viewed as an even stronger resistance level (Figure 1). On the downside, key reference points are the YTD low of 152 and the post-Takaichi administration low of around 150. While further downside could technically extend to 145 (the level immediately before the Takaichi administration took office), market expectations for faster BoJ hikes could start to fade if USDJPY breaks below 150, suggesting sustained JPY appreciation would be difficult without a full-fledged change in fundamentals. Risks of reversal depending on BoJ and GPIF The recent JPY appreciation could unwind sharply back towards the upper-150s if neither hawkish BoJ rate-hike expectations nor anticipation of pension funds shifting back toward domestic assets materializes. First, further JPY appreciation may require a surprise relative to already hawkish BoJ expectations. Amid hawkish comments from the BoJ officials and indicative comments from US Treasury Secretary Bessent on prospective hikes, the OIS market already prices almost quarterly rate hikes, taking the policy rate to nearly 1.9% by July next year (Figure 2), exceeding our forecast of hikes in September, January and July to 1.75%. Hence, unless the BoJ clearly signal quarterly hikes towards 2%, any JPY appreciation from a hawkish surprise would likely be limited if not unwound outright. While signaling of hikes beyond 2% could add further fuel to the JPY strength, the currently-priced 2% is already the central point of BoJ's neutral rate estimates, therefore rendering it unlikely at least at this point. However, given the low yields on FX- hedged foreign bonds, this appears unlikely. Other Japanese institutional investors are unlikely to follow pension repatriation. Life insurers already sold a significant amount of foreign bonds amid the global duration selloff and rise in FX hedge costs during 2022-23. While higher JGB yields should attract more of their new money flows, we do not expect much JPY buying from foreign bond sales. Indeed, if they view underlying JPY headwinds as largely unchanged, they may instead see the current JPY strength as an opportunity to reengage in JPY selling via FX-unhedged foreign bonds (see FY25 life insurer results). Banks fund their foreign bond portfolios via repo markets; hence, repatriation, if any, should not drive much JPY buying flow. Budget requests for FY27 reached JPY143.1trn, exceeding the combined total of the FY26 supplementary budget and FY27 initial budget of JPY140.6trn, and are likely to rise further once unspecified requests are included. With a reasonably hawkish BoJ hiking path already priced in, the risk that renewed fiscal concerns push JGB term premia higher and weaken the JPY at the same time warrants attention. Longer-term fundamental and supply/demand-based pressure for JPY depreciation is persistent and makes it difficult to see sustained JPY appreciation. In addition to fundamental factors – including the still-wide US-Japan interest-rate differential even with faster BoJ hikes, the low equity risk premium, and concerns over the Takaichi administration’s “high-pressure economy policy” – FX supply-demand remains structurally biased toward JPY selling, excluding the temporary improvement from intervention. Persistent intervention concerns (see Appendix on scope for additional interventions) mean that a move to fresh highs no longer appears likely. However, more sustained medium- to long-term JPY appreciation is difficult to see without structural changes such as 1) full-fledged convergence in US-Japan interest-rate differentials through Fed rate cuts, 2) a rise in the equity risk premium driven by a reversal of the AI-led equity rally narrative, or 3) a shift in the Takaichi administration’s reflationary policy stance. 10 September 2026 5 Barclays | FX Views

On a four-quarter moving-average basis, FX supply-demand for JPY (excluding flows that do not drive FX spot transactions6 ) shows that net JPY selling narrowed from around JPY20trn last year to roughly JPY10trn as of Q2 26, reflecting JPY-buying intervention during Golden Week and other operations (Figure 7). With the additional JPY15trn intervention at end-July also reflected, the balance is likely to shift to net JPY buying of around JPY7trn in Q3. Looking at FX supply-demand in recent years, “overseas investment, etc. ” (JPY selling), centered on household overseas securities investment through the new NISA scheme and corporate outward direct investment, has been running at around JPY40trn, while the current account surplus (JPY buying) has been around JPY20trn, leaving net flows on a JPY-selling trend of roughly JPY20trn. JPY-buying intervention since the start of the year, totaling JPY27trn, has more than offset these net JPY sales. Of course, our FX supply-demand analysis does not explicitly include speculative positioning, and part of the intervention may have been absorbed by position adjustments among speculative investors (speculative positions declined sharply after the July intervention; Figure 8). Nevertheless, continued large-scale M&A, together with overseas investment associated with the new NISA scheme, suggest that structural JPY selling pressure will persist. As noted above, inward direct investment flows should gradually ease the additional pressure, but will not immediately alter the overall supply-demand balance. As shown in the following sections, 1) active yen selling by retail investors via New NISA is recently accelerating and it is likely to persist unless the AI equity narrative sees a significant change, and 2) with regards to corporate investment flows, inward FDIs are only partially offsetting outward FDIs and revenue flow is biased toward JPY selling. While pension repatriation may generate one-off JPY16-32trn JPY buying as noted above, we do not expect other institutional investors to follow suit. Hence, as long as the active investment outflows from retail investors and corporations continue to exceed the current account surplus, structural supply-demand conditions will likely remain a headwind to the JPY, in our view. 10 September 2026 6 Barclays | FX Views

Investment trusts are a popular investment vehicle, and household holdings of investment trusts reached a record JPY165trn at end-2025, supported by the new NISA scheme introduced in 2024. However, more than 90% of investment trust inflows have gone into products targeting overseas assets, and households’ active investment stance has contributed to JPY depreciation (see JPY: Household investment in overseas assets and implications for the JPY, 8 June). JPY selling associated with NISA investment is accelerating. Individual investor inflows into NISA-eligible investment trusts totaled JPY12.6trn in 2024, of which JPY11.7trn targeted overseas assets, and JPY9.9trn in 2025, of which JPY9.7trn targeted overseas assets. YTD flows in 2026 have already reached JPY11.5trn, including JPY9.4trn invested in overseas assets. NISA- related investment is typically concentrated at the start of the year when annual allowances reset, but investment trust flows have shown no slowdown even in H2 this year. By investment destination, flows into investment trusts targeting domestic assets increased in June and July, but slowed from August, with no sign of a shift back toward JPY assets (Figure 9). Meanwhile, overseas asset investment through investment trusts has continued at a faster pace than in 2024 and 2025, suggesting that JPY selling pressure from individual investors has not receded even in H2 (Figure 10). Annualizing YTD overseas investment through NISA noted above implies JPY selling of JPY14.1trn, up JPY3.4trn y/y. Such active investment flows into overseas equity markets (especially in the US) are likely to persist unless the AI equity narrative sees a significant change. FDI: Inward investment growth eases JPY selling pressure Japanese companies remain active in outward direct investment and the persistent JPY- selling pressure generated by this, together with robust outward portfolio investment, remains largely unchanged. Japanese corporate M&A has continued to expand since the COVID pandemic, with deal sizes also increasing. The average deal size rose to JPY45.6bn in 2025, reflecting the weak JPY environment and large transactions such as Nippon Steel’s acquisition of US Steel. At the same time, the weak JPY has increased the investment appeal of Japanese companies to overseas firms in recent years, while improvements in Japanese corporate governance have also provided a tailwind7 , and the resulting expansion of inward direct investment is beginning to offset some of the JPY selling pressure associated with Japanese corporate M&A. The government has continued to promote inward direct investment, and growth in inward direct investment flows has consequently begun to outpace that in outward direct investment flows. Inward direct investment flows totaled JPY6.9trn in 2025, still well below outward direct investment flows of JPY32.6trn, but increased by JPY4trn y/y, exceeding the JPY1.1trn rise in outward direct investment (Figure 12). Excluding the pandemic period, this was the first such occurrence since 2016. However, the reinvestment of direct investment income appears to differ between inward and outward investment. Japanese companies’ outward direct investment primarily consists of long-term investment involving local production, and profits earned locally are often reinvested. By contrast, much of the direct investment into Japan is thought to consist mainly of equity acquisitions by overseas investment funds8 , which may be more likely to lead to income outflows through demands for higher dividends and other channels. We therefore believe that while the expansion of inward direct investment may ease JPY selling pressure in the short term, it could also generate sustained pressure for JPY depreciation over the longer term through dividend payments and related outflows. Appendix: FX intervention - Need for further action has receded following largest intervention on record The Ministry of Finance has conducted record JPY-buying interventions. The MoF reported a JPY-buying/USD-selling intervention of JPY15.4trn at end-July, the largest monthly total on record. Including the intervention from late April to early May, cumulative interventions in 2026 have reached JPY27.1trn, also setting a new annual record. The scale of the US intervention has not been disclosed, but past cases suggest that it was likely limited. Over the past 30 years, the US has participated in only three FX interventions: coordinated US-Japan JPY-buying/USD-selling intervention in 1998 (USD0.83bn); G7 EUR-buying/USD-selling intervention in 2000 (USD1.34bn); and G7 JPY-selling/USD-buying intervention in 2011 (USD1bn). All were coordinated with overseas authorities and the amount per intervention was around USD1bn, substantially smaller than Japan’s unilateral interventions. The funding was also split evenly between the US Treasury’s Exchange Stabilization Fund (ESF) and the Fed’s SOMA account, leaving the US Treasury’s share at only about USD0.5bn. The US appears to have played an important signaling role in the coordinated intervention. The New York Fed reportedly sold EURJPY during the intervention at end-July, but no significant change has been observed in the SOMA account’s holdings of EUR or JPY. The intervention was therefore likely funded using the ESF’s holdings of foreign currency and foreign-currency-denominated assets (a combined USD4.4bn in JPY and EUR; Figure 13). If so, the effective size of the US intervention was likely well below the “USD5-10bn” reportedly noted in Treasury Secretary Bessent’s memo (Reuters, 31 July). In short, while the US again played an important signaling role in the coordinated intervention, Japan appears to have provided most of the actual funding. Although the need has receded, Japan still has ample capacity for further FX intervention. In a 31 August interview with CNBC, Treasury Secretary Bessent said, “it’s my belief that the Japanese government and that the BOJ will do the things that will lead to a stronger yen. ” This could be interpreted as signaling that the initiative for further JPY-strengthening measures and, if necessary, FX intervention now rests with the Japanese authorities. The JPY’s recent appreciation has reduced the need for intervention, but even after conducting the largest intervention on record in 2026, the MoF still held USD1.09trn in foreign reserves as of end-June (Figure 14). In addition, according to the MoF statement on 3 August, the authorities plan to utilise the FIMA Repo Facility in the future, which would enable them to raise intervention funds flexibly without having to sell their US Treasury holdings. Even if depreciation pressure on the JPY intensifies again, the Japanese authorities should retain ample intervention capacity. 10 September 2026 9 Barclays | FX Views
