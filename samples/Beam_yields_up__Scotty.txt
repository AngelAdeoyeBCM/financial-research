Lee Ferridge 26 August 2026 1 Insights Classification: General, Complimentary Material DM FX Weekly : Beam yields u p , Scotty? US Treasury Secretary Bessent last week managed to create a mini-crisis all of his own doing. By the surprise announcement that he was intending to increase the size of long-end Treasury buy-backs to $4bn from $2bn he highlighted to the market how concerned he was about rising yields. With the 30-yr last week reaching its highest yield since 2007 one can perhaps understand the worry, but the announcement (and subsequent TV appearances) only served to highlight the problem. This week we take a look at the implications for FX markets, in particular the US dollar. The broad conclusion? Well, we remain underweight the USD in our portfolio. Does that answer the question? We also maintain our CAD long versus the AUD. There is plenty of opportunity for more U-turns before meaningful trade sanctions are imposed on January 1st 2027. This week’s CAD reaction seems, to us at least, an over-reaction. While yields have been generally rising since the beginning of July, this was more of a drift higher than true signs of stress. In terms of the yield curve, we had seen some steepening but, again, nothing dramatic. 2s10s last week hit 50bp, up from 26bp at the end of June but still below the 70bp at which it started 2026. 2s30s reached 113bp last week, ahead of Bessent’s buyback announcement, which was its steepest since April. But it started the year at 137bp. Nothing really to see here. Except, now there is. As Figure 1 illustrates, the Treasury Secretary’s intervention has caused the media (and, hence, the market) to suddenly focus more keenly on the yield curve and, to a lesser extent (at least so far) the issue of debt sustainability. Scott Bessent’s nervousness over rising yields is understandable given that US debt interest payments this year are set to be over $1tr, or 3.3% of GDP, and are now higher than the primary deficit of $814bn (2.6% of GDP). 2025 was the first time that US interest payments were higher than the primary deficit and the trend is certainly not good. The CBO projects that net interest payments will top $2tr by 2035 (4.5% of GDP). Its 2026 projections, meanwhile, are based on an average 3m bill rate through the year of 3.3% and an average 10-yr yield of 4.1%. This is clearly a huge problem and, by showing his hand last week, Bessent has alerted the market to his concerns, leaving US duration an unattractive investment opportunity at current yield levels. Will the buybacks be enough to stem rising yields? Unlikely given it only increased the minimum buyback from $2bn to $4bn dollars. Over the quarter this is effectively an increase in buybacks in the 10-30yr area of the curve from $16bn to $32bn. Even then, it’s unlikely Treasury will accept $32b worth of par in offers. Given Q3 will see issuance in the 10yr+ part of the yield curve of $231bn, the extra $16bn in buybacks is the definition of a drop in the ocean. Increase market focus on a problem and then tackle it with a pea-shooter. Hardly the way to build confidence in US duration. 2 Insights Resistance is futile. When it comes to long-term debt sales, a major issue facing the US Treasury is that the US is no longer the only game in town. AI investment has led to a sharp increase in long duration corporate debt issuance, a fact bemoaned by Bessent last week when he argued they should be issuing more in the belly (3-7yr) part of the curve. Five megacap companies are expected to issue around $250bn in debt in 2026, double their $120bn in 2025. $400bn is estimated for 2027. At the same time, Japanese flows have turned much less supportive. As we wrote here, and as Figure 2 illustrates, in four of the last six years, Japan has seen a surplus is net portfolio flows into long-term debt as rising JGB yields make them attractive again both for overseas and domestic investors. In the first half of 2026 Japanese investors actually sold over $50bn in overseas long-term debt leading to a net surplus of over $110bn. That is money not going into USTs. How is your $16bn looking now Scott? Live long and prosper So what does all of this mean for FX markets? For now at least, higher yields are offering the dollar some support but, this may not be true for much longer. The 3m correlation between changes in the DXY and changes in yields is falling sharply for longer-duration yields (Figure 3). For the 30-yr it is now at just 12% and i28% for 10-yr yields. Rising yields due to strong growth and the potential for or actual short-end rate hikes are clearly a dollar positive. Rising yields due to concerns over the level of the deficit/debt are much less positive. Again, Secretary Bessent hardly instilled confidence by his words and actions last week and we expect this to continue to weigh on the dollar. We maintain our dollar short versus the Euro. When it comes to the short- end, the 2-yr yield correlation is a still healthy 50%, but now all eyes will switch to this week’s Jackson Hole symposium and Kevin Warsh’s keynote address on Friday. You can find our in depth preview for Jackson Hole here, but suffice to say Chair Warsh has a needle to thread. He needs to avoid the muddled message he delivered following the July FOMC meeting and, in doing so, reassure long-end investors. Expect much continued talk of tackling inflation being the primary goal but, we fear, little of substance when it comes to the Fed’s actual reaction function. Given his avowed intent to reduce the size of the Fed’s balance sheet and known aversion to extending the duration of its Treasury holdings, how does this sit with Bessent’s moves last week? Luckily for Chair Warsh, there is no Q&A following his speech. Again, we are happy to sit short of dollars into the gathering. One other implication of rising yields is that it makes the FX carry trade much less compelling. Last week saw real money investors start to actively move out of carry positions (Figure 4) but positioning remains overweight. Our other broad indicators show that real money risk appetite remains positive so we are not too concerned yet but, should yields rise once more, carry would be an obvious loser. In our G10 portfolio we are short both the AUD (versus CAD) and GBP (versus the EUR). 3 Insights Information Classification: General Currency Snapshots and central bank monitor Currency View (Buy, Neutral, Sell) CAD Recent Canadian data releases have proved stronger than expected but both the Bank of Canada and the rates market have thus far failed to react. We also see the recent renewed trade spat with the US as a very moveable feast with plenty of TACO possible before January 1st 2027. With this in mind we stay overweight the CAD versus the AUD. JPY The co-ordinated intervention in USDJPY by the MoF and US Treasury has changed the JPY dynamic, at least for the time being. The key questions remain if this will be followed by more JPY buying and, will the BoJ back up the moves with rate hikes? One move by the BoJ is fully priced by year-end but could we see more? We keep a JPY long versus the CHF. EUR Real money demand for the EUR is positive once again and they are now net buyers. Although the market already interprets the ECB as net hawkish, pricing for the next year is in line with that for the BoE. Given the tone of relative central bank speak and relative economic surprises we maintain longs versus GBP and the USD. NZD New Zealand’s economy appears to have troughed and is starting to recover while inflation remains above the RBNZ’s target range. But a lot is expected from the RBNZ in the coming months. We maintain a neutral Kiwi stance. SEK The SEK is typically a risk on currency, so it is often vulnerable during market shocks. This is exacerbated currently given oil sensitivity, investor positioning and a relatively low level of interest rates. However, if fears over the conflict recede, along with the oil price, there is room for the SEK to regain its footing. We stay neutral this week. NOK NOK is vulnerable to further declines in energy prices, especially as there appears limited appetite for further geopolitical escalation, but the outlook remains uncertain. We adopt a neutral stance for now. USD Economic surprises in the US have recently turned sharply lower. This suggests the bar for the Fed to hike in September is relatively high. That said, rate markets still have close to a 40% chance of a hike priced in for September. This makes the dollar particularly vulnerable in the near term. We maintain a short against the EUR as a result. CHF Swiss data surprises are the weakest in the G10, the SNB is the most dovish central bank and there is nothing priced in the rates market. All of this is consistent, but with risk appetite positive and geopolitical risk elevated but stable, the CHF makes an ideal funder. The Machines have noticed and are short the CHF versus the JPY. GBP Sterling has proven stubborn and the last month has seen real money buying back their underweight, despite a sluggish economy. However, BoE pricing is almost exactly the same as that for the EUR despite a more dovish message from the BoE and disappointing data. We maintain a GBP short versus the EUR this week. AUD The RBA has raised rates three times and is now taking a wait-and-see approach as unemployment remains elevated. The fallout from the Iran conflict could yet impact growth, spelling further weakness in the labour market. The AUD is currently the most overweight holding among real money investors. We maintain a short vs CAD.
